## 8 (cont.) Antiphonal writing woodwinds/strings (scan p.278–280, printed p.273–275)

**Concepts**
- Antiphonal writing (escritura antifonal): a motif or chord block is tossed between the woodwind
  section and the string section (or between single players of each), then usually recombined in tutti.

**Rules**
- R1 [PREFER] When a motif is passed antiphonally, assign it deliberately to different members of the
  woodwind and string sections, at different pitch levels, then recapitulate the passage in a tutti
  cadential phrase. *Why:* Beethoven's model (Ex. 8-35).
- R2 [PREFER] After a ff tutti, create an instant contrast by thinning to single solo winds (p dolce)
  over a soft string accompaniment figure.
- R3 [PREFER] The third time an idea (or its instrumentation) is used, alter it, even slightly; the
  altered repetition normally serves as the link to a new idea or section. Adler calls this a general
  habit of great composers, with exceptions.
- R4 [ALLOWED] In the tutti recapitulation, winds and strings may play the figure together in octaves and
  unison while horns and trumpets supply only the dominant–tonic motion. [era: Classical/early-Romantic
  natural brass]

**Examples**
- (tail of the previous example, printed p.273, title on an earlier page [?]; D major, D horns/trumpets,
  probably a Classical/early-Romantic symphony): accented chord blocks alternate between the wind group
  and the string group, then all join with a cresc. and timpani roll.
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 3 "Eroica", I, mm. 37–57 (Ex. 8-35, Eb major, 3/4): ff tutti; at m. 45 a
  3-note motif goes oboe → clarinet → flute → Vln 1 at different pitches, p dolce, over Vln 2/Vla eighth-note
  accompaniment and pizz-like bass punctuation. The exchange restarts at m. 49 and m. 53; the third time,
  clarinet and bassoon in octaves bring in a recapitulating figure that winds and strings then play in
  octaves/unison at ff, horns and trumpets on dominant–tonic.

**Agent applicability**
- For a call-and-response passage, rotate the motif across ≥3 timbres (e.g. Ob → Cl → Fl → Vln 1) at
  different octaves, then merge everyone on the cadence; change something on the 3rd repetition.
- Keep the accompaniment under passed motifs soft (p) and constant (strings eighths) so the hand-offs read.

## 8 (cont.) Using the woodwind section to double other instruments (scan p.281–287, printed p.276–282)

**Concepts**
- Unison doubling (duplicación al unísono) vs octave doubling (duplicación a la octava) of a string line
  by woodwinds.
- Discreet doubling (duplicación discreta): woodwinds reinforce only the weak or exposed string voices,
  entering and leaving to shape dynamics.

**Rules**
- R5 [PREFER] Woodwinds double the strings mainly in tutti sections.
- R6 [era: 18th–19th c.] Unison doubling of strings by woodwinds was very popular.
- R7 [PREFER] (today) Double strings at the octave rather than the unison. *Why:* unison doubling
  costs a line clarity, thickens the sound and muddies the upper partials of both instruments
  (Adler restates his earlier point in the chapter).
- R8 [ALLOWED] A unison doubling that seems redundant still changes the timbre noticeably; use it for
  warmth and richness (flute + Vln 1 in Berlioz, especially in the top of the line).
- R9 [PREFER] A single bassoon an octave below Vln 1 enhances a melody and adds warmth even in an
  already overtone-rich layout (Brahms).
- R10 [ALLOWED] Full-woodwind doubling of strings in a loud tutti (19th–20th c. standard): Vln 1 by
  flutes + piccolo an octave above; violas by oboes and clarinets; cellos by bassoons at the octave
  above and the unison (Dvořák).
- R11 [MUST] In a passage that grows from dark to bright, keep brass volume low enough that strings and
  woodwinds stay on top (Tchaikovsky).
- R12 [AVOID] Adding piccolo (or a higher octave) early in a calm, rising passage. It would sound more
  exuberant but would cover the calm mood. Bring the piccolo in only at the climax.
- R13 [MUST] When a string voice lies in a weak low register (e.g. Vln 2 placed under the violas), double
  it with a soft wind of similar register (Copland: 2nd clarinet and muted horn).
- R14 [PREFER] Orchestrate a diminuendo by removing doubling instruments, and replace a too-prominent
  colour with a softer one. Copland drops Oboe 1 in its extremely low register, which would be too
  prominent, and gives the violin doubling to Flute 2, whose low register is calm and rich.
- R15 [ALLOWED] A doubling instrument can leave the unison to play in thirds, highlighting one chord tone
  (Copland's horn moves from doubling Vln 2 to thirds that stress E, the 3rd of the C major chord).
- R16 [ALLOWED] Voice-crossing inside strings: violas above Vln 2 for several bars, with the low Vln 2
  reinforced by winds (R13).

**Examples**
- Berlioz, *Symphonie fantastique*, III, mm. 20–37 (Ex. 8-36, F major, 6/8): solo flute + Vln 1 (soli)
  in unison pp, Vln 2/Vla/Vlc pizz pp chords; later a ppp clarinet solo sustained note with cresc. poco a
  poco and a p horn solo answer.
- Brahms, Symphony No. 1, II, mm. 1–6 (Ex. 8-37, E major, 3/4, Andante sostenuto): Bassoon 1 an octave
  below the Vln 1 theme, p→pp; horns add a stopped (gestopft) pp chord with a < > swell.
- Mozart, *Le nozze di Figaro* overture (mentioned, not shown): two bassoons double the bass line of
  the opening theme.
- Dvořák, *Carnival* overture, mm. 25–31 (Ex. 8-38, A major, Presto, ff tutti): the full-woodwind
  doubling of R10, with brass chords, timpani roll, cymbals, tambourine and triangle trills.
- Tchaikovsky, Symphony No. 6, I, mm. 237–244 (Ex. 8-39, Allegro vivo): starts with 4 horns in p poco
  cresc. triplet repeated chords, mp low brass and low strings; clarinets, then flutes, join the violin
  figure as the line rises; piccolo enters only at the ff arrival (m. 243), where flutes, piccolo,
  clarinets, oboes and violins share the figure in octaves over sustained brass.
- Copland, *Appalachian Spring*, from rehearsal 70 (Ex. 8-40, captioned "c. 70–71", about 10 bars,
  "Moving forward"): Adler's prototype of discreet doubling. The string-solo phrase and the wind phrase,
  heard separately before, return together in a 3rd statement before the coda. Strings mf sonore →
  pp; Cl 2 + muted horn under the low Vln 2; Ob 1 dropped at bar 5 for Fl 2; the final chord without
  its 5th (G) resembles the end of Stravinsky's *Symphony of Psalms*, but dark and subdued, not
  brilliant.

**Agent applicability**
- Default doubling of a string melody: winds at the octave above (Fl/Picc over Vln 1) or below
  (Bsn under Vln 1); keep unison doublings for colour (Fl + Vln 1 pp solo) or for tutti weight.
- In a loud tutti, map winds to string parts: Fl/Picc→Vln 1 (8va), Ob+Cl→Vla, Bsn→Vlc (8va + unison).
- Fade-out (diminuendo) by MIDI: end doubling notes one by one; swap the low oboe for flute when the
  line falls into the oboe's bottom register (roughly Bb3–E4, MIDI 58–64, inferred).
- Rising crescendo: keep brass CC1/velocity low (mp) under strings and woodwinds; bring the piccolo in
  only at the peak.

## 8 (cont.) New types of articulation for woodwinds (scan p.288–292, printed p.283–287)

**Concepts**
- Woodwinds have two basic articulations: slurred (ligado) and tongued (picado). Tonguing can be
  single, double or triple, and hard or soft.
- Pulsating unison (unísono "pulsante"): two winds share a melody in unison; one holds the long notes,
  the other re-articulates the same pitch, dividing each duration into 2 or 3 repeated notes.
- Mixed articulation in one line (combinación de articulaciones en la misma línea melódica): the same
  gesture is played at once by two instruments, one slurred and one tongued/staccato.

**Rules**
- R17 [ALLOWED] Pulsating unison: the oboe repeats the flute's sustained notes in duplets or triplets
  (portato). *Why:* the melody feels more animated than a plain rhythmic unison.
- R18 [PREFER] Use pulsating unison with instruments of contrasting tone quality (flute + oboe). As both
  descend into their low register, the pulsing oboe line stands out over the calmer flute.
- R19 [ALLOWED] Split woodwinds so one instrument plays a gesture slurred while another plays the same
  gesture tongued. The result is like divided violins with the 1sts arco and the 2nds pizzicato on the
  same gesture.
- R20 [PREFER] In such a texture, keep fully slurred articulation for the cantabile melody and one
  characteristic accompanying figure, so they stand apart from the tongued lines (Stravinsky: melody in
  Ob 1 + 3 and the English-horn arpeggio are the only all-legato lines).

**Examples**
- Debussy, *La Mer*, III, mm. 157–170 (Ex. 8-41, Db major, "Plus calme et très expressif", Retardez):
  solo flute + solo oboe pp in pulsating unison, the oboe in triplet/duplet repeated notes. Underneath:
  two harps in continuous pp arpeggios; Vln 1 (first desk of each section, soli) holding a high pp
  pedal; Vln 2/Vla tremolo *sur la touche*; Vlc molto pp and 4 solo basses sustaining; horns on held pp
  notes.
- Stravinsky, *Symphony of Psalms*, I, mm. 26–36 (Ex. 8-42, ♩=92): 4 oboes, English horn and 3 bassoons
  carry mixed articulations (Ob 2/4 and Bsn 1–3 tongued against slurred partners) under the
  alto and then SATB chorus; cellos double the choral line cantabile.

**Agent applicability**
- MIDI recipe for pulsating unison: flute = long notes; oboe = same pitches as 2–3 repeated short notes
  per flute note (portato, short gaps, velocity at the same level), both pp–p.
- Mixed articulation: duplicate a figure on two tracks; one track legato (overlapping notes or legato
  patch), the other staccato/short patch, same pitches and rhythm.

## 8 (cont.) Special effects (scan p.293–295, printed p.288–290)

**Rules**
- R21 [ALLOWED] Experiment freely with new woodwind sounds, but [PREFER] check the effect's playability
  with an experienced professional orchestral player first.
- R22 [MUST] Give clear instructions for producing any non-standard effect at the start of the score.
  *Why:* most problems in contemporary scores come from notation that is unfamiliar to players and not
  clear enough.
- R23 [PREFER] Use extended woodwind techniques mainly in solo/chamber writing. *Why:* many effects are
  soft and work better with small groups in small halls; only some players can or will play them.
- R24 [AVOID] Assuming multiphonics from European catalogues (e.g. Bartolozzi, *New Sounds for
  Woodwind*) will work on all instruments: some are very hard on US-made instruments, because results
  depend on the instrument's make. [era: late-20th-c. avant-garde]

**Examples**
- G. Schuller, *Seven Studies on Themes of Paul Klee*, "Arab Village", mm. 60–73 (Ex. 8-43, Allegro
  ♩≈136): oboe, harp (*près de la table*) and solo viola in rhythmic unison imitate an Arab dance, using
  quarter-tones ("R" = ¼ tone lower) and changing meters (2/4, 3/8, 5/8, 7/8), over a cello drone
  "much bow". The score retunes two harp strings a ¼ tone down and gives oboe fingerings for ¼-tone-flat
  Bb4 and Eb5 [?].
- Penderecki, *Dies Irae*, "Apocalypse", mm. 2–4 (Ex. 8-44): flutes, piccolo, 3 oboes, 2 alto saxes,
  baritone sax, 3 bassoons and contrabassoon first hit their highest possible note (arrow notehead) sff,
  then trill on specified pitches (given as cue notes) until the end of a thick line with an arrow.
  Adler says Polish scores of the period are full of such effects.
- Stachowski, *Irisation for Orchestra*, III, mm. 79–83 (Ex. 8-45): 3 oboes, 3 clarinets and 3 bassoons
  remove the reed/mouthpiece (boquilla) and blow through it alone, in rhythmic noise notation, f.

**Agent applicability**
- Sample-based MIDI rarely covers these effects. Emulate "highest possible note + trill" with a top-of-range
  note into a trill articulation, and quarter-tones with pitch bend (needs per-voice channels).
  (inferred)

## 8 (cont.) Transcribing piano music for woodwinds and strings (scan p.296–298, printed p.291–293)

**Rules**
- R25 [MUST] Before orchestrating another composer's piece, study how that composer orchestrates in other
  works. Note their favourite woodwinds, the figures they give the woodwinds and their typical
  doublings, so the new version keeps their style.
- R26 [PREFER] When piano material implies a harmonic pedal, give the sustained pedal notes to winds.
  For a Turkish (alla Turca) character, use double reeds (oboes, bassoons) for the held tonic and
  dominant.
- R27 [PREFER] Realise the piano's spread harmony (disposición dispersa) by adding a sustained pedal,
  octave doublings (e.g. after a double bar, for the repeat/contrast) and extra 3rds/chord tones. The
  result sounds fuller than the piano.
- R28 [PREFER] Match the composer's own orchestral habits. Schumann orchestrated heavily with many octave
  doublings, so octave doubling suits a Schumann transcription.

**Examples**
- Mozart, Piano Sonata K. 331, III (Alla Turca), mm. 1–8 (Ex. 8-46, A minor, 2/4): melody in Vln 1 + Vln 2
  unison; the left-hand repeated chords go to divisi violas (eighths); cello + bass pizz take the bass
  notes. 2 oboes + 2 bassoons hold tonic/dominant pedals mf, then, from bar 5, punch sf grace-note
  figures in octaves.
- Schumann, "Melodie" (piano piece), mm. 1–8 (Ex. 8-47, C major, Moderato): pairs of winds only
  (2 Fl, 2 Ob, 2 Cl, 2 Bsn). Melody in Fl 1 + Ob 1; the clarinets hold a pp pedal; the bassoons (tenor
  clef) play the left-hand broken-chord eighths. After the double bar (m. 5) the flutes and oboes add
  3rds and octaves, the clarinets take the eighth-note figure, and the bassoons hold the pedal.

**Agent applicability**
- Piano-to-orchestra recipe: melody → violins or a Fl/Ob pair; left-hand broken chords → violas,
  clarinets or bassoons in eighths; the bass → low strings pizz (or bassoon); then add 1 sustained wind
  pedal (tonic/dominant) the piano can only imply with the sustain pedal.
- Fill out the piano's chords with extra 3rds and chord tones in the winds; double the melody at the
  octave for the second phrase.

## Chapter close and study list (scan p.298–299, printed p.293–294)

- Adler holds that orchestration can be taught by training the ear: get to know the orchestral
  repertoire, analyse every sound you hear, then reproduce it in your own scores. He points ahead to
  brass and percussion (ch. 11–14) and writing for full orchestra (ch. 15).
- The list of additional passages for study (pasajes adicionales para el estudio) is grouped by flute/
  piccolo, oboe/English horn, clarinet family, bassoon/contrabassoon, saxophone and works with very large
  woodwind sections (e.g. Ravel *Daphnis*, Stravinsky *Rite of Spring*); see printed p.293–294.
