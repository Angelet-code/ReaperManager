## 15 Bloch, *Schelomo*, bars 228–243 (Ex. 15-31; scan p.592–595, printed p.591–595)

The analysis text starts on printed p.590 (previous range); only its end is here (printed p.595).

**What the score shows** (quarter = 108–112, 4/4 then 3/4, 2/2 "Più vivo"; full orchestra + solo cello)
- b.228: one tutti accented *f* eighth-note hit (cello solo *ff*), then trombones + tuba hold a pedal *sfp*
  and timpani roll *sf* → *p*, *poco cresc.* Background held under the active layers.
- b.229–234: strings in octaves on a triplet gesture; violins then hold a long note "senza dim." while low
  winds (bass clarinet, bassoons, contrabassoon), horns 1–2 and violas/cellos/basses carry the triplet
  figure; upper woodwinds (flutes, piccolo, oboes, English horn, clarinets) take an accented
  sixteenth-triplet line *a2*, in octaves; trumpets have their own rhythmic sextuplet gesture.
- b.235 ff. (Più vivo): brass (horns 3–4, trumpets, trombones, tuba, double basses) play a descending
  quarter-note line *mf marc. molto*; the string section plays a fast accented sixteenth figure in unison/
  octaves; woodwinds and horns 1–2 hold chords *cresc. poco a poco*; bass drum rolls *p* with timpani sticks
  ("becch. di timpano").
- b.240–243: flutes and piccolo (8va) join the string figure; brass line becomes chromatic, *cresc.*

**Rules (from the closing text, printed p.595)**
- R1 [ALLOWED] Reinforce a middle-plane string theme with first flute (unison/octave) to keep it present as
  the texture thickens (b.241).
- R2 [PREFER] Support a long orchestral *crescendo* with a percussion roll: first timpani, then bass drum.
- R3 [ALLOWED] Bass drum played with timpani sticks: more articulated and less loud than with a normal
  beater, though still somewhat "wilder" than the timpani.
- R4 [MUST] Start the supporting rolls *piano* before the crescendo. *Why:* a *ff* roll would cover the
  orchestra and shift the emphasis away from the combined themes.
- R5 [PREFER] Build a climax by stacking several rhythmic/gestural themes into a heterogeneous web that
  raises tension and level; then, at the climax (b.244), let one theme emerge alone. *Why:* after the
  combination it sounds completely new, even if heard several times before.

**Agent applicability**
- Climax build: 3 layers (sustained wind chords cresc. poco a poco = background; ostinato string figure =
  middle; marcato brass line = foreground), timpani/bass-drum roll entering at *p* (CC1/velocity low) and
  growing; at the climax bar drop the layering and state the main theme.

## 15 Schoenberg, *Five Orchestral Pieces* Op.16 No.5 "Das obligate Rezitativ", bars 421–455 (Ex. 15-32/33; scan p.596–600, printed p.595–599)

**Concepts**
- Hauptstimme (voz principal, sign H with bracket): the principal voice; a closing bracket marks where a part
  stops being principal.
- Nebenstimme (voz subsidiaria, sign N): the secondary voice. Footnote: in parts written in his late US years
  Schoenberg marked them P and S in a box.
- Orchestration "by colour mixing" rather than "by section": colours of all sections are blended for each
  gesture.
- Schoenberg's view: any instrument can carry any function (foreground, middle ground, background).

**Rules**
- R6 [PREFER] In a dense contrapuntal texture, mark the principal voice one dynamic level above the
  subsidiary voices. Ex.: b.423–424, cello Hauptstimme *p*; subsidiary ideas (trumpet 2, D clarinet,
  trombone 3, English horn, even flutes) *pp*.
- R7 [MUST] Give each simultaneous fragment to an instrument or group that can be heard clearly in the
  texture.
- R8 [ALLOWED] A principal gesture on a single exposed solo instrument needs no doubling when the texture
  around it is light (oboe, b.425–426).
- R9 [PREFER] When the brass writing thickens, reinforce the principal line with extra woodwinds (flutes +
  2nd violins b.431–433 get clarinets added at b.432).
- R10 [PREFER] When a complete Hauptstimme sits in one inner instrument (clarinet 3) and a prominent active
  counter-gesture competes (English horn + clarinet 2 + violas, b.429), let other instruments trace the
  outline of the principal line (clarinets 1–2, then oboes, b.427–429).
- R11 [PREFER] Make doublings only at the unison or the octave in this kind of texture.
- R12 [PREFER] To make a very wide-ranging line sound smooth, hand it through instruments that cover each
  register, adding them discreetly (b.447–451, Ex. 15-33, *fff*):
  - start on a woodwind blend (flutes 1–2, oboes 1–2, clarinets 1–3);
  - on the high note reached by a leap of a 10th, add 2nd violins and D (piccolo) clarinet, also to
    compensate for the oboes, weak at the top;
  - at the end of b.448 add English horn, bass clarinet and bassoons to cover the register gap as the line
    descends;
  - 2nd violins bring string tone into the blend; violas (helped by 1st violins) finish the phrase.
- R13 [ALLOWED] A doubling instrument may enter on a dissonant note to add momentary tension: 1st violins
  enter on E6 (MIDI 88) at b.448, "resolved" on C#4 (MIDI 61) at b.450.
- R14 [ALLOWED] Give a soft, high, lyrical solo to muted first trombone instead of cello (b.452–456).
- R15 [ALLOWED] Use soft, carefully spaced woodwind chords as a colour (b.455; spacing not described in text).

**Exceptions / conditions**
- Adler notes this passage sounds closer to the rich 19th-c. orchestral sound than to lean 20th-c. scoring
  (esp. b.434–439); what is modern is the use of extreme registers, which adds tension and expansiveness.

**Examples (score, what it shows)**
- b.432–445: *ff*–*fff* tutti, piccolos/flutes/oboes/clarinets *zu 2* on the Hauptstimme with 1st/2nd
  violins; horns marked "Schalltrichter oben" (bells up); cellos and basses "3 fach get." (divided in 3);
  harp doubles the bass line; trumpet/trombone mutes come off ("ohne Dämpfer") for the loud section and go
  back on for the soft end ("mit Dämpfer"); harp and celesta together, then harp alone, on the last
  pointillist notes.

**Agent applicability**
- Tag one track per bar as Hauptstimme; set its CC1/velocity ~1 dynamic step (about +10–15 CC values,
  inferred) above all others.
- For a long line spanning > 2 octaves, split it across instruments by register and overlap them at the
  handover, rather than forcing one instrument through its weak extremes.

## 15 Orchestrating a melody or primary gesture (orquestación de una melodía o gesto primario) (scan p.600–602, printed p.599–601)

Model: Berlioz, *Le Carnaval romain* overture, English horn solo b.21–28 (Ex. 15-34; Andante sostenuto,
3/4, *mf espress.*; EH sounds a P5 (7) lower than written). Melody starts on concert C4 (MIDI 60).

**Choosing the solo instrument**
- R16 [ALLOWED] This melody (concert C4 and up, about an octave and a half) can be played by flutes, oboes,
  clarinet, solo bassoon, all strings, trumpet, horn and trombone.
- R17 [AVOID] Solo bassoon for it: sounds somewhat high and forced in that register.
- R18 [AVOID] Double bass for it: sounds clumsy.
- R19 [AVOID] Trombone for it: the top notes sound tense, and in that intense high register it cannot give
  the calm elegance the melody needs.
- R20 [PREFER] The best choices are English horn, alto flute (flauta en SOL) and soprano, alto or tenor
  saxophone.
- R21 [MUST] Choose the soloist whose comfortable register contains the melody and whose timbre and
  emotional character fit it. The same melody an octave higher or lower may need a different instrument.
- R22 [MUST] Take the melody's dynamic level into account. Use knowledge of each instrument's strong and
  weak registers.

**Techniques to raise a melody's level or importance**
- R23 [ALLOWED] (1) Unison doubling (duplicación al unísono), preferably with acoustically compatible
  instruments. The usual partners:
  - English horn + oboe, bassoon, muted trumpet, viola or cello;
  - flute + violin or clarinet (viola or cello if the register allows);
  - oboe + English horn, flute, muted trumpet, horn, violin or viola;
  - clarinet + flute, violin, viola, cello, horn or even trumpet;
  - bassoon + oboe, English horn, any string, soft trombone or tuba.
- R24 [PREFER] Weigh every blend before choosing a unison doubling, and keep unison doublings mainly for
  *tutti* passages and very specific effects. *Why:* a unison doubling changes the basic timbre of the line.
- R25 [PREFER] (2) Octave doubling (duplicación a la octava): add instruments one or more octaves above or
  below, like organ stops (4′ and 2′ add one or two octaves up, 16′ and 32′ one or two down).
- R26 [PREFER] A three-octave melodic doubling is preferable and usable in many passages. Adler's sample
  set: 2 flutes and piccolo, English horn, 2 oboes, bass clarinet, Bb clarinet, Eb clarinet.
- R27 [ALLOWED] (3) Double only some notes of the melody, or give the doubling instrument an elaboration of
  the melody. See also Debussy *La Mer* (Ex. 8-41, earlier chapter).

**Examples**
- Ex. 15-35, two hypothetical elaborations of the Berlioz melody, both over the English horn line:
  (a) a flowing sixteenth/eighth ornamental counter-line a 5th–octave above (instrument label cut off in
  the scan [?], probably flute); (b) oboe 1 in a dotted, more rhythmic paraphrase of the same notes.

**Agent applicability**
- Melody track selection: check the line's span against each candidate's comfortable range in
  `data/instruments.json`; avoid any candidate for which the line sits in its top 4–5 semitones (inferred
  from R17/R19).
- To make a theme louder without changing its colour much, prefer an octave doubling over a unison one. Keep
  unison doublings for tutti.

## 15 Orchestral means for special effects (usos de la orquesta para la creación de efectos especiales) (scan p.602–608, printed p.601–607)

### Sforzandi and forte subito piano (scan p.602–603, printed p.601–602)

**Rules**
- R28 [PREFER] In a *sforzando* passage, an accent alone is often not enough. Build the *sf* from two
  combined colours (Ex. 15-36):
  1. string *pizzicato* *sf* on the attack of a long woodwind note (flute, oboe, clarinet *sf* whole note);
     see Beethoven Sym. 1, I, opening;
  2. a short trumpet *sf* note (trumpets 1–2, an eighth) over sustained strings *sf*;
  3. a short oboe *sf* note over sustained strings *sf* → *pp*;
  4. a viola *pizzicato* (divided, *sff*) under sustained violins *pp*.
- R29 [MUST] For *forte subito piano*: one section plays a *ff* note or chord; before it ends, a different
  section or combination enters *p* and holds that level. When the loud sound drops out, the listener hears
  a sudden *piano*. The two groups must overlap (Ex. 15-37: 3 C trumpets *ff* chord with 3 flutes entering
  *pp* under it; divided 1st violins *ff* with divided 2nd violins entering *pp*).

**Agent applicability**
- *sf* hit: add a 1-beat (or shorter) pizz/trumpet/oboe note at high velocity on the attack of a held note
  in another track. Do not raise only the held note's CC1.
- *fp* subito: end the *ff* note 1–2 beats after the *p* group has entered on the same chord. Do not cut the
  CC1 of a single section.

**Tags:** #orchestration/effects/sforzando #orchestration/effects/subito-piano

### Dovetailing (la "cola de milano" o superposición) (scan p.603, printed p.602)

- R30 [MUST] When a passage is handed between similar instruments for a seamless colour change, make the
  seams inaudible. Each new instrument enters on the last note of the previous one, so both share it (Ex.
  15-38a: Presto 4/4, a rising triplet arpeggio passed cello → viola → 2nd violin → 1st violin).
- R31 [ALLOWED] Hand over without overlap only when you want each group (here each six-note group) to sound
  accented (Ex. 15-38b).

**Agent applicability**
- Relay arpeggios across string tracks: each track's segment starts on the previous segment's last note
  (shared pitch, overlapping duration). This also hides sample-library attack differences.

**Tags:** #orchestration/dovetailing #orchestration/strings/relay

### Colouring a note (coloración de una nota) (scan p.604, printed p.603)

- R32 [ALLOWED] Colouring a single held note: pass one sustained pitch between instruments, each entering
  before the previous one releases, all at the same soft dynamic (Ex. 15-39: a held C, probably C5 / MIDI
  72 [?], oboe 1 → flute 1 → bassoon (tenor clef) → C clarinet 1 → English horn, all *pp*).
- R33 [ALLOWED] Colouring each note with a different instrument (Ex. 15-40, 3/4, *p*): successive melody
  notes on clarinet 2, alto flute (Fl. in G), muted trumpet 1, viola, English horn, 2nd violin *pizz.* and
  a cello note marked with a harmonic circle. The second staff gives the same line for muted C trumpet 1
  (comparison/reference, inferred).
- R34 [ALLOWED] Use alternative fingerings to colour one note with the same or similar instruments.
  Adler calls this a relatively recent technique. Ex. 15-41: flutes 1 and 2 alternate one sustained
  pitch, each with a different fingering (fingering charts printed).
- R35 [ALLOWED] On strings, colour a note by specifying the string it is played on (see ch.3). On brass,
  use alternate valve fingerings, but ask a professional brass player first for the best way.

**Agent applicability**
- A held pedal note can "breathe" timbrally by cross-fading CC11 between 2–5 tracks on the same pitch.
  Overlap each pair by about a beat.
- A note-per-instrument line (R33) is only practical when every instrument can play its single note in a
  comfortable register at the same dynamic.

**Tags:** #orchestration/timbre/note-colouring #orchestration/extended-techniques

### Pointillist orchestration and Klangfarbenmelodie (orquestación puntillista y Klangfarbenmelodie) (scan p.605–608, printed p.604–607)

**Concepts**
- Pointillism (orquestación puntillista): like colouring each note of a melody with a different instrument
  (R33). Adler compares it to French pointillist painting of the early 20th c., where small dots of pure
  colour are set side by side. Webern was its main exponent.
- Klangfarbenmelodie (melodía de timbres): "tone-colour melody". Footnote: the term is often used for a
  repeated note coloured by several instruments. The archetype, though, is a continuous phrase with almost
  every note on a different instrument.

**Rules**
- R36 [PREFER] [era: Second Viennese School, 1909–1940s] In pointillist writing, give each note its own
  dynamic and add characteristic octave displacements (Ex. 15-42, 3/4: trumpet 1 *p*, flute 1 *f* (high),
  viola *mf*, muted trumpet 1 *pp*, 2nd violin *pizz. sf*, cello *p*).
- R37 [MUST] In pointillist texture, notate durations, articulations and dynamics precisely, and have them
  followed strictly. *Why:* the listener (and the player) must reconstruct the line from isolated notes.
- R38 [ALLOWED] Vary the playing technique on successive notes of one instrument as a colour change (Webern
  Op.21, b.5–7: violas play three successive notes, two *pizz.* and one *arco*).
- R39 [ALLOWED] Apply pointillism or Klangfarben to tonal music too, especially with octave transpositions
  that make a "doubly staggered" melody (Webern, *Passacaglia* Op.1: a Bach/Brahms-modelled tonal theme
  that still sounds 20th-c.).

**Examples**
- Webern, *Symphony* Op.21, I, b.1–14 (Ex. 15-43/44; study b.35–39 too). Row P-0: A F# G Ab E F | B Bb D
  C# C Eb; the 2nd hexachord is the retrograde of the 1st, a tritone (6) higher. Scoring: clarinet, bass
  clarinet, 2 horns, harp, violins 1–2, viola, cello. Ruhig schreitend, half note = ca.50, 2/2. Dynamics
  only *pp*–*mp*, nearly every note on a different instrument or technique: pizz/arco alternation, harp
  harmonics, horn stopped then open (gedämpft → offen, b.13), accents and tenuto on single notes. Ex.
  15-44a condenses the canonic row statements (P-0, I-0, I-8, P-4, I-3, P-9, P-5, I-7) on four staves; Ex.
  15-44b is the full score. The texture is very thin, yet it gives an impression of contrapuntal density
  because the colour keeps changing.

**Agent applicability**
- A Klangfarben line: one note per track, each track at its own CC1/velocity. Use octave displacement
  (±12/24) between neighbouring notes and leave silence between them. Use solo patches, not ensembles.
- Use it sparingly in film/tonal contexts for a "fragmented", glassy or modernist colour.
