# Walter Piston, Orchestration — scans s050L–s059R (printed p.100–119)

Double-bass (contrabajo) sounds an octave below written. Concert open strings of the 4-string bass:
E1 (28), A1 (33), D2 (38), G2 (43); written E2, A2, D3, G3. Below, "written" is marked; MIDI numbers are
concert (sounding) pitch, converted by subtracting 12 from the written pitch.

## Ch.5 The Double-Bass — five-string bass and E-string extension (printed p.100)

**Concepts**
- Five-string bass (contrabajo de cinco cuerdas): extra low string tuned to C, sounding C1 (24); larger body.
- E-string extension (extensión de Mi / máquina de Do): fingerboard extension past the pegbox with
  keys near the nut that stop C#, D, D#, E remotely; sounding down to C1 (24). Rests with E stopped.

**Rules**
- R1 [era: 1955] Five-string basses are uncommon; some orchestras have none. Extensions were spreading,
  some whole sections equipped.
- R2 [MUST] Treat the 4-string bass (lowest E1 = 28) as the normal instrument and design bass parts for it.
- R3 [AVOID] Notes below E1 (C1–Eb1, 24–27); if indispensable, treat as exceptional and assume only one or
  two players (or none) can play them.
- R4 [PREFER] Where such low notes occur, write the passage in octaves (divisi), showing clearly what the
  players whose lowest note is E must do.
- R5 [MUST] To keep the low notes from being taken up an octave, mark "five-string basses only" or
  "not an octave higher" (or similar).

**Examples**
- Mussorgsky-Ravel, *Pictures at an Exhibition* (Ex.120): fast Allegro alla breve bass passage written
  in octaves where it dips below E [? exact notes].
- Mahler, *Das Lied von der Erde* (Ex.121): heavy ff→pp figure with trills ending on a sustained low C
  marked for basses with low C only.

**Agent applicability**
- Default DB floor = MIDI 28. Notes 24–27 only as a lower-octave divisi doubling of a line that is also
  present at +12; never as the only bass.

## Ch.5 — Fingering and positions (printed p.101–102)

**Rules**
- R6 Hand span from 1st to 4th finger in lower positions = major second (2 semitones); fingering 1-2-4,
  with 3rd finger joined to 4th until 6th position, where 3 and 4 become independent.
- R7 [ALLOWED] Some players stretch a whole tone between fingers 1 and 2 (extended position), but the
  basic system is R6.
- R8 There are twelve positions per octave (one per semitone); numbering (1st, 2nd…) follows the diatonic
  step under finger 1, the in-between ones are half positions (½, 1½, 2½…). Other numbering systems exist.
- R9 The thumb comes into play above the 7th position (on the G string, above roughly written D4/sounding
  D3 [?]).
- R10 [PREFER] Scales/keys that use open strings need fewer shifts (Fig.36: G major is easier than
  B major).
- R11 Chromatic scales are fingered with the repeating pattern 2-4-2-4 or 1-2-4-1-2-4.

**Agent applicability**
- Fast bass lines are easier in sharp keys around the open strings (E, A, D, G); in remote keys, slow
  the figuration or simplify.

## Ch.5 — Clefs and range (printed p.102)

**Rules**
- R12 Clefs: bass, tenor, treble (same as cello), all sounding an octave lower than written (except
  harmonics in treble clef, R17).
- R13 [ALLOWED] High notes are very effective and not very difficult apart from intonation.
  [PREFER] approach them by step or small intervals.
- R14 Practical upper limit (excluding harmonics): the D an octave and a fifth above the open G =
  written D5, sounding D4 (62).

**Examples**
- Shostakovich, *Symphony No.5* (Ex.122): ff accented line exceeding the limit, basses in unison with
  violas, cellos and bassoons (into treble clef).

**Agent applicability**
- Bowed DB range for normal writing: 28–62. Above ~55 only stepwise, preferably doubled.

## Ch.5 — Harmonics (printed p.102–104)

**Rules**
- R15 [AVOID] Artificial harmonics: possible only in high positions, impractical in the orchestra.
- R16 [PREFER] Natural harmonics are especially useful: medium pitch range, easy to produce (long strings),
  excellent quality.
- R17 Notation: harmonics written in treble clef are notated at actual pitch (to save leger lines);
  in bass/tenor clef the usual octave transposition applies.
- R18 Fig.37 (G string, sounding G2): natural harmonics sound the partials of the open string, roughly
  G3 (55), D4 (62), G4 (67), B4 (71), D5 (74), [F5?], G5 (79); the same pitches are obtainable on nodes
  from mid-string toward the bridge and toward the nut. Other strings: transpose down by fourths
  (D string a 4th lower, etc.) [? exact series].
- R19 The harmonic touched at a minor third above the nut speaks more clearly on the bass than on any
  other string instrument (inferred: 6th partial, 2 octaves + fifth above the open string; e.g. D5
  (74) on the G string).

**Examples**
- Ravel, *Le Tombeau de Couperin* (Ex.123): pp 3/4 melody in natural harmonics alternating G and D
  strings ("sur Sol / sur Ré"), sounding in the treble-to-tenor register.
- Stravinsky, *Concerto en Ré* (Ex.124): sustained pp chord in harmonics by 4 solo basses + 1 solo cello;
  the cello supplies a C natural the bass cannot produce at that pitch; the 4th bass plays a harmonic
  double-stop.

**Agent applicability**
- For an eerie high pp bass colour, use natural-harmonic pitches only (open-string partials of
  E1/A1/D2/G2), as sustained notes or slow melody; never fast.

## Ch.5 — Double-stops and chords (printed p.104)

**Rules**
- R20 Playable double-stops: thirds, fourths, fifths, and any interval with one open-string note.
- R21 [AVOID] Double-stops and 3–4 note chords are rare in orchestral bass parts.
- R22 [AVOID] Small harmonic intervals in the low register sound poor (overtone conflict); for special
  effects, divide the notes among two or more players instead.

**Examples**
- Kodály, *Dances of Galánta* (Ex.125): unusual ff accented sequence of parallel fifth double-stops.

## Ch.5 — Bowing (printed p.105–106)

**Concepts**
- German bow (arco alemán): held with thumb on top of the stick, saw-like grip, thumb presses down.
- French bow (arco francés): like a large cello bow, finger pressure. Choice is the player's schooling.

**Rules**
- R23 All bow strokes of the other strings are available, but with more inertia (short heavy bow, thick
  strings, cumbersome instrument).
- R24 [AVOID] Over-long legato slurs.
- R25 A full section tolerates a slower bow (longer slurs) than a solo bass; longer slurs are possible in
  soft dynamics than in loud ones.
- R26 [era: 19th-c. scores] Long pedal notes lasting many bars: players change bow freely and staggered
  so the section never changes together; tone stays continuous.
- R27 [PREFER] Staccato on the string; controlled bouncing bow (spiccato) is feasible and common.
- R28 The modern section can play delicate light passages.
- R29 Sul tasto and sul ponticello are regular on the bass; bass col legno staccato is more musical than on
  other strings (greater resonance).
- R30 Bowed tremolo is very common in bass parts, often sul tasto or sul ponticello.
- R31 [AVOID] Fingered tremolo in low positions; a minor-third stretch becomes feasible above 2nd position.
- R32 [ALLOWED] All major and minor trills are excellent.

**Examples**
- Wagner, *Tristan und Isolde* (Ex.126): long pp slurred chromatic line Langsam, then pizz.
- R. Strauss, *Don Juan* (Ex.127): light giocoso p grazioso staccato with grace notes and triplets.
- Rimsky-Korsakov, *Scheherazade* (Ex.128): fast detached sixteenths in a long gradual crescendo f→ff.
- Mahler, *Symphony No.9* (Ex.129): Ländler line with marked bowings, staccato, trills, mf→ppp.

**Agent applicability**
- DB legato: keep slurs to ~1 bar at f, longer at p. Sustained pedals can last indefinitely (section).
- Tremolo pedal in DB = idiomatic suspense texture; trills fine.

## Ch.5 — Pizzicato (printed p.107)

**Rules**
- R33 [PREFER] Basses play pizzicato much more often than other strings: resonant, gives relief and
  variety, lightens and clarifies the whole texture.
- R34 [ALLOWED] Basses may double in pizzicato a line the cellos play arco.
- R35 Plucked with the first finger, bow held in the hand. Tone and dynamics vary with plucking point.
- R36 [AVOID] Careless low pizz: risk of a booming or dull thud.
- R37 Pizz upper limit rarely exceeded: written B4, sounding B3 (59).

**Examples**
- R. Strauss, *Also sprach Zarathustra* (Ex.130): pp pizz line climbing in a crescendo to the high B
  (written B4) then descending f.
- Ravel, *Rapsodie espagnole* (Ex.131): pp pizz ostinato not doubled by cellos; bass clarinet underlines
  the accented bars.

**Agent applicability**
- Pizz DB range: 28–59. Pizz bass under arco cellos is a stock lightening device; a bass clarinet on
  accents can add body to an undoubled pizz.

## Ch.5 — Mutes (printed p.107–108)

**Rules**
- R38 The bass mute must be heavy (large bridge).
- R39 Use mutes for homogeneity when the rest of the strings are muted, and for a veiled bass sound.

**Examples**
- Ravel, *La Valse* (Ex.132): opening pp, muted basses div. in 3, alone: two parts in measured tremolo,
  the third pizz.

## Ch.5 — The harmonic bass (printed p.108–109)

**Rules**
- R40 [PREFER] In >90% of orchestral music the basses provide the harmonic foundation in octaves with the
  cellos (DB sounding an octave below Vc).
- R41 [ALLOWED] When cellos are busy, bassoons, horns or violas can take the upper octave of the bass.
- R42 The upper octave clarifies the bass (reinforces its octave overtone); the difference tone may
  suggest an even deeper octave.
- R43 The octave bass's upper partials enrich the whole sonority but often clash with upper notes.
- R44 [AVOID] Soft minor triad placed so the bass's major-third overtone (5th partial, 2 octaves + major
  third above the bass) competes with the chord's minor third (Fig.38).
- R45 [MUST] Fix R44 by lowering the basses' dynamic and strengthening the second violins (minor third).
- R46 [ALLOWED, rare] Bass as an independent voice without cello octave doubling: exceptional in practice.
- R47 [AVOID] Excessive use of the lowest tones: makes the orchestra heavy and overponderous.

**Examples**
- Fig.38: minor triad in Vn I/II over Va, Vc and DB; bass overtones produce a major third against Vn II's
  minor third.
- Beethoven, *Symphony No.9* finale (Ex.133): theme in violas+cellos, bassoon counter-melody, basses an
  independent line (no octave doubling).

**Agent applicability**
- Default: DB = Vc − 12 on the bass line. In soft minor chords, set DB velocity/CC1 lower and the minor
  third voice louder. Don't park DB on E1–A1 for long stretches.

## Ch.5 — Melodic uses (printed p.109–111)

**Rules**
- R48 [ALLOWED] The harmonic bass can be a real melodic bass line.
- R49 [ALLOWED, modern] Undoubled high-register bass melodies.
- R50 [ALLOWED, modern] Basses as melodic doubling of a solo violin (an octave below).
- R51 [era: Mahler/Strauss/Ravel/Stravinsky] Modern bass parts demand agile bowing and fingering, wide
  skips and complex rhythms.

**Examples**
- Franck, *Symphony in D minor* (Ex.134): molto legato pp melodic bass line, poco cresc. then ppp.
- Riegger, *Symphony No.3* (Ex.135): fugue subject in the high bass register, unaccompanied after an
  opening cello eighth-note figure.
- Prokofiev, *Violin Concerto No.2* (Ex.136): basses (espress., mp, into treble clef) double the solo
  violin's sul G melody an octave lower; flutes and clarinets add light chords.
- R. Strauss, *Ein Heldenleben* (Ex.137): ff virtuoso wide-leap triplet arpeggios across bass, tenor and
  treble clefs.

## Ch.5 — Divided basses (printed p.112–113)

**Rules**
- R52 [ALLOWED] Basses are frequently divided in two (consistent with modern French practice of dividing
  each string section).
- R53 [PREFER] Division in octaves gives the bass octave within the DB part itself.
- R54 [AVOID] Intervals smaller than fifths or fourths in the low register.
- R55 [ALLOWED] Divide so half joins a melody while half stays on the bass; or half pizz, half arco on the
  same figure.
- R56 3–4 part chords in the low register sound percussive, especially pizz (Berlioz, *Symphonie
  fantastique*, March to the Scaffold, G minor chords).
- R57 [MUST] For clear bass chords: open position, or in the upper part of the bass range.
- R58 [ALLOWED] Limit the number of basses ("la metà", "4 bassi", "1 desk only") for better balance.

**Examples**
- Debussy, *La Mer* (Ex.138): pp divided staccato figure, mostly in wide intervals [?], ending on a chord.
- Debussy, *Pelléas et Mélisande* (Ex.139): divided basses in close intervals, pp, for a foreboding effect
  (deliberate exception to R54).
- Dvořák, *Symphony No.5* (old numbering = "New World"), Largo (Ex.140): unaccompanied pp final chord for
  basses alone, notated as double-stops but played divided in four.

**Agent applicability**
- DB divisi: octaves or fifths/fourths; close intervals only for dark/ominous effect. Bass chords: spread,
  upper range (~40–55).

## Ch.5 — The solo bass (printed p.113)

**Rules**
- R59 Solo bass is infrequent; novel effect.
- R60 [MUST] Solo bass has surprisingly little carrying power: accompany it very lightly.

**Examples**
- Ravel, *L'Enfant et les sortilèges* (Ex.141): unaccompanied espressivo solo, written for five-string
  bass ("sul Do", playable with E extension), rising to a harmonic.

## Ch.6 Woodwind Instruments — section make-up (printed p.114–115)

**Concepts**
- Woodwind (maderas): any orchestral wind that is not brass (brass = lips vibrating in a cup mouthpiece);
  material is irrelevant (flutes are metal).
- Four families: flutes, piccolo (alto flute); oboes, English horn (oboe d'amore, heckelphone); clarinets,
  bass clarinet (Eb and D clarinets, basset horn); bassoons, contrabassoon. Parenthesized = occasional.
  Families match audible tone-colour differences.

**Rules**
- R61 Woodwind by twos (a dos): 2 fl, 2 ob, 2 cl, 2 bn.
- R62 Woodwind by threes (a tres): 2 fl + picc, 2 ob + EH, 2 cl + bcl, 2 bn + cbn.
- R63 Woodwind by fours (a cuatro): 3 fl + picc, 3 ob + EH, 3 cl + bcl, 3 bn + cbn.
- R64 [era: 20th c.] Woodwind by threes is the normal modern section; treat other layouts as variations.

**Agent applicability**
- Default orchestral template: woodwinds by threes = 12 parts (2 fl, picc, 2 ob, EH, 2 cl, bcl, 2 bn,
  cbn) (inferred count).

## Ch.6 — Tone production (printed p.115–116)

**Concepts**
- Pitch of an air column depends (approximately) on its length only. Organ "foot" names: 16-ft C = C1 (24),
  8-ft C = C2 (36, cello open C), 4-ft C = C3 (48), 2-ft C = C4 (60), 1-ft C = C5 (72).
- Doubling length lowers an octave; halving raises an octave.
- Bores: clarinet = cylindrical tube stopped at the mouthpiece; oboe and bassoon = conical; flute = mostly
  cylindrical but open at the embouchure (open pipe).

**Rules**
- R65 Lengths give the same pitches for all winds, brass included, except the clarinet: a stopped
  cylindrical pipe sounds an octave lower than an open one of the same length (clarinet an octave below
  flute with equal tube length).

## Ch.6 — Shortening-hole system and fork fingering (printed p.116–118)

**Concepts**
- Six-hole pipe (3 fingers per hand; 4th fingers unused, thumbs support): basis of all woodwind
  fingering. ~21 in. = flute low D (D4, 62).
- Opening holes from the bottom upward shortens the sounding length: D E F# G A B C# (D major scale).
- Fork (cross) fingering (horquilla): closing the hole just below the sounding (open) hole flattens it a
  half step, giving in-between chromatic notes (e.g. F natural, a second F#/Gb fingering) [? full list
  in Fig.42].

**Rules**
- R66 The lowest note cannot be fork-flattened (Eb below D impossible: no further hole to close).
- R67 More fundamentals require new holes and keys (left thumb takes a hole; 4th fingers need keys);
  keys also extend the tube downward. Details per instrument in later chapters.

## Ch.6 — Overblowing (printed p.118–119)

**Concepts**
- Overblowing (sobresoplado): producing harmonics of the air column by opening a vent hole at or near a
  node (a normal tone hole, or a speaker/register/octave key). Overblown tones are treated as normal notes,
  not "harmonics".
- On the flute, and some oboe notes, harmonics can be produced by lip/air direction alone.

**Rules**
- R68 Open pipes (flute, oboe, bassoon) overblow at the octave first: e.g. flute D5 = D fingering with the
  C# hole (at half the tube length) opened as vent.
- R69 The clarinet (stopped cylinder) cannot sound even-numbered harmonics; its first overblown note is
  the 3rd harmonic, a twelfth (octave + fifth, 19 semitones) above the fundamental, via the register key.

**Agent applicability**
- Clarinet registers repeat at the twelfth, not the octave (relevant to its break and register colours,
  covered in the clarinet chapter).

## Ch.6 — Embouchure (printed p.119; continues past p.119)

**Concepts**
- Flute: lips direct an air stream onto the far edge of an oval hole.
- Oboe: double reed (two scraped cane blades on a small tube), held gently between the lips.
- Bassoon: larger double reed on a curved metal pipe (bocal) leading into the wing joint.
- Clarinet: single reed held by a metal ligature on the mouthpiece; reed against the lower lip (covering
  the lower teeth), upper teeth on the mouthpiece top.

**Rules**
- R70 Embouchure is the main factor in a wind's tone colour, together with bore length/diameter
  proportions and size/position of holes (text continues on p.120).
