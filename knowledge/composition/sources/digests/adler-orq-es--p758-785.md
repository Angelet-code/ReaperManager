## 18 Preparación de la partitura general y las partes (scan p.758–772, printed p.757–771)

**Concepts**
- Full score (partitura general): every instrument on its own staff, in fixed order.
- Reduced score (partitura reducida): after page 1, pages that show only the instruments playing in
  that passage, possibly several systems per page.
- Abbreviated / "cut-away" score (partitura abreviada): staves appear only where an instrument plays
  and stop when it rests; started by Stravinsky, common 1960s–early 1970s, no longer popular.
  `[era: mid-20th-c., 1960s–70s]`
- Condensed score (partitura condensada): the whole score compressed to 3–4 staves (usually one per
  section), all notes at concert pitch, keeping the essential melodic notes and basic rhythmic and
  harmonic elements. Requested for educational or commercial music.
- Piano reduction (reducción a piano): two staves, playable by two hands, with superfluous octave
  doublings removed.
- Cue notes (notas de entrada): small notes in a part showing another instrument's line, to help a
  re-entry after a long rest or to stand in for a missing instrument.
- "Muta in" / "cambiar a": the marking for a player who switches instrument (doubling).

**Rules — score order (= track order for an orchestral template)**
- R1 [MUST] Standard full-orchestra order, top to bottom: piccolo; flutes 1–2; oboes 1–2; English
  horn; clarinet in D or Eb; clarinets 1–2 in Bb or A; bass clarinet; bassoons 1–2; contrabassoon /
  horns 1–4; trumpets 1–3; trombones 1–3; tuba / timpani (number of drums stated at the start);
  percussion (list every instrument at the start); harp; piano (or celesta) / strings (vln 1, vln 2,
  viola, cello, double bass).
- R2 [MUST] Leave visible space between families: woodwinds | brass | percussion | harp & piano |
  strings. A conductor must see the section boundaries at a glance. *(In REAPER: one folder bus per
  family, in this order, inferred.)*
- R3 [MUST] Extra instruments go inside their own family: Wagner tubas or euphonium (bombardino)
  between the trombones and the tuba; saxophones between the bass clarinet and the bassoons.
- R4 [ALLOWED] In some North American scores saxophones sit below the bassoons, or even below the
  brass just above the timpani.
- R5 [MUST] A dedicated piccolo part sits above the flutes. If flute 2 or 3 doubles on piccolo, the
  piccolo music goes on that player's line. Alto flute (flauta en Sol) always goes below the normal
  flutes and is most often played by flute 3.
- R6 [PREFER] Usual doublings: oboe 2 or 3 to English horn, a clarinettist to bass clarinet, a
  bassoonist to contrabassoon. Mark "muta in X" early enough in both score and part; show the new key
  signature or clef at the switch point if the transposition changes.
- R7 [PREFER] Horns 1–2 share one staff and 3–4 the other. Group them 1–3 and 2–4 only when the horns
  play many high and low passages in unison (1 and 3 are the high horns).
- R8 [PREFER] Trumpets on two staves: tpt 1 alone, tpts 2–3 together. Trombones: tenor trombones 1–2
  on one staff, bass trombone (3) on the second.
- R9 [MUST] Barlines run through each section only, never through the whole score.
- R10 [MUST] Tempo marks go at the top of the score and are repeated above each section. Dynamics go
  below the staff, except when two instruments share a staff and need different dynamics.
- R11 [ALLOWED] After page 1, instrument names may be abbreviated, and the transposition need not be
  repeated unless a player changes instrument mid-work (e.g. Bb to A clarinet).
- R12 [AVOID] Omitting clefs or key signatures after the first page (a jazz-score habit).
- R13 [MUST] In reduced-score pages, keep the page-1 section order, label every instrument on every
  system, and separate systems with two thick slashes.
- R14 [MUST] In a condensed score, write every note at concert pitch.

**Rules — parts and cueing (relevant to substitution and doubling)**
- R15 [MUST] Two instruments that share a staff in the score each get their own part.
- R16 [PREFER] Give cue notes for re-entries after long rests. Mark which instrument plays the cue and
  where, and transpose the cue to the key of the part that carries it.
- R17 [PREFER] For school orchestras, cue important oboe, bassoon, viola and even horn lines in other
  parts so the line survives if the player is absent:
  - oboe or English horn lines → clarinet, violin or muted trumpet, whichever fits the register;
  - bassoon lines → clarinet or cello (sometimes an extra tenor-sax part replaces a missing bassoon);
  - missing violas → split the viola part between a "violin 3" and cello 2 (use only if there are no
    violas).
  All cues must also appear in the full score so the conductor can assign substitutes.
- R18 [PREFER] A substitute never truly replaces the original timbre, but a cue keeps the line from
  disappearing.
- R19 [MUST] Page turns: a woodwind or brass player needs rests at the end of a page to turn it.
  In string parts the inside desk player turns while the outside player keeps playing. Avoid putting
  an important string passage where half the section drops out to turn.
- R20 [ALLOWED] Copying shortcuts: 8va / 15ma / 8va bassa to avoid more than four ledger lines;
  numbered repeat bars for identical passages; "sim.", "sempre stacc.", "sempre legato" instead of
  repeating marks.
- R21 [MUST] Put rehearsal letters or numbers (or bar numbers every 5 or 10 bars) in both score and
  parts. A rehearsal letter or meter change that falls inside a multi-bar rest must break that rest.
- R22 [MUST] When two parts share a staff in notation software, write every accidental the extracted
  parts will need. Software may drop "courtesy" accidentals in chromatic music, so check that they
  stay. Software may also distort ties that cross a system break. Proof-read score and parts, then
  have a second person proof-read them.

**Examples**
- Ex.18-1 (generic layout): full-orchestra score page with 5 timpani, xylophone, triangle and snare,
  harp; shows the family brackets and one large time signature per section.
- Stravinsky, *Movements for Piano and Orchestra*, bb.13–26: cut-away score. Flute, clarinet, bass
  clarinet, piano, then viola/cello/bass pizz. *sf* stabs. Each instrument stays in its section slot
  and is named at every entry. `[era: 1950s–70s]`
- Mahler, Symphony No.1, mvt 1, bb.269–274 (full) and 275–287 (reduced): fl/ob/cl pairs doubling a
  chromatic figure *pp*; horns 1–2/3–4 on two staves with long *p* lines; harp *ff* single plucks;
  pizz. cello and bass *f* reinforcing; "geth." (divided) violins. The reduced page shows only flute,
  clarinet, horns, harp, strings: a harp ostinato "Resonanztisch… deutlich" *mf* under *sempre pp*
  strings.
- Brahms, Symphony No.1, mvt 1, bb.82–87 (full / condensed / piano): winds in octaves *zu 2* *ff*;
  horns, trumpets and timpani on a rhythmic pedal (cresc. to *f*); strings carry the theme with *sf*
  accents in the basses. The condensed version uses three groups (woodwinds; brass + timpani; strings
  on two staves). The piano version keeps melody, bass and inner harmony and drops octave doublings.
- Ex.18-7: a cello part carrying a cue from violin 2 before its own *mf* entry.

**Agent applicability**
- Build orchestral templates in R1 order, with one folder bus per family as in R2. Insert saxes,
  euphonium and Wagner tubas per R3.
- One MIDI track per player-part. Two players on one "score staff" (horns 1–2) can still be two
  tracks; give each its own dynamics (CC) when they differ (R10, R15).
- When a library lacks an instrument (e.g. no English horn or no violas), re-voice the line on the
  cue substitute from R17 in the right register, rather than dropping it.
- For a sketch or "condensed" export, write 3–4 concert-pitch staves (one per section) and remove
  octave duplications for a piano reduction.

## 19 Orquestación para banda o conjunto de viento (scan p.773–785, printed p.772–784)

**Concepts**
- Band (banda): began as an outdoor ensemble, so several players double each part for power.
  Characteristic sound: e.g. ten clarinets on one line. Slight section out-of-tuneness adds character.
- Wind ensemble (conjunto de viento): Frederick Fennell's 1952 concept (Eastman Wind Ensemble). Woodwinds,
  brass and percussion with one player per part and no duplicated parts, "like the orchestra without
  strings". `[era: post-1952 American]`
- "Bandstration" (bandestación): Adler's joking word for band-specific orchestration.
- "Reeds" (cañas) in band condensed scores means all woodwinds, not literally reed instruments.

**Rules**
- R23 [PREFER] Playing techniques and the balance of foreground / middle ground / background planes are
  the same for band and orchestra.
- R24 [PREFER] Band music routinely gives important parts to cornets, saxophones and euphoniums.
  Orchestral music uses them rarely.
- R25 [MUST] Main difference: a band writer never knows how many players will be on a part (first
  flute and second clarinet are often heavily doubled). Write parts that work with any number of players
  (inferred).
- R26 [PREFER] To get orchestral clarity inside a band, mark "solo" for one player and "tutti" when the
  whole section returns. This combines band and wind-ensemble concepts.
- R27 [ALLOWED] Piano, celesta and harp may be used in band or wind-ensemble scores; place them next to
  the percussion. Double basses (or even cellos), which add volume and smoothness to the bass, go below
  the tuba.
- R28 [MUST] Prepare band and wind-ensemble scores and parts exactly as for orchestra (ch.18).
- R29 [PREFER] Concert-band percussion is integrated like the 20th-c. orchestral section, with many
  pitched instruments (timpani, xylophone, marimba, vibraphone, crotales, rototoms). Of these, only the
  glockenspiel can be marched. Street-band percussion leads the band.

**Instrumentations (standard lists)**
- Street band (banda de calle), each line 2–20 players: piccolo in C (the Db piccolo is no longer
  used) `[era: Db piccolo obsolete]`; flutes (some may double piccolo); Bb clarinets (usually 2 parts);
  Eb alto sax; Bb tenor sax; Eb baritone sax; Bb cornets (2 parts); Bb trumpets (2 parts); horns in Eb
  or F; baritones/euphoniums; tubas or sousaphones.
- Standard concert band: piccolo; flutes (2 parts); oboes (2, may include English horn); Eb clarinet; Bb
  clarinets (3 parts); Eb alto clarinet; Bb bass clarinet; (bassoons); Bb soprano sax (some bands); Eb
  alto sax; Bb tenor sax; Eb baritone sax; Bb cornets (2–3 parts); Bb trumpets (2–3 parts); F horns
  (4 parts, sometimes Eb horns); trombones; baritone/euphonium (sometimes 2 parts); tuba; timpani;
  percussion (usually 4 players).
- Expanded concert band: fl 1, fl 2 (picc), oboe, Eb cl, solo & cl 1, cl 2, cl 3, alto cl, bass cl,
  bassoon, alto sax 1–2, tenor sax, bari sax, trumpet 1–3 (= cornet 1–3), horn 1–2 in F, trombone 1–3,
  baritone T.C. (treble clef) and B.C. (bass clef), basses (tubas), string bass, timpani. Perc. 1:
  glockenspiel, xylophone, vibraphone, marimba. Perc. 2: Chinese block, temple blocks, hand cymbals,
  suspended cymbal, triangle, snare, tenor drum, tam-tam, bass drum.
- American Wind Symphony (Pittsburgh): 4.4.4.4 / 4.4.4.1 + percussion, normally without saxes,
  euphoniums or cornets (added when needed).
- Adler's own wind-ensemble example (one per part, the composer's choice): 2 piccolos in C; 2+2 flutes;
  oboes 1–2; English horn; bassoons 1–2; contrabassoon; Eb clarinet; 4+4 clarinets (1, 2); 2 Eb alto
  clarinets; 2 Bb bass clarinets; Bb contrabass clarinet; Eb alto, Bb tenor, Eb baritone saxes; 2 Bb
  cornets; 2+2+2 Bb trumpets (1, 2, 3); 4 F horns; trombones 1–2 and bass trombones 3–4; baritones 1–2
  (T.C. and B.C.) with euphonium; 2 tubas; string bass; timpani; 2+2 percussion.
- Ex.19-1, concert-band score order, top to bottom: flutes; piccolo; oboes; English horn; Eb clarinet;
  Bb clarinets 1–3; alto clarinet; bass clarinet; contrabass clarinet; bassoons; saxophones (alto 1 or
  soprano, alto 2, tenor, baritone); Bb cornets 1–3; Bb trumpets; F horns 1–2, 3–4; trombones 1–3;
  euphoniums; tuba(s); string bass; timpani; percussion. *(Note: in band order the flutes sit above the
  piccolo, and the bassoons sit below the clarinets, unlike R1.)*

**Condensed band scores**
- R30 [ALLOWED] Condensed scores are a band tradition, now declining because sophisticated band and
  wind-ensemble works do not fit them. Some publishers still require one.
- R31 [PREFER] Typical march condensed score: line 1 = flutes, oboes, clarinets, alto saxes, trumpets,
  horns. Line 2 = bassoons, bass clarinets, tenor and bari saxes, trombones, euphoniums, tubas. Use
  generic family names in tutti and name the solo instruments. Notate the percussion in more detail
  because it is central to a march.
- R32 [PREFER] A more colourful condensed score names specific instruments on every line, with enough
  detail to rebuild a full score from it.

**Examples**
- Bagley, *National Emblem* (march, ed. Fennell), bb.1–9 and 29–38. Two staves: "High reeds 8va; brass
  loco" and "Low reeds & brass". The opening *f*<*ff* call is in octaves. The trio/strain (b.29ff) has a
  *p* chordal background in high reeds, an oboe countermelody *mf*, a trombone melody *mf*, and
  bass on beats 1 and 3. Percussion shows kettledrums; snare and field drum with R/L sticking, rolls
  and flams; cymbals and bass drum on beats 1 and 3, dynamics *p*–*ff*. Typical march layering:
  melody, afterbeat chords, oom-pah bass, percussion.
- Ward / arr. Carmen Dragon, *America the Beautiful*, bb.1–6 and 51–53. Detailed condensed score with
  4 pitched lines plus percussion:
  - Opening: a solo timpani roll *sffp* cresc., then a sweeping run (fls, obs, Bb cls, Eb cl col 8va;
    alto cl, alto & tenor saxes, harp). Low reeds and baritone, then cornets, trumpets, horns and bells
    enter in parallel chords, with basses and trombones on the bass line.
  - Then English horn and alto clarinet take the melody (harp tacet).
  - Climax (bb.51–53): instruments are added one by one (add oboes; add piccolo, flutes, Eb cl; add
    English horn; add bassoons and bass cl; add horns; add baritone, string bass and tuba; add harp;
    add timpani). Brass and reeds play *fff* triplet chords. A 14-note descending run doubles at the
    octave (bassoons, alto clarinet 8va lower). It ends on a *sfz* fermata with tri/cym roll and
    SD/BD *ff*.
  - Demonstrates building a band tutti by successive "add" layers, with "tacet" thinning.

**Transcribing orchestra → band (simulating string techniques)**
- R33 [PREFER] Pick the band instrument that best produces the string effect.
  - A *pp* two-note string tremolo → a woodwind (flute or clarinet, depending on register). In some
    registers saxophones or even horns work better.
- R34 [AVOID] Wind flutter-tongue as the default replacement for a single-note string tremolo (too
  radical for most styles).
  - [PREFER] Use a xylophone or piano tremolo on the note instead.
- R35 [PREFER] Pizzicato (short, dry pulse, little ring) → staccato brass, or woodwind notes doubled
  in unison by xylophone or marimba. Or a single wind/brass instrument plus piano or harp in unison
  for the "ping". The choice depends on register, dynamic and taste.
- R36 [PREFER] For sul tasto, sul ponticello and col legno, imagine the sound and choose the closest
  wind combination. When literal transcription fails, recast the idea creatively instead.
- R37 [AVOID] Expecting any band bass (tuba, trombone, bass clarinet, bassoon, low saxes) to reproduce
  the smooth, powerful cello + double bass combination: it cannot be imitated. Use the band's bass
  instruments on their own terms. Strings are often added to band scores, but Adler prefers the band's
  own low sound.

**Examples (pointers to ch.17 examples, not reprinted here)**
- Milhaud, *Suite Française* (band/orchestra versions, Ex.17-16a/b): the band version's double-bass
  pizzicato appears as a bowed passage in the orchestral version. The *secco* idea is given there to
  two trombones playing eight staccato notes (bb.1, 5), so the strings can focus on a uniform sound.
- Schoenberg, *Theme and Variations* op.43a/b (Ex.17-19, 17-20):
  - The triplet figure, nearly all strings in the orchestra (clarinets in b.24), is spread across many
    instruments in the band version.
  - bb.106–113: flute melody in both versions. bb.125–128: clarinets in both.
  - bb.113–120: violas + 2 muted trumpets (orch.) become muted cornet + euphonium + 2 muted trumpets
    (band), which is more colourful.
  - From b.114, the high double-bass solo is passed around tenor, alto and bari saxes, bassoon, bass
    clarinet and tuba.
- Study list for band / wind ensemble (printed p.783–784), a pointer only: works by Adler, Benson, Brant,
  Copland, Colgrass, Dahl, Giannini, Gould, Hanson, Hindemith, Kurka, McBeth, Mennin, Maslanka, Nelson,
  Persichetti, Piston, Reed, Schwantner, Stamp, Stravinsky (*Symphonies of Wind Instruments*), Ticheli,
  Tower, Tull, Washburn, Wilson. Also Sousa marches and band works by Holst, Vaughan Williams and Grainger.

**Agent applicability**
- For a concert-band template, use the Ex.19-1 track order. Band = doubled sections (one track per
  part, but balance as a section). Wind ensemble = one player per part (solo patches, orchestral-style
  balance).
- In a band arrangement, use cornets, saxes and euphonium as real melodic voices (R24). Mark solo and
  tutti passages by switching between a solo and an ensemble patch (inferred).
- March texture: melody in high reeds/trumpets, afterbeats in horns/alto saxes, bass on beats 1 and 3
  (tuba, trombones, low reeds), cymbal + bass drum with the bass, snare roll-and-flam figures.
- Build a band climax by adding families layer by layer, and end tuttis with the full percussion roll
  on the final chord.
- When converting an orchestral cue to band, map pizz. → staccato brass + mallet/harp doubling, single-
  note tremolo → xylophone/piano tremolo, two-note *pp* tremolo → flute/clarinet trill-tremolo, and
  re-voice the bass for the band rather than imitating cello + bass.
