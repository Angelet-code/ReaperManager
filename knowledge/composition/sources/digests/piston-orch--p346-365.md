## Piano as percussion, end (scan s173L, printed p.346)

**Examples**
- Ex. 368 Prokofiev, Symphony No. 5 (Andante, 3/4, Bb major, f): timpani roll, bass drum roll and a piano tremolo in the low register, the left hand in deep octaves marked 8va bassa. All three roll together on the same repeated rhythm as one dark percussive rumble.

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] Combine a low piano tremolo with timpani and bass drum rolls to make a single percussive mass (Ex. 368). Continues R60 of the previous digest.

**Agent applicability**
- For a dark tremolo swell, write the piano as fast alternating low octaves or chord tremolo (about A0–C3, MIDI 21–48, inferred) aligned with the timpani and bass drum roll lengths.

## Piano and harp (scan s173L–s173R, printed p.346–347)

**Concepts**
- Piano as harp substitute (piano en lugar del arpa): some 20th-c. composers prefer the piano where the harp would be used, perhaps to avoid the harp's romantic association.

**Rules**
- R2 [ALLOWED] [era: 20th c.] Replace the harp by the piano to avoid a romantic colour.
- R3 [ALLOWED] Combine piano and harp, or use a larger group of piano, harps and celesta, all playing harp-like arpeggios, broken chords and figuration (d'Indy, *Summer Day on the Mountain*; Stravinsky, *The Firebird*).
- R4 [ALLOWED] Two harps plus piano in unison make a deep, weighty bass sound. Double with bass pizzicato and timpani (Ex. 369).

**Examples**
- Ex. 369 Britten, *Sinfonia da Requiem* (Andante ben misurato, 6/8, ff unis., then dim.): 2 harps + piano in unison on accented low D's in octaves, short repeated notes [exact octave ?]. In the full score this is also doubled by bass pizz and timpani, over a held octave D in 2 bassoons, contrabassoon, bass trombone, tuba and a bass-drum roll (those parts are omitted from the example).
- Unusual uses (one line each):
  - Debussy, *Printemps*: piano four hands.
  - Stravinsky, *Symphony of Psalms*: two pianos.
  - Stravinsky, *Les Noces*: an orchestra of four pianos and percussion.
  - Copland, *Billy the Kid*: an upright piano, not necessarily in tune, for local colour.

**Agent applicability**
- For a cinematic low "pulse" hit, stack harp + piano on the same low notes (D1/D2, MIDI 26/38 range) and add pizz basses and timpani. Under it, hold a sustained low octave in bassoons, contrabassoon, bass trombone and tuba (inferred from the full-score description).
- For a neutral, non-romantic arpeggio texture (neoclassical, modern), give harp-style figuration to the piano instead of the harp.

## Piano in the small orchestra; placing the piano (scan s173R–s174L, printed p.347–348)

**Rules**
- R5 [ALLOWED] [era: 20th c.] In the small/chamber orchestra the piano covers missing harp and percussion and fills middle parts in place of horns.
- R6 [ALLOWED] Full, sonorous piano writing can give a small orchestra the impression of a tutti (Ex. 370).
- R7 [PREFER] Write for the tone of a 9-foot concert grand when the piano plays with a full symphony orchestra. A smaller piano may be forced by stage size.
- R8 [AVOID] Placing the piano far to the side of the stage when it is combined with woodwinds. *Why:* the distance spoils the blend. [PREFER] The piano belongs in the centre of the stage.

**Examples**
- Ex. 370 Copland, *Music for the Theater* (Allegro molto, 5/8, f/sff): the left hand plays sff low octaves on the downbeat, then chords with pedal. The right hand plays dense accented/staccato chords in the treble. Shows tutti-like weight from piano alone.

**Agent applicability**
- In a chamber-orchestra mockup, let piano chords fill the horn register (about F3–C5, MIDI 53–72, inferred) when there are no horns.
- When the piano doubles woodwinds, pan and place it near the centre, close to the winds, not hard left/right.

## The celesta (celesta) (scan s174L–s175L, printed p.348–350)

**Concepts**
- Celesta: a keyed glockenspiel. Hammers strike steel bars, each over a tuned wooden resonator box, which gives a softness and delicacy the glockenspiel lacks. It has a piano keyboard and a damper pedal.

**Rules**
- R9 [MUST] Range (Fig. 109): written C3 to C7 [top note read as C7, ?]. It sounds an octave higher, C4–C8 (MIDI 60–108). Converted from written.
- R10 [MUST] Write the celesta an octave below the sounding pitch (8va transposing) when producing parts. MIDI is at sounding pitch.
- R11 [AVOID] Expecting long sustain or true staccato. The tones are bell-like but short-lived.
- R12 [AVOID] Exposing the celesta against loud textures. *Why:* it is easily covered. [PREFER] Use it for decorative colour in soft dynamics.
- R13 [PREFER] Double celesta chords with harp, high flutes/piccolo and muted solo violins for a glittering, shimmering chord colour (Ex. 371).
- R14 [ALLOWED] A short, simple celesta figure can mark the final cadence of a movement (Ex. 372).
- R15 [ALLOWED] The celesta takes part in harp-like tracery (fast arpeggios, scales) together with harp and piano (Ex. 373).

**Examples**
- Ex. 371 Strauss, *Der Rosenkavalier* (Ziemlich langsam, F# major, mf dim. → p): the silver-rose motive. Alternating high chord tremolos, then chromatic parallel chords in both hands, 8va. Doubled by harp, 2 flutes + piccolo, and 3 muted solo violins.
- Ex. 372 Shostakovich, Symphony No. 5 (Moderato, quarter = 42, pp, *morendo*): end of a movement. The celesta (p) holds a high note, then plays rising chromatic 16th-note fragments. Around it: horns and 2 trumpets on a soft dotted figure, timpani pp, a solo violin harmonic, muted Vln I/II on repeated 8ths, violas, and cellos/basses with harp on a low chord.
- Ex. 373 Bartók, *Music for Strings, Percussion and Celesta* (quarter = ca 66, pp/ppp): celesta 32nd-note scale and arpeggio sweeps in both hands, combined with harp glissandos and piano glissandos/runs. Strings and timpani are omitted from the example.

**Agent applicability**
- Celesta MIDI notes belong in 60–108. Keep velocity low-to-moderate and the texture thin around it, or it disappears.
- For "magic/sparkle" chords, layer celesta + harp + flute/piccolo + solo violin (con sordino) on the same chord tones.
- For a quiet ending, add a short high celesta figure over muted strings and a held low chord.

## The harpsichord (clavecín, cembalo, Cembalo) (scan s175L–s175R, printed p.350–351)

**Concepts**
- Harpsichord: strings plucked by quills or leather tabs, not struck. 5–8 pedals operate register stops, and a coupler joins its two keyboards. These give dynamic variation (stepped, by registration) and octave doubling above and below the played note.

**Rules**
- R16 [MUST] Range (Fig. 110): written F1 to F6 (MIDI 29–89) [low note read as F1, ?]. It sounds as written.
- R17 [ALLOWED] [era: 20th c. revival] Modern composers combine the harpsichord with the small orchestra.
- R18 [AVOID] Using the harpsichord against a large orchestra unless the instrument is a modern high-power type. *Why:* modern makers build louder, more brilliant instruments for large halls, but they lose some of the intimacy and charm.
- Pointer: modern works with harpsichord are listed (Falla, *El Retablo de Maese Pedro* and *Concerto*; Martin, *Petite Symphonie Concertante*; Poulenc, *Concert Champêtre*; Strauss, *Dance Suite after Couperin*).

**Agent applicability**
- Harpsichord dynamics change by stops/manuals, not by velocity. Change level in blocks (per phrase), not with crescendos (inferred).
- Keep the accompanying orchestra light (strings, a few winds) so the harpsichord is heard.

## The organ (órgano) (scan s175R–s176L, printed p.351–352)

**Rules**
- R19 [MUST] [era: Bach/Handel, Baroque] In oratorios and cantatas the organ realizes the figured bass (continuo).
- R20 [AVOID] [era: Classical–mid-Romantic] The organ in symphonic scores. It dropped out when texture became homophonic. Organ parts return in the late 19th c. (Saint-Saëns, Symphony No. 3).
- R21 [ALLOWED] The organ joins the orchestra for special purposes, not as a regular member:
  - choral-orchestral works, especially religious ones (Honegger, *Le Roi David*);
  - church/cathedral scenes in opera, for realism (Gounod, *Faust*);
  - realistic effects, e.g. a sustained low pedal cluster (Verdi, *Otello*, opening storm: the organ holds the low pedal notes C, C# and D through 53 pages of score);
  - grandiose climaxes aiming at an overpowering mass of sound (Mahler, Symphony No. 8).
- R22 [AVOID] Treating the organ as a dependable orchestral resource. *Why:* not all halls have one, organs differ widely in colour and registration, and the organ is too self-sufficient to blend into the symphony orchestra.

**Concepts**
- Organ stops imitate orchestral instruments. Combining stops resembles orchestration (comparison only).

**Agent applicability**
- For a storm/dread rumble, hold a chromatic low cluster (C, C#, D at the bottom of the pedal range, about C1–D1, MIDI 24–26 with 16-ft stops, inferred) on organ for the whole scene.
- Bring organ in for sacred scenes or the final climax only, then let it go.

**Tags:** #orchestration/organ #orchestration/keyboard #film-scoring/effects #style/sacred

## The harmonium (armonio) (scan s176L, printed p.352)

**Rules**
- R23 [ALLOWED] The harmonium is a small movable reed organ (usually 2 manuals, stops, pedals). It substitutes for an organ when none is available, though its tone is clearly not a large organ's.
- R24 [ALLOWED] [era: early 20th c.] In the small orchestra it compensates for a lack of wind-tone volume (Strauss, *Ariadne auf Naxos*).

**Agent applicability**
- In a small ensemble, a soft harmonium/reed-organ pad can stand in for sustained woodwind chords.

**Tags:** #orchestration/harmonium #orchestration/small-orchestra

## The Ondes Martenot (scan s176L, printed p.352)

**Concepts**
- Ondes Martenot: an electronic instrument (Maurice Martenot, 1928), much used by French composers. It is played from a keyboard or from a ring-on-ribbon controller that gives continuous pitch glides between notes. Its range is roughly the piano keyboard. Filters change the overtone content (formant) almost without limit.

**Rules**
- R25 [ALLOWED] Use the ribbon for portamento/glissando effects between any pitches.
- R26 [ALLOWED] [era: 1955] Piston calls its orchestral use experimental.
- Pointer: works listed (Canteloube, *Vercingétorix*; Honegger, *Jeanne d'Arc au Bûcher*; Jolivet, *Concerto*; Messiaen, *Turangalîla*).

**Agent applicability**
- For an Ondes/theremin-like line, use a monophonic synth lead with pitch-bend glides (wide bend range) and a soft, vibrato-rich tone (inferred).

**Tags:** #orchestration/electronic #orchestration/ondes-martenot

## Part Two, Ch. 19 — Analysis of orchestration: method (scan s177R–s178L, printed p.355–356)

**Concepts**
- Analysis of orchestration (análisis de la orquestación): finding how the orchestra presents the musical thought. It shows how instruments combine for balance, unity/variety of colour, clarity, brilliance and expressiveness, and how style differs between composers and periods.
- Textural element (elemento textural): a component of the texture, such as melody, harmonic background/accompaniment, contrapuntal line or chords. Most music has only a few, and they are easy to tell apart.
- Sub-element (subelemento): a division inside one element.

**Rules**
- R27 [PREFER] Study one instrument's part through a whole score (e.g. Bassoon I through the *Eroica*): range, difficulty, playing time vs. the whole, and its relation to the rest at each moment.
- R28 [MUST] Analysis order, the four steps:
  1. Identify the textural elements, apart from instrumentation.
  2. Note which instruments/sections are assigned to each element.
  3. Compare the elements for balance and contrast.
  4. Examine each element closely: tone colours, doubling, spacing, accent reinforcement, sub-elements.
- R29 [PREFER] Analyse short sections, sometimes only a few bars. *Why:* good orchestration keeps changing its layout and often its texture type.
- R30 [PREFER] Also judge the whole movement: proportion of tutti, unity and variety of textures, in relation to form and content.
- R31 [AVOID] A pedantic, literal analysis. Some choices have no reason, masters have imperfections, and strokes of genius exist.

**Agent applicability**
- Before orchestrating, list the textural elements of each section (melody, accompaniment, bass, counterline, chords). Assign instruments per element, then check balance between elements, then refine colour/doubling/spacing inside each element. Re-plan at every few-bar change.
- Use step 3 as a check: each element must be audible, and the melody must not be covered.

**Tags:** #orchestration/analysis #orchestration/texture #workflow/orchestration

## Ch. 19 — Texture of one element: orchestral unison (unísono orquestal) (scan s178R–s181R, printed p.357–363)

**Concepts**
- Texture of one element (textura de un solo elemento): most often one melodic phrase played by anything from one instrument to the whole orchestra.
- Actual unison (unísono real): every participant on the same pitch, without octaves.
- Octave reduplication (duplicación a la octava): doubling a line in octaves. It adds no new element. It widens the vertical plane of the same line, and the ear accepts the octave almost as a unison because the upper partials agree.

**Rules**
- R32 [ALLOWED] A true unison by all legato instruments in whose range the melody lies comfortably gives a rich mixed timbre (Ex. 374).
- R33 [AVOID] Adding trumpets to a mixed unison when the melody lies low for them. *Why:* it is the poorer part of their range, and the trumpet tone would stick out of the blend (Ex. 374).
- R34 [ALLOWED] Trombones can join a strong unison very softly (ppp against f) to add body without dominating (Ex. 374).
- R35 [ALLOWED] Violins on the G string and cellos on the A string make the string tone dominate a mixed unison. A viola tremolo adds "incandescence". Four horns legato are absorbed into the whole (Ex. 374).
- R36 [PREFER] In a tutti statement of one line, spread the orchestra over several octaves so each instrument plays in its best range (Ex. 375, Fig. 111). This is not an actual unison.
- R37 [PREFER] Weight the octaves. Put most instruments on the middle octaves. A single flute on the top octave only reinforces the upper partial of the lower notes (Fig. 111).
- R38 [MUST] [era: natural brass, Classical] Classical horns and trumpets cannot follow a chromatic or diatonic line. Leave out notes or substitute other (harmonic) notes rather than lose the brass tone-weight (Beethoven, Ex. 375). Horns III/IV in Bb basso could reach the A only as an inferior stopped note, so they leave it out.
- R39 [PREFER] Let individual woodwind parts depart from the melodic pattern where needed:
  - for range (Oboe II cannot go lower);
  - for register (Flute I would enter a weak register going down);
  - for balance (oboe/clarinet doubling layout).
  These departures are expected (Ex. 375).
- R40 [ALLOWED] A loud, forceful unison need not be a tutti. Hold back trumpets and high woodwinds for the consequent phrase (Ex. 376).
- R41 [PREFER] Inside a unison, reinforce accents with short trombone hits. Bass clarinet and bassoons help a fast upward string rush, and a piano glissando can add to it. Rearrange voices (e.g. Vln II jumping to join violas and cellos on a high note) so the arrival note is strong (Ex. 376).
- R42 [PREFER] Treat octave doubling as the same element, not a new voice. Cellos + basses in octaves is the standard case.
- R43 [PREFER] Treat consistent doubling in 3rds or 6ths as harmonic thickening of one line, not a new melody.
- R44 [ALLOWED] A melody in parallel triads (planing) is still a single element. Put the soprano and bass of the triads in octaves to stress the parallelism (Ex. 377).
- R45 [PREFER] Doubling only one voice at the unison fixes the perceived pitch location in that voice's register (Ex. 377). Unequal doubling also places a line in its upper octave (Ex. 380, see Ch. 20).
- R46 [PREFER] Even a very simple one-element texture can be rich if colours are combined and dynamics are staggered per instrument (Ex. 378).
- R47 [ALLOWED] A double-bass harmonic can double a viola note at the unison for a veiled colour (Ex. 378).
- R48 [ALLOWED] Add a stopped, loud, accented horn note (cuivré) and a chalumeau clarinet trill at the peak of a crescendo. Turn the cello pizzicato from p to sfz there too (Ex. 378).

**Examples**
- Ex. 374 d'Indy, *Istar* (Très lent, f sempre):
  - Actual unison (no octaves) in English horn, 2 clarinets, bass clarinet, 3 bassoons, 4 horns, 3 trombones (ppp), Vln I/II on the 4th string, violas in tremolo, cellos.
  - Tacet: piccolo, 2 flutes, 2 oboes, 3 trumpets, tuba, timpani, cymbals, triangle, 2 harps and basses (an opening harp/bass chord with timpani roll is omitted).
  - The melody lies roughly in the G3–E5 zone (inferred from the G-string/A-string remark, ?).
- Ex. 375 Beethoven, Symphony No. 9, I (Allegro non troppo, ff): forceful tutti statement of the Vln I line in D minor over four octaves. Fig. 111 shows the distribution of the A in bar 1 (concert, written octave for basses):

  | Pitch | Instruments | Count |
  |---|---|---|
  | A6 (MIDI 93) | Fl I | 1 |
  | A5 (81) | Vln I & II, Fl II, Ob I, Cl I | 5 |
  | A4 (69) | Va, Ob II, Cl II, Hn I, Tpt I | 5 |
  | A3 (57) | Vc, Bn I & II, Hn II, Tpt II | 5 |
  | A2 written (45) | Basses (sounding A1, 33), timpani | 2 |

  The timpani and natural brass leave out or change notes elsewhere.
- Ex. 376 Stravinsky, *Symphony in Three Movements*, opening (quarter = 160, fff/sff):
  - A low unison/octave G is held, then a fast 32nd-note scale rush up to a high Ab.
  - Strings play it, with bass clarinet, 2 bassoons and contrabassoon on the rush, and a piano glissando.
  - Trombone I–II give an sff accent on the arrival. Tuba/Trb III and timpani mark the downbeat.
  - Horns a 4 continue with accented notes. Strings and piano hammer accented chords on Ab.
- Ex. 377 Debussy (orch. Caplet), *Le Martyre de Saint Sébastien*, opening (Lent, 3/2, pp):
  - The melody moves in parallel minor triads.
  - Fl II + Cl I (soprano) and bass clarinet (bass) are in octaves.
  - English horn, Cl II/III and Bn I/II fill the chord tones.
  - Only the top voice has a unison doubling, so the line reads in the soprano register.
- Ex. 378 Falla, *El Amor Brujo* (Allegro ma non troppo):
  - Violas hold a trilled E (pp ↔ ff swells), doubled at the unison by a double-bass harmonic on the "3a corda" (read as the A string, inferred) [octave of the E ?].
  - Cello pizz on the downbeats, mf → sfz.
  - Horn I gives a soft staccato start (p), later a loud accented stopped note (+, cuivré).
  - Clarinet I adds a chalumeau trill at the crescendo peak. In the last 4 bars it does both colour and dynamics (mf > swells).

**Agent applicability**
- Mixed unison (all winds and strings on one pitch): pick only instruments whose comfortable range contains the whole line. Drop trumpets if the line sits below about D4 (MIDI 62, inferred threshold for "poor low register"). Add trombones at very low CC1/velocity.
- Tutti octave statement: lay the line out in 4–5 octaves. Load the middle octaves (about 5 instruments each), put a single flute on top and basses/timpani at the bottom.
- When a doubling instrument would leave its range or enter a weak register, let it break off or jump an octave rather than follow blindly.
- Planing triads: write the voices in parallel, double soprano and bass at the octave, and double only the top voice at the unison so the line reads high.
- Check: does any doubling instrument (trumpet, oboe low, flute low) stick out of a blend? Move it or drop it.

## Ch. 20 — Melody and accompaniment (melodía y acompañamiento), start (scan s182L–s182R, printed p.364–365)

**Concepts**
- Texture of two elements: in its simplest, most common form, homophonic texture (textura homofónica), i.e. melody + accompaniment.
- Element A / Element B: Piston's labels for melody and accompaniment in analysis.

**Rules**
- R49 [ALLOWED] A single instrument in its middle register (e.g. clarinet) can carry the melody over a simple accompaniment of carefully chosen soft colours (Ex. 379).
- R50 [PREFER] Double a soft harp accompaniment figure with muted low strings, divided so each note gets the most suitable string. In Ex. 379 the violas take the initial D on the C string, accented, and the cellos take the next note with a delicate portamento.
- R51 [PREFER] A soft sustained horn note between the accompaniment and the melody gives horizontal and vertical cohesion (Ex. 379).
- R52 [PREFER] Differentiate melody and accompaniment clearly in tone colour (Ex. 380: woodwinds vs. strings + bassoons + horns).
- R53 [MUST] Mark the accompaniment softer (e.g. pp) than the melody's expressive dynamics. Even so, the accompaniment can cover the melody if it is played without care, so markings alone don't guarantee balance.
- R54 [ALLOWED] Uneven octave doubling in the melody places the line in the upper octave (Ex. 380).
- R55 [PREFER] Two clarinets doubled by oboe and English horn balance each other. Adding a flute on top strengthens the upper line and softens the slight hardness of an oboe + clarinet unison in that register (Ex. 380).
- R56 [PREFER] Build a quiet accompaniment from interlocking parts:
  - gently pulsating divided violins and violas (without mutes);
  - soft low horn and bassoon chords in alternate bars;
  - a bass partly doubled by cellos.
  Interlock the upper strings and the wind chords (Ex. 380).

**Examples**
- Ex. 379 Mahler, Symphony No. 4, IV (Sehr behaglich, pp):
  - Clarinet I in Bb plays the melody with grace notes and accents, in its middle register.
  - Horn I in F holds a sustained written A4, concert D4 (MIDI 62), converted from written.
  - The harp plays a low dotted D–G bounce plus high short notes.
  - Muted violas take an accented D on the C string (D3, MIDI 50), and muted cellos play the figure with portamento.
- Ex. 380 (printed p.366, outside this range; composer not visible here):
  - Element A (melody): Fl I, Ob I, English horn, Cl I & II.
  - Element B (accompaniment): Vln I & II, Va, Vc, Db, Bn I & II, Hn III & IV.
  - The analysis continues on p.366.

**Agent applicability**
- Melody + accompaniment: assign the melody to one colour family and the accompaniment to another. Keep accompaniment CC1/velocity about one dynamic level below the melody (e.g. melody mp–mf, accompaniment pp).
- Add a pp horn pedal in the register between the bass figure and the melody to glue a thin texture.
- For a woodwind melody in octaves, put more instruments on the upper octave (or add a flute on top) so the line reads high and the oboe + clarinet edge is softened.
- Write the string accompaniment as interlocking divisi pulses (offset 8ths between Vln II and Va) rather than block chords on every beat.
