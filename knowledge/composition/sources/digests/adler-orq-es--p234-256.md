## 8 Intro — repertoire and the role of the woodwinds (scan p.234, printed p.229)

**Concepts**
- Wind-band repertoire (madera): few large works for wind section alone compared with string orchestra; notable
  exceptions are Mozart divertimenti/serenades, Dvořák and R. Strauss serenades, Gounod and Milhaud symphonies. Most
  include 2+ horns in the "wind section" (reason given later: the horn's historic double role as woodwind and brass, see "Acompañamiento pedal").
- String section vs. woodwind section: strings are **homogeneous** in sound and play almost continuously; woodwinds
  are **heterogeneous** and normally reserved for specific functions.

**Rules**
- R1 [PREFER] Give the woodwinds one of four standard functions (funciones de la madera), not continuous filler:
  (1) solo passages: whole melodies, melodic fragments or short figures; (2) a harmonic background (fondo armónico)
  under the strings; (3) a contrasting timbre: echoing or repeating a passage the strings just played, or taking part
  of a passage divided between strings and winds; (4) doubling other instruments.
- R2 [PREFER] Keep the strings as the continuous base and bring woodwinds in and out by function (inferred from the
  homogeneous/heterogeneous contrast).

**Examples** (posed as questions the chapter answers by analysis)
- Schubert, Symphony 8 "Unfinished": first theme in oboe + clarinet unison.
- Debussy, *La Mer*, finale: long theme in flute + oboe unison.
- Berlioz, *Symphonie fantastique*: English horn chosen to express despair.
- Stravinsky, *Rite of Spring*: opens with a high bassoon.

**Agent applicability**
- Before writing a woodwind part, label it with one of the four functions; if none applies, leave the winds out.

## Melody assignment — Schubert "Unfinished" case study (scan p.235–239, printed p.230–234)

**Concepts**
- Adler's method: study a long passage, then try the alternatives the composer could have used, to learn why a
  colour was chosen. Rimsky-Korsakov's "orchestrating is creating; it can't be taught" is quoted to be refuted.

**Rules**
- R3 [ALLOWED] Oboe + clarinet in unison on a pp melody in the middle register: it gives a mysterious, slightly
  unstable colour that differs a little in every performance (Schubert). This contradicts the textbook ban.
- R4 [AVOID] (the traditional textbook view, kept for reference) Oboe + clarinet unison doubling, because (a) the
  nasal oboe masks the clarinet, (b) the conductor has to make the oboe play softer to balance, (c) they may have
  trouble staying in tune with each other. Adler treats this as a rule worth knowing and breaking on purpose.
- R5 [PREFER] Flute + oboe unison: the oboe (in its favourable register) stays on top, but the flute neutralises its
  nasality, giving a rounder, richer sound.
- R6 [AVOID] Flute + clarinet unison on a melody around B4–F#5 (MIDI 70–78) if you want both heard: the flute has
  no brilliance in that register, so the clarinet dominates almost alone and the flute adds little body. It does
  remove the oboe's edge.
- R7 [ALLOWED] The bassoon cannot share the treble register of the flute/oboe/clarinet line, but it can double any
  of them an octave below; every such octave pairing is sonorous. Fl + Cl + Bsn spread over three octaves is one of
  Mozart's favourite colours.
- R8 [AVOID] Octave-doubling a theme's first statement when the orchestration later depends on **register
  expansion** (expansión de registros): the octave spread would use up the effect Schubert keeps for mm.20–21 and the
  cadential climax mm.28–29. Save the wide spacing for the build-up and climax.
- R9 [PREFER] Add an instrument "under cover": Schubert's flute slips in doubling the oboe at the crescendo (m.26)
  and is only really heard when it takes the top notes of the cadential chords (mm.28–29).
- R10 [PREFER] Orchestrate each main thematic element with a different colour/texture, so each idea keeps its own
  character and the form is clearer (Schubert gives theme 1 to ob+cl over string murmur, theme 2 to cellos then
  violins over syncopated wind/viola accompaniment).

**Examples**
- Ex. 8-1, Schubert Sym. 8/I mm.12–36 (B minor, 3/4, Allegro moderato): ob 1 + A cl 1 in unison, pp; theme
  F#5 (dotted half) – B4, A#4–B4–C#5 – F#5 … D5–E5–F5–E5–D5–C#5 (concert, read from Ex. 8-2). Vln 1/2 murmur
  in 16ths, vla/vc/cb repeated quarter + two-eighth bass figure. Mm.20–21 bassoon and D horn enter fz>p on sustained
  notes. Fl enters ~m.26; mm.28–29 tutti fz chords with trumpets, trombones and timpani; at m.32–36 the winds
  sustain and crescendo to ff with the whole brass.
- Ex. 8-2: same theme fl + ob unison (R5). Ex. 8-3: fl + cl unison (R6). Ex. 8-4: ob + bsn an octave apart.
  Ex. 8-5: fl + cl + bsn across three octaves (R7).
- Footnote, Mozart octave-pair wind colours: Piano Concerto in C K.503, I mm.76–82 and III mm.83–89; *Le nozze di
  Figaro* Act I duet (sc.1) mm.67–73; "Venite inginocchiatevi" (cl + bsn); "Dove sono" (ob + bsn); chorus
  "Ricevete, o padroncina" (fl + bsn).

**Agent applicability**
- For a soft lyrical theme in the treble: ob+cl unison = mysterious; fl+ob unison = round and rich, with the oboe
  leading; avoid fl+cl unison below ~F#5 unless you want plain clarinet colour.
- Plan the registral span of a section: start narrow (unison), widen by octaves only at the build/climax.

## Woodwinds as accompaniment — Schubert "Unfinished" second theme (scan p.239–243, printed p.234–238)

**Rules**
- R11 [PREFER] Make an off-beat (syncopated) accompaniment (acompañamiento a contratiempo) from wind dyads that
  pass between partners: clarinets first pair with violas, then with bassoons. The figure is eighth rest + slurred
  repeated eighths, pp.
- R12 [PREFER] Under such a pattern, put the bass on the downbeat only (cb pizz, pp) and the melody in one string
  section (cellos, then violins), so melody, off-beat filler and bass each occupy their own rhythmic slot.
- R13 [ALLOWED] Sustained bassoon + horn notes with fz/fp > pp make the bridge from a tutti to a quiet new theme.
- R14 [ALLOWED] Use a sudden ff tutti chord with all winds a2 plus trombones after a pause to break off a lyrical
  section (Schubert m.63).

**Examples**
- Ex. 8-6, Schubert Sym. 8/I mm.36–80: mm.36–38 tutti with fz; bsn + D hn sustain fp → pp; from m.42 2 A cl + vla
  in off-beat dyads, pp, vc state the G-major second theme pp, cb pizz on downbeats. Mm.52–59: 2 cl + 2 bsn take the
  off-beat dyads, D horns add their own off-beat pattern pp, vln 1 + vln 2 take the theme in parallel (thirds/sixths)
  pp, cellos play an accented off-beat answer. Mm.60–62 decresc. into a bar of silence; m.63 ff a2 tutti with
  trombones; mm.68–71 repeated fz wind + brass chords crescendo to ff; mm.72–80 woodwinds hold long chords (p, then
  f) while strings trade the motive.

**Agent applicability**
- For a quiet second theme: melody in cellos (or violins), off-beat repeated eighth dyads in clarinets doubled by
  violas (or bassoons), pizz basses on beat 1 only, all pp.

## La variedad en los tratamientos orquestales — melodic treatment (scan p.243–248, printed p.238–243)

### Duplicación al unísono (unison doubling)

**Concepts**
- Whether two identical winds in unison sound louder than one is disputed. Adler compares unison doubling to the
  string mute or a brass mute: it changes the basic colour as well as the level.

**Rules**
- R15 [AVOID] Doubling a normal orchestral **solo** passage with a second instrument of the same kind (2 fl, 2 ob,
  2 cl, 2 bsn in unison). *Why:* two winds are never perfectly in tune, so the doubling upsets the balance, thickens
  the sound and in some registers muddies it, changes the solo timbre, and stops the player shaping the phrase
  (rubato and other expressive nuance).
- R16 [ALLOWED] Unison doubling (a2) in tutti, where sheer volume matters and the characteristic solo timbre doesn't.

**Examples**
- Ex. 8-7, Rossini, *Semiramide* overture mm.178–181: the same theme written for fl, ob, A cl and bsn, each first
  solo, then a2, to compare the sounds. Grace-note figure, then a dotted-half note and triplet-eighth staccato
  figures.

### Duplicación a la octava (octave doubling)

**Concepts**
- Organ-registration analogy: an organist adds 4' (octave) and 2' (double octave) stops to the 8' fundamental for a
  transparent but powerful sound. Adding more 8' stops only clouds it.

**Rules**
- R17 [PREFER] To reinforce a woodwind line, double it at the octave (8' + 4') rather than in unison; this is often
  more effective (cross-ref. ch.7).
- R18 [PREFER] Pass a long melody through successive **octave pairs** of different colours so it keeps renewing
  itself (Rossini): cl + bsn → cl + hn → fl + ob → picc + ob. Keep the accompaniment unchanged under it.

**Examples**
- Ex. 8-8: Semiramide theme as A cl solo + bsn solo an octave apart (bsn in tenor clef), p; and fl 1 + ob 1 an
  octave apart, p.
- Ex. 8-9, Rossini, *Semiramide* overture mm.177–192 (original): A cl 1 + bsn 1 solo in octaves, p, over strings in
  plain staccato quarter-note chords (vln/vla double stops, vc + cb unison quarters). M.181 the bassoon drops out and
  D horn 1 solo joins the clarinet. M.185 clarinet alone, with horns 3–4 in A adding a soft chord. M.187 fl 1 + ob 1
  solo in octaves; m.189 the piccolo takes over the top of the octave pair with ob 1 (p) and the flute drops out.

**Agent applicability**
- Light Classical/Rossini allegro: a tune in two winds an octave apart, p, over string quarter-note chords; change
  the pair every 2–4 bars.
- Never write `a2` unison for a solo line unless the passage is tutti.

### Elección de un color para caracterizar o clarificar una figura (colour as character and form marker)

**Concepts**
- In symphonic poems especially, one instrument (or small group) stands for a person, object, *idée fixe* or
  leitmotif.
- 19th-c. view: the clarinet was "the nightingale of the orchestra" [era: 19th-c. Romantic].

**Rules**
- R19 [PREFER] Tie a recurring motive to one fixed colour and keep that colour for every return; this makes the form
  audible. Debussy keeps the same instruments for every repeat of a figure even in a very colourful movement.
- R20 [PREFER] Keep a signature colour **out** of the contrasting middle section, so its return marks the reprise
  (Debussy's English horn in "Nuages" yields a clear A–B–A).

**Examples**
- Berlioz, *Symphonie fantastique*: clarinet (idée fixe). Weber, *Der Freischütz* overture: two clarinets. R.
  Strauss, *Till Eulenspiegel*: horn as character. Stravinsky, *Rite of Spring*: high solo bassoon opening.
- Ex. 8-10, Debussy, *Nocturnes* "Nuages" mm.21–28 (Modéré): parallel chords in clarinets + bassoons; the only
  contrasting gesture is a solo English horn line (rising triplet pick-up, then slow descending notes, < > swells).
  It defines the first and last sections and returns with the opening chords in the original key 23 bars before the
  end. It is absent from the middle section.
- Ex. 8-11, Debussy, *Nocturnes* "Fêtes" mm.27–29 (Un peu plus animé, 15/8): a p staccato figure in parallel
  chords for EH + A cl + bsn 1–2 + bsn 3, accented on the group starts; the same combination returns 4 bars later
  and many times in the recap of the first part (m.208 ff).

**Agent applicability**
- Assign each leitmotif a fixed instrument or wind group in the arrangement spec; reuse it on every recurrence and
  withhold it from contrasting sections.

## Tratamiento armónico (scan p.248–256, printed p.243–251)

### Acompañamiento pedal (pedal accompaniment)

**Rules**
- R21 [PREFER] In Classical and Romantic tutti, reinforce the harmony with one or more wind pedals (woodwinds with
  horns) for a strong, continuous, solid base [era: Classical–Romantic].
- R22 [PREFER] Early-Classical scoring: give the horns the **same** tonic/dominant pedal notes as the oboes (2 ob +
  2 hn on the tonic, in octaves) under string figuration [era: early Classical symphony, c.1760–1780].
- R23 [PREFER] Let a pedal **break into octave leaps** at the cadence (horns jumping an octave) to sharpen the
  dissonance against the moving parts and strengthen the cadence.
- R24 [AVOID] Removing the held wind pedal from such a tutti: the same string writing loses stability and splendour
  (Adler's A/B comparison).

**Concepts**
- Score order: the horns doubled the woodwinds' pedals and lines, so they were placed in the score **below the
  woodwinds and above the trumpets**, which is still the standard order. From the Classical orchestra on, the horn
  was counted partly as woodwind and partly as brass, and it belongs to both the wind quintet and the brass quintet.

**Examples**
- Ex. 8-12, Mozart, Symphony 29 K.201/I mm.1–5 (A major, Allegro moderato): strings only, p (oboes and horns
  tacet); octave-leap theme in vln 1 over sustained half/whole notes in the lower strings.
- Ex. 8-13, same, mm.13–23 (first tutti, f): 2 ob + 2 hn hold an A pedal in octaves (tied whole notes) for six
  bars, while the strings play the theme in canon between violins and lower strings. In the cadence bars the oboes
  move in long notes and the horns leap an octave.
- Ex. 8-14: the same bars without the pedal, for comparison (R24).

**Agent applicability**
- Classical-style tutti: tie whole-note tonic (later dominant) pedals in 2 oboes + 2 horns, in octaves, for 4–6
  bars under busy strings; at the cadence give the horns octave leaps.

### Acompañamiento tenido (sustained accompaniment)

**Rules**
- R25 [PREFER] Hold large-scale sustained harmonies in the woodwinds under active strings; this is a common
  Classical and Romantic texture.
- R26 [PREFER] Write the winds' sustained line as a **slow counterpoint** (long notes in flute and oboe, high and
  far above the strings) against a fast string theme. *Why:* the wide registral gap gives the counterpoint weight
  without pulling attention from the theme.
- R27 [ALLOWED] Let the bassoon double the cello theme instead of joining the wind counterpoint. As the only
  non-string instrument on the theme it adds a new colour that is audible even very soft. Mozart also had practical
  reasons: theatre orchestras had tiny cello/bass sections, so bassoons often reinforced the bass [era: 18th-c.
  theatre orchestra]. The effect is still valid today.
- R28 [PREFER] Use sustained woodwind chords (fl, ob, cl) to prepare and reinforce a tutti cadence, then prolong the
  cadential harmony with agitated repeated notes in the violins on the dominant, while winds, brass and lower strings
  punch the chords [era: Classical opera overture].
- R29 [ALLOWED] Tied wind harmony under a light, energetic tune does not weigh it down: it focuses attention on the
  melody and its accompaniment and keeps the passage harmonically unified (J. Strauss).
- R30 [PREFER] Break a long wind pedal at the seam between two statements of a tune: (a) the next statement's
  pick-up in vln 1 is heard clearly, (b) the wind players get a breath. Dropping such breaks later raises tension.

**Examples**
- Ex. 8-15, Mozart, *Le nozze di Figaro* overture mm.19–42 (D major, alla breve): fl 1 holds a high D6 and ob 1
  long notes (whole/half notes, slurred) above strings running pp eighths in unison/octaves; bsn doubles vc/cb. Mm.
  23–28: soft answering chords in ob, cl and D horns. M.29: ff tutti with D trumpets and timpani, winds in repeated
  chords; m.35 ff: fp held chords in fl/ob/cl and horns.
- Ex. 8-16, same, mm.43–58: cadential half-note chords in the winds; then violins repeat the dominant A5 in eighths
  while winds, brass, timpani, violas and basses hit the chords on the beat.
- Ex. 8-17, J. Strauss II, *Die Fledermaus* overture mm.76–101 (2/4 Allegro, D major): bassoons hold a tied dyad p,
  A clarinets a tied dyad pp, flutes add staccato off-beat chord dabs p. Vln 1 has the melody; vln 2 and violas
  play off-beat eighth chords; vc/cb play the downbeat. The pedal stops at m.83 and resumes (bsn, then cl) for the
  second statement; from m.99 on there is no break, to build tension.

**Agent applicability**
- Classical overture allegro: strings play the running theme in octaves; fl/ob hold long high notes as a slow
  counterpoint 1.5–2 octaves above; the bassoon doubles the cello line.
- Waltz/polka-style accompaniment: tied bsn/cl dyads (p–pp) plus off-beat string chords plus a downbeat bass; leave
  a 1–2 bar gap in the wind pedal between phrases.
- At a Classical cadence, after the held wind chords, write repeated eighths on the dominant in the violins and
  tutti chord hits on the beats.
