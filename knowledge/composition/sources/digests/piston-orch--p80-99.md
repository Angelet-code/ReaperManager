## Ch.4 The Violoncello — instrument and strings (printed p.80)

**Concepts**
- 'cello (violonchelo; Fr. violoncelle, It. violoncello, Ger. Violoncell): pitched an octave below the viola.
  Its body is small for its pitch, and the much deeper ribs (top to back) make up for it. Sounding string
  length is about 27 in. The player holds it between the knees and it rests on an adjustable endpin (pica).
- Strings: A and D usually plain gut, G and C wire-wound. Metal is increasingly used for all four
  [era: 1950s; today steel/synthetic strings are the norm].

**Agent applicability**
- Cello pitches are concert pitch. Lowest note is C2 (36), the open C string.

## Ch.4 — Fingering, first position (digitación) (printed p.80–81, Fig. 29)

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] In one position, the span from 1st to 4th finger is a minor third (3 semitones) and the
  fingers fall by semitones. (On the violin the same span is a perfect fourth.)
- R2 [ALLOWED] Extension (extensión): a whole-tone stretch is possible between the 1st and 2nd fingers, so
  the hand covers a major third (4 semitones) without shifting.
- R3 [ALLOWED] Patterns on the other strings are the C-string patterns transposed up by fifths.

**Examples**
- Fig. 29, C string, first position:
  - (a) C2 D2 E2 F2, fingers 0-1-3-4.
  - (b) C2 D2 Eb2 F2, fingers 0-1-2-4.
  - (c) all half steps: C#2 D2 D#2 E2 with fingers 1-2-3-4.
  - (d) extension: D2 E2 F2 F#2, fingers 1-2-3-4 [? exact notes].

**Agent applicability**
- In a fast cello passage, groups of four stopped notes on one string should span at most a major third (inferred).

## Ch.4 — Positions (posiciones) (printed p.81–82, Fig. 30–32)

**Concepts**
- Positions are numbered diatonically, as on the violin, even though the cello hand moves almost by
  semitones. On the C string with pattern 1-3-4:
  - 1st position: D2–F2.
  - 2nd: E2–G2.
  - 3rd: F2–Ab2.
  - 4th: G2–Bb2.
  - 5th: A2–C3.
  - 6th: B2–D3.
  - 7th: C3–Eb3, an octave above the open string.
- Chromatically displaced hand placements keep the same position name. Some cellists use more precise terms:
  - "raised first" and "lowered second" positions.
  - "intermediate" and "one-and-a-half" positions, applied enharmonically.
- Half position (media posición) is used as on the violin. On the C string it covers C#2–E2.

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] Expect more shifts (cambios de posición) than on violin or viola. A minor third per hand does
  not fill the perfect fifth between strings.
- R2 [PREFER] Keys that fit first position with open strings are easiest. C major from C2 to C4 (36–60)
  needs no shift. E major from E2 to E4 needs about five shifts (Fig. 32).
- R3 [PREFER] Shifts are normally almost inaudible. Portamento (portamento) is used on purpose when it suits.
- R4 [AVOID] Unintended portamento on long shifts. Long distances on one string exaggerate it, and a full
  section of about ten cellos makes it more marked.

**Examples**
- Ex. 94, Tchaikovsky, Symphony no. 6 (5/4 Allegro con grazia): a lyrical cello section theme rising into
  tenor clef, with expressive slides and a marked gliss.; shows section portamento.

**Agent applicability**
- For a cello line in sharp keys, expect less resonance and more shifting than in C, G, D or F. Open strings
  ring in C, G, D and A.
- When modelling the slides of a cello section (a 1950s section is about 10 cellos), use them sparingly and
  only on expressive leaps.

## Ch.4 — Thumb position (posición de pulgar, capotasto) (printed p.83–84, Fig. 34)

**Concepts**
- At 7th position (an octave above the open string) the hand meets the body of the instrument. The thumb
  leaves the neck and lies across the string, where it acts as a movable nut.
- In thumb position the 3rd finger reaches further than the 4th, so the 4th finger is rarely used.

**Rules**
- R1 [NEVER] Thumb notes cannot appear suddenly inside a continuous passage in normal position. The player
  needs time to place the thumb.
- R2 [ALLOWED] Thumb position is sometimes used low on the string, when the longer thumb-to-3rd-finger
  stretch is needed.
- R3 [ALLOWED] The span from thumb to 3rd finger is about a quarter of the string length. That is a
  diminished fifth (6 semitones) in half position and an octave (12) in 7th position. Wide extended patterns
  are possible in high positions.

**Examples**
- Fig. 34 shows thumb patterns:
  - alternating thumb and 3rd finger in octaves and fifths;
  - scale fragments with the thumb held;
  - octave pairs in tenor clef;
  - an arpeggio rising from about Bb2 [?] to about F4 [?] and back.

**Agent applicability**
- Above about A4 (69), cello writing is in thumb position. Reach it by an approach or a rest, not by a sudden
  jump inside a running figure (inferred).

**Tags:** #orchestration/strings/cello #technique/thumb-position

## Ch.4 — Clefs (claves) (printed p.84)

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] The normal clef is bass clef. Tenor clef (middle C on the 4th line) is for high passages, to
  avoid ledger lines. Treble clef is for passages too high to read easily in tenor clef.
- R2 [MUST] [era: 19th-c. editions] In older editions, treble-clef cello parts were often written an octave
  above sounding pitch, except where the treble clef followed a tenor clef. The practice is now abandoned.

**Agent applicability**
- When transcribing cello parts from 19th-century editions into MIDI, check whether a treble-clef passage
  must be lowered an octave. When producing notation, write treble clef at pitch.

**Tags:** #orchestration/strings/cello #notation/clefs #era/19th-century

## Ch.4 — Range (registro, ámbito) (printed p.84–85)

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] Practical top for orchestral cello parts is two octaves above the open A, i.e. A5 (81),
  without harmonics.
- R2 [ALLOWED] Solo cello parts go a fourth or fifth higher: D6–E6 (86–88).
- R3 [AVOID] Taking the whole cello section above about A5. Piston calls it exceptional.

**Examples**
- Ex. 95, Britten, Four Sea Interludes from Peter Grimes (Storm, Presto con fuoco): the whole cello section
  plays ff marcato in high treble clef, with repeated accented and slurred figures. It is an exceptional case
  of section writing at the top of the range [exact peak ?].

**Agent applicability**
- Cello section range for MIDI generation: C2–A5 (36–81). Solo cello: C2–E6 (36–88).

## Ch.4 — Harmonics (armónicos) (printed p.85–87)

**Concepts**
- Natural harmonic (armónico natural): sounds a partial of an open string. The longer cello string makes
  upper partials speak more securely than on violin or viola.
- Artificial harmonic (armónico artificial): the thumb stops the fundamental and the 3rd finger lightly touches
  the node a perfect fourth above. It sounds two octaves above the stopped note (+24).

**Rules**
- R1 [AVOID] Natural harmonics above the 8th partial (three octaves above the open string) are seldom
  written. The player may take even the 8th as an artificial harmonic, with the fundamental two octaves below
  the sounding pitch.
- R2 [AVOID] The 7th partial: it is too flat.
- R3 [ALLOWED] The node a minor third above the nut gives the 6th partial (two octaves and a fifth above the
  open string, +31). It is usable but rarely notated.
- R4 [ALLOWED] Artificial harmonics with the 4th node work over the whole range, from stopped Db2 (37) on the
  C string upward. The lowest one sounds Db4 (61).
- R5 [PREFER] Use the 4th-node artificial harmonic. The 5th-node one (sounds an octave plus a fifth above
  the stopped note, +19) is possible but rarer, harder to produce and of poorer quality.
- R6 [ALLOWED] As a color effect, a legato glissando through the natural harmonics of one string can reach
  the 12th partial. On the D string that is A6 (93) if the 12th partial is meant (inferred). Not every
  player has to sound every note.

**Examples**
- Ex. 96, Stravinsky, L'Oiseau de feu: cellos con sord., glissando of natural harmonics sul D, up and back
  down; the upper limit of natural harmonics.
- Ex. 97, Mahler, Symphony no. 1 (opening, Langsam): cellos div. in 3 hold flageolet A harmonics sempre ppp;
  shows harmonics as a sustained, shimmering pedal.
- Ex. 98, Ravel, Piano Concerto: a single cello harmonic at p. Ravel notates the sounding pitch an octave too
  low. The real sound is E6 (88), the 6th partial of the open A.
- Ex. 99, Casella, Pupazzetti (Andantino): 2 solo cellos con sord. play artificial-harmonic dyads ppp over
  the other cellos con sord. pizz. (bass plus chord); ends morendo.
- Ex. 100, Ravel, Rapsodie espagnole (Très modéré): cellos and basses each div. in 4 play a pp harmonic chord.
  It sounds as a close chord in the treble (cellos) and the tenor/bass register (basses).

**Agent applicability**
- For harmonic pads in a sample library, use the "harmonics" articulation at pp–ppp. Keep pitches to
  partials 2–6 of open strings, or 4th-node artificial harmonics (stopped note + 24).

## Ch.4 — Bowing (arco) (printed p.88)

**Concepts**
- The cello bow (arco) is shorter (28¼ in), thicker and less springy than violin or viola bows. All violin
  bowings are available and effective.
- Playing position puts the lowest string nearest the bow arm. Up-bow and down-bow keep their names.

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] These are common on cello:
  - sul tasto (sur la touche);
  - sul ponticello;
  - col legno;
  - every kind of tremolo.
- R2 [PREFER] Bowed tremolo sur la touche on the two lowest strings (C, G; about 36–55) gives a mysterious
  color unlike any other tremolo.
- R3 [ALLOWED] Fingered tremolo (trémolo digitado) wider than a major third needs thumb position. The possible
  stretch depends on how high on the string it is.

**Examples**
- Ex. 101, Debussy, La Mer (Animé): low cello bowed tremolo sur la touche, mf e dim. molto to ppp; a dark,
  mysterious tremolo bed.
- Ex. 102, Schmitt, Antoine et Cléopâtre (Animé): cellos div. in 4 combine fingered tremolos and trills in
  tenor and bass clef, p cresc. to ff; dense tremolo and trill texture.

**Tags:** #orchestration/strings/cello #technique/bowing #technique/tremolo

## Ch.4 — Double-stops and chords (dobles cuerdas, acordes) (printed p.89–90)

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] In the lower positions, without thumb, every double-stop interval within the octave is
  playable except seconds.
- R2 [ALLOWED] Major second (1st–4th finger) only from 3rd position up. Minor second only from 4th position up.
- R3 [AVOID] Using the thumb for double-stops inside a running passage: it needs a break. Thumb-stopped notes
  are also less expressive.
- R4 [PREFER] Chords with one or more open strings: they are the most sonorous.
- R5 [ALLOWED] Thumb or any finger can stop a perfect fifth across two adjacent strings.
- R6 [PREFER] Three-note chords. Four-note chords are usually too heavy, though sometimes right.
- R7 [PREFER] Arpeggios across the strings should fall under the fingers like a four-note chord, one note per
  string with no shifts.
- R8 [MUST] Chords are bowed low note first unless marked. An arrow before the chord reverses the order, to
  stress the bass. Check chord fingerings with string diagrams, as for violin.

**Examples**
- Ex. 103, Strauss, Symphonia domestica (sehr lebhaft): ff sixteenth arpeggios crossing all four strings in
  D major, each group laid out as a chord shape.
- Ex. 104, Bartók, Rhapsody no. 2 for violin and orchestra (Moderato): cellos non div. play f three- and
  four-note chords, including chromatic chords.

**Agent applicability**
- For a cello double-stop (one player), check:
  - each note sits on a different adjacent string;
  - intervals of a third to an octave in low positions;
  - no seconds below 3rd/4th position.
- Otherwise write divisi.
- Cello arpeggio figures: one note per string, at most about a fifth between adjacent strings, using open
  C/G/D/A when the harmony allows.

**Tags:** #orchestration/strings/cello #technique/double-stops #technique/arpeggio

## Ch.4 — Pizzicato (printed p.90–92)

**Rules**
- R1 [PREFER] Cello pizzicato is especially sonorous and vibrant. It is an everyday way to give the harmonic
  bass body and transparency.
- R2 [ALLOWED] Pizzicato arpeggio accompaniment figures are common.
- R3 [PREFER] Pizzicato tone stays good up to a little over an octave above the open A, about A4–B4
  (69–71). Above that it turns wooden, which suits special effects only.
- R4 [ALLOWED] Plucked harmonics sound harp-like. Limit them to the octave and octave-plus-fifth natural
  harmonics (partials 2 and 3, i.e. +12 and +19 above an open string).
- R5 [AVOID] Plucked harmonics on stopped notes, where the right hand touches the node and plucks at once.
  Not all cellists can do it.
- R6 [MUST] Pizzicato chords are rolled upward with the thumb by default. Rolling downward with the fingers
  needs an arrow.
- R7 [ALLOWED] Chord effects range from softly rolled four-note chords to short, abrupt chords on the three
  upper strings.

**Examples**
- Ex. 105, Brahms, Symphony no. 2 (Allegretto grazioso): p pizzicato eighth-note arpeggio accompaniment in G
  over about two octaves.
- Ex. 106, Shostakovich, Symphony no. 5 (Allegretto): high pizzicato cello melody with accents, p–f; an
  effective use of the high pizzicato register.
- Ex. 107, Mussorgsky, A Night on Bald Mountain (ending): cellos play a p pizzicato harmonic sul D. It doubles
  a bell in D, flutes a2, clarinets and a bassoon pp; a bell-like plucked harmonic.
- Ex. 108, Debussy, Ibéria (Mouvt. de marche): cellos div. play f–ff three- and four-note pizzicato chords
  that imitate a guitar, with the whole string section joining.

**Agent applicability**
- For cello pizzicato bass lines and arpeggios, keep notes at or below about B4 (71) unless a dry, wooden
  effect is wanted.
- Pizzicato chords: humanize as an upward roll, low note first, a few ms per note (inferred).

**Tags:** #orchestration/strings/cello #technique/pizzicato #texture/accompaniment

## Ch.4 — Melodic uses and string colors (printed p.92–93)

**Concepts**
- A string (about A3–A5, 57–81): expressive warmth, intensity and dynamic power. It is the classic cello
  melody register.
- D string (about D3–D4+): smooth and unobtrusive, less nasal than the A.
- G and C strings: little difference in timbre between them, both full and rich in overtones. The C is
  slightly more ponderous in forte.

**Rules**
- R1 [PREFER] Give cello section melodies to the A-string register for intensity and power.
- R2 [MUST] Accompany soft melodies on the two lowest strings (about C2–D3) very lightly, or they are covered.

**Examples**
- Ex. 109, Charpentier, Impressions d'Italie (Assez vite): unaccompanied cellos, fff, play a long melody in
  treble clef on the A string. The whole solo spans 66 bars, with élargissez, glissez and rubato marks.
- Ex. 110, Wagner, Siegfried Idyll (Ruhig bewegt): p dolce cello melody in E major on the smooth middle (D)
  string register.
- Ex. 111, Franck, Variations symphoniques (Lento sostenuto): p dolce melody low on the C/G strings. Its only
  accompaniment is a pp piano figuration, which shows the light scoring a low melody needs.

**Agent applicability**
- If the cello melody sits below about D3 (50), keep accompanying parts at pp and thin. Avoid sustained
  low-mid pads in the same register (inferred).

## Ch.4 — Cellos on the bass line (printed p.93)

**Rules**
- R1 [PREFER] The cellos regularly play the bass, alone or with the double-basses.
- R2 [PREFER] Common practice is the same written notes for cellos and basses. The basses sound an octave
  lower, so the bass comes out in octaves, with the cellos reinforcing the basses' octave overtone.
- R3 [ALLOWED] Occasionally write the cellos an octave lower, in unison with the sounding basses, for a more
  concentrated sonority.

**Agent applicability**
- Default orchestral bass: Vc at pitch P and Cb sounding P−12. For a dark, concentrated bass: Vc and Cb
  both at the bass's sounding pitch.

**Tags:** #orchestration/strings/cello #orchestration/doubling #orchestration/bass-line

## Ch.4 — Accompaniment figures and passage work (printed p.93–95)

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] Cello parts carry much accompanying or animating passage work. It can cover a wide range, and
  elaborate figuration needs virtuosity.
- R2 [AVOID] Rapid low-register passages with staccato or détaché bowing are hard to make clear because of
  the inertia of the low strings. They need good players to avoid scratchiness.

**Examples**
- Ex. 112, Beethoven, Symphony no. 8 (Tempo di Menuetto, trio): p staccato triplet broken-chord figures in F
  that cross registers, under a melody.
- Ex. 113, Ravel, Rapsodie espagnole (Assez animé, 6/8): f to ff. Triple-stop chords on the beats alternate
  with slurred sixteenth figures; a rhythmic chord-and-figure accompaniment.
- Ex. 114, Dukas, La Péri (Modérément animé): ff sweeping sixteenth arpeggios and scales over about three
  octaves, into tenor clef; virtuoso animating figuration.
- Ex. 115, Stravinsky, Le Sacre du printemps (Prestissimo): p rapid sixteenths in the low register, with
  pedal C2/D2 [?] alternating with chromatic upper notes, plus hairpins; the hard low fast passage work.

**Agent applicability**
- Fast staccato cello figures below about G2 (43) will sound muddy in samples and live. Raise the octave,
  slow them down or thin the doubling (inferred).

**Tags:** #orchestration/strings/cello #texture/accompaniment #technique/passage-work

## Ch.4 — Divided cellos (violonchelos divisi) (printed p.95–96)

**Rules**
- R1 [PREFER] Dividing the cellos is common: half play the melody, usually on the A string, and half play
  the bass or a subordinate part. Either half can be divided further.
- R2 [ALLOWED] Division can go as far as one part per player (Strauss).

**Examples**
- Ex. 116, Mahler, Symphony no. 4 (3rd mvt, Ruhevoll):
  - cellos "div. a due parti eguali": one half plays a pp espress. molto cantabile melody going into tenor
    clef, the other half holds sustained notes;
  - violas div. sustain;
  - basses pizz. mark the beat.
- Ex. 117, Strauss, Also sprach Zarathustra (Mässig langsam): cellos in 8 parts, two per desk at four desks,
  mf espr. cresc. The parts are:
  - desk 1: melody with figuration;
  - desk 2: chordal sixteenth figures;
  - desks 3–4: sustained harmony.

**Agent applicability**
- To get a cello melody plus bass from one section in MIDI, use two cello tracks (or div. patches): melody
  roughly A3–A5, bass below. Balance them as two half sections, lowering CC/velocity by about 3 dB each
  versus tutti (inferred).

**Tags:** #orchestration/strings/cello #orchestration/divisi #texture/layering

## Ch.4 — Solo cello (violonchelo solo) (printed p.97)

**Rules**
- R1 [PREFER] The most characteristic use of the solo cello is one player given an outstanding melodic role.
- R2 [ALLOWED] Several solo cellos, apart from the section, are possible:
  - Rossini, William Tell Overture: the opening is for 5 solo cellos plus the rest of the section;
  - Tchaikovsky, 1812 Overture: 4 solo cellos with 2 solo violas.

**Examples**
- Ex. 118, Brahms, Piano Concerto no. 2 (3rd mvt, Andante): long mp espress. solo cello melody in Bb in tenor
  clef, about F3–Bb4 [?]; the solo cello as melodic protagonist.

## Ch.5 The Double-Bass — instrument (printed p.98)

**Concepts**
- Double-bass (contrabajo; Fr. contrebasse, It. contrabbasso, Ger. Kontrabass). Also called bass viol,
  contrabass, string bass or bass, the last being most common.
- Sloping shoulders show its viol ancestry, and it has geared (cog-wheel) tuning machines. It stands on an
  endpin, supported by the player's body and left knee. Players sometimes sit on a high stool.
- The three-quarter size is preferred over full size for orchestral use:
  - sounding string length about 42¾ in;
  - bow 26–27 in.
- Strings: G and D gut, A and E wire-wound gut. Metal sets are being adopted. They respond more readily to
  the bow and are more durable [era: 1950s].

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] The double-bass sounds an octave below written. All Piston's bass examples are written pitch
  (sound −12) unless marked.

**Agent applicability**
- MIDI is concert pitch, so enter bass notes at sounding pitch (written −12). Standard open strings are
  E1 (28), A1 (33), D2 (38), G2 (43). These are standard tuning, not restated on these pages.

**Tags:** #orchestration/strings/double-bass #orchestration/transposition

## Ch.5 — Low range below E and the three-string bass (printed p.99)

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] Almost all composers write notes below the open E, down to written C2, sounding C1 (24).
- R2 [era: Beethoven's time, three-string bass] The three-string bass was then common. Its lowest string was
  tuned to A or G above the modern low E, about A1 (33) or G1 (31), yet composers still wrote below it.
- R3 [AVOID] Writing below the instrument's real range. A player without the extension plays the notes an
  octave higher. Doubling in the cellos covers this, but it can seriously distort the shape of a phrase.
- R4 [ALLOWED] There are three ways to go below E1:
  - scordatura (scordatura) of the E string;
  - a five-string bass;
  - a mechanical extension of the E string down to C1 (24).
- R5 [AVOID] Scordatura is the least satisfactory way. It upsets the instrument, and a slackened string
  sounds poor.
- R6 [ALLOWED] Tuning the E string down to Eb1 (27) or D1 (26) is entirely practical, and easier with
  machine heads than with pegs.

**Examples**
- Ex. 119, Strauss, Also sprach Zarathustra (Sehr langsam): a pp phrase for the 4th desk of basses dips below
  E. Set against how a four-string bass plays it, with notes folded up an octave, the melodic contour is
  broken.

**Agent applicability**
- For basses, keep notes at or above E1 (28) unless you want an extension/C-string bass (down to C1 = 24).
  For period (Classical) bass parts, do not fold notes below E1 up an octave automatically if the line's
  contour matters (inferred).
