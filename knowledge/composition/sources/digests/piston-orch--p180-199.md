## Ch.9 The Clarinet — Bass clarinet (clarinete bajo), end of section (scan s090L–s091R, printed p.180–181)

**Concepts**
- Shadowy fleetness: quick, light, dark runs in the low register, a colour typical of the whole clarinet
  family and available on the bass clarinet too.
- German notation (notación alemana): bass clef, sounding a major 2nd lower. French notation (notación
  francesa): treble clef, sounding a major 9th lower. Piston prints examples in both.

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] Use the bass clarinet's low register for fast, soft, "shadowy" runs and trills (p/pp).
- R2 [PREFER] Expect the bass clarinet's clarion register (registro de clarín) to sound paler and less
  incisive than the same concert pitches on the Bb clarinet. *Why:* overblown register of the larger
  instrument. When brilliance is needed at those pitches, use Bb clarinet instead (inferred).
- R3 [ALLOWED] The bass clarinet is nearly as agile as the other clarinets despite its bulky mechanism;
  a solo fugue subject with only snare drum accompaniment is idiomatic.
- R4 [AVOID] Writing above written F6 (French notation) = concert Eb5 (MIDI 75) (converted from written,
  −14). Piston calls this the highest advisable note.

**Examples**
- Milhaud, Symphony no.2 (Ex.209): bass cl. in German notation, half note = 100, p/pp rising 32nd-note runs
  and trills in the low register — the "shadowy fleetness".
- Strauss, *Ein Heldenleben* (Ex.210): bass cl. (French notation), "Lebhaft bewegt", f→ff, a vigorous
  ascending line climbing into the clarion register.
- W. Schuman, Symphony no.3 (Ex.211): bass cl. alone (plus snare drum) states a fugue subject, Leggiero,
  half note = 108–112, p, dotted/triplet figures; climbs to written F6 at mf then descends to the low
  register at p. Shows agility and the advisable top.

**Agent applicability**
- Cap generated bass-clarinet parts at concert Eb5 (MIDI 75); keep fast runs light (p–mf).
- For a pale, veiled colour on a mid-register line, the bass clarinet's clarion register is a valid choice;
  for a brighter one, the Bb clarinet.

## Ch.9 — The clarinets in Eb and D (requinto en mi bemol / clarinete en re) (scan s091R–s092L, printed p.181–182)

**Concepts**
- Eb and D clarinets stand a half step apart, like Bb and A clarinets.

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] D-clarinet parts are almost always played on the Eb clarinet (reading a half step lower).
  The D clarinet is little used; the choice of D was probably for fingering reasons.
- R2 [PREFER] Treat the Eb clarinet as a specialist's instrument: one member of the section usually
  specializes in it. *Why:* harder than the Bb to play well and in tune; small reed and cramped finger
  positions need time to adjust.
- R3 [AVOID] Asking a player to switch quickly from Bb/A clarinet to Eb clarinet. *Why:* intonation and
  embouchure adjustment.
- R4 [PREFER] Use the Eb clarinet mainly in its high register: to strengthen high woodwinds in a large
  orchestra, reinforce violins and flutes, and extend the upper tones of the other clarinets. *Why:*
  penetrating tone, incisive staccato, brilliance and agility.
- R5 [PREFER] Safe upper limit: written G6 = concert Bb6 (MIDI 94) (converted, +3). Written G#6 and A6
  (concert B6/C7, MIDI 95/96) appear more often for Eb than for Bb clarinet.
- R6 [ALLOWED] The whole range is playable down to written low E3 = concert G3 (MIDI 55) (converted, +3).
- R7 [ALLOWED] The two lower octaves lack the Bb clarinet's warmth and expressiveness; they have often been
  used for parody and imitation — a legitimate colour if used deliberately ("weak" registers of auxiliary
  instruments are new colours, as with the piccolo).

**Examples**
- Ravel, *Daphnis et Chloé* (Ex.212): Eb clar., "Animé", pp→mp→f→p, long slurred chromatic 16th-note
  figuration high in the range (passages marked 8va) — the characteristic brilliant, agile use.
- Strauss, *Till Eulenspiegel* (Ex.213): the famous D-clarinet solo, "Sehr lebhaft", mf, staccato triplet
  leaps up to the top (sfz on a high held note), then dim. falling to a low pp end — the mocking,
  grotesque character.

**Agent applicability**
- Concert range for an Eb clarinet part: G3–Bb6 (MIDI 55–94); centre it high (roughly concert G5 upward,
  inferred) when it reinforces flutes/violins.
- Use the low Eb register only for caricature/parody effects.

## Ch.9 — The basset horn (corno di bassetto) (scan s091R–s092L, printed p.183–184)

**Concepts**
- Basset horn: a clarinet with extension down to written low C3, pitched in F (sounds a perfect fifth
  lower). Lowest sound = concert F2 (MIDI 41), a fourth above the cello C string (converted, −7).
- Narrower bore than alto and bass clarinets (sometimes used as substitutes), hence a distinct timbre.
- Early basset horns had an S-turn of tubing in a box; modern ones do not. [era: 18th-c. instrument]

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] Basset horn as a period/Mozartian colour. [era: Mozart, late 18th c.; revived by Strauss]
- R2 [ALLOWED] Notation: Strauss writes it in treble clef (sounding a 5th lower) even with ledger lines for
  low C; when he uses bass clef, the part is written to sound a perfect fourth **higher** (old horn-style
  bass-clef convention). [era: late-Romantic notation]
- R3 [ALLOWED] Substitute alto or bass clarinet if no basset horn is available (timbre differs).

**Examples**
- Repertoire pointer: Mozart, *Die Entführung*, *Die Zauberflöte*, Requiem, Serenade K.361; Beethoven,
  *Prometheus* (prominent solo); Strauss, *Elektra*, *Die Frau ohne Schatten*.
- Strauss, *Elektra* (Ex.214): two basset horns, "Langsam", p, I sustains a slow chromatic line in its
  middle register while II moves below (low register, around concert C#3–A2 [?]) with 32nd-note pickups,
  mf espressivo, then dim. to p — warm, dark duet. Notation shows both clefs with the "sound" staff.

**Agent applicability**
- When the brief asks for a Mozart-period wind colour, a basset horn line in concert F2–F5 (top inferred,
  not stated here) between clarinet and bassoon is idiomatic.

## Ch.9 — The contrabass clarinet (clarinete contrabajo) (scan s092L, printed p.184)

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] Contrabass clarinets in Eb and Bb sound in the octave below the bass clarinet; the Bb one is
  metal and folded like a contrabassoon.
- R2 [ALLOWED] Concert range (Bb): D1 (just above 16-foot C) to D4 (just above middle C) = MIDI 26–62.
  Great dynamic variety, rich tone.
- R3 [AVOID] Relying on it in symphonic scoring: too rare to be an available resource. [era: 1955 USA]

## Ch.9 — Large clarinet sections (secciones grandes de clarinetes) (scan s092L, printed p.184)

**Examples** (early-20th-c. large orchestras) [era: late Romantic / early 20th c.]
- Mahler, Symphony no.8: 2 Eb, 3 Bb/A, 1 bass clarinet.
- Strauss, *Elektra*: 1 Eb, 4 Bb/A, 2 basset horns, 1 bass clarinet.
- Schoenberg, *Gurrelieder*: 2 Eb, 3 Bb/A, 2 bass clarinets.
- Stravinsky, *Le Sacre du Printemps*: 1 D (Eb), 3 Bb/A, 2 bass clarinets.

**Agent applicability**
- For a "huge late-Romantic" template, a clarinet family of 6–8 players (Eb + 3–4 Bb + 1–2 bass) is
  historically grounded.

## Ch.9 — The saxophone (saxofón; Fr. saxophone, It. saxofono/sassofono, Ger. Saxophon) (scan s092R–s093R, printed p.185–187)

**Concepts**
- Invented c.1840 by Adolphe Sax; brass body but classed as woodwind (single reed and mouthpiece like a
  clarinet); widely conical bore; commonly played by clarinettists. Fingering resembles the oboe's;
  fully chromatic.
- Registers (Fig.55, written, all sizes in treble clef): low keys written Bb3–C#4; fundamental scale
  D4–C#5; + octave key D5–C#6; extra keys + octave key D6–F6. Written range Bb3–F6 (MIDI 58–89 written).

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] Transpose from written (Fig.56): Bb soprano sounds M2 lower; Eb alto M6 lower; Bb tenor M9
  lower; Eb baritone an octave + M6 lower. Concert ranges (converted from written Bb3–F6):
  - Bb soprano: Ab3–Eb6 (MIDI 56–87)
  - Eb alto: Db3–Ab5 (MIDI 49–80)
  - Bb tenor: Ab2–Eb5 (MIDI 44–75)
  - Eb baritone: Db2–Ab4 (MIDI 37–68)
- R2 [ALLOWED] Rarer sizes: Bb bass; soprano and tenor in C; others in F. The soprano is usually straight.
- R3 [ALLOWED] The F sopranino part in Ravel's *Bolero* (sounds a 4th higher) can be played on Bb soprano,
  as it does not go too high.
- R4 [ALLOWED] Agility equals the clarinet's, **except** fast tonguing, hindered by a slightly slower
  embouchure response.
- R5 [AVOID] (Piston's 1955 view) Blending saxophone into orchestral wind combinations: its dance-band tone
  had become tremulous, oversweet and often out of tune, so it never joined the symphony orchestra.
  [era: 1950s view; contrasts with pre-1920 European use (Bizet) of a pure, steady tone between horn and reed]
- R6 [PREFER] Use the saxophone as a special instrument for a featured solo, or for a jazz flavour.

**Examples**
- Mussorgsky–Ravel, *Pictures at an Exhibition* (Tableaux d'une Exposition): pointer to the classic
  featured alto-sax solo.
- Copland, Piano Concerto (Ex.215): Bb soprano sax with piccolo and Eb clarinet, half note = 104, f,
  accented grace-note figures — 1920s jazz flavour; bassoon accented low notes, pizzicato omitted.
- Britten, *Sinfonia da Requiem*, Dies Irae (Ex.216): Eb alto sax solo, "Alla marcia", p espressivo, long
  legato phrase — unusual expressive use in a religious-character work.

**Agent applicability**
- In an orchestral cue, give sax a solo/jazz-colour role, not a blending inner voice (style choice per
  Piston; modern practice may differ).
- Avoid long fast single-tongued sax staccato; slur or simplify.

## Ch.10 The Bassoon (fagot; Fr. basson, It. fagotto, Ger. Fagott) — construction and fingering (scan s094L–s096L, printed p.188–192)

**Concepts**
- Air column about 9 ft 2 in, folded in two; lowest sound Bb1 (MIDI 34), just below the open cello C string.
  Not a transposing instrument; pitch adjusted only by reed position on the crook.
- Four joints (bell, bass/long joint, butt, wing/tenor joint); double reed on a curved crook/bocal
  (tudel). Conical bore, ~1/8 in at the crook to ~1.5 in at the bell. Wing joint thickens the wall so
  slanted tone holes reach the fingers.
- Two types: French (Buffet) and German (Heckel); German much more common in 1955 [era: 1955]. Boehm-type
  improvements never adopted: they destroy the bassoon's characteristic tone. 17–22 keys.
- Fundamental six-finger-hole scale: G2–F3 (G, A, B in the butt by right hand; C, D, E on the wing by left
  hand; open F3). Downward extension by right fourth finger/thumb (F, E) and bass/bell joints; left thumb
  operates four low keys (D, C, B, Bb).
- Three speaker keys; a pinhole vent in the crook (crook key closes automatically on low E). Overblowing by
  half hole (for F#, G), speaker keys and venting. Half hole made by the finger alone (no half-hole key as
  on oboe). Fork fingering normal for middle Eb (Eb3).
- Special trill keys: G–Ab, A–Bb, C–Db and F–Gb (key numbers 8, 15, 19, [20?]).
- High-note fingerings vary by instrument and player (high A, C keys; some have high D, E keys); finding
  workable high fingerings is experimental.

**Rules**
- R1 [AVOID] Assuming a slurred step is easy because it looks simple: some adjacent notes need coordinated
  movement of both thumbs and both fourth fingers and must still be seamless.

**Examples**
- Tchaikovsky, *1812 Overture* (Ex.217): Bn I pp, Allegro giusto, repeated slurred neighbour figures around a
  note and Eb — the move to Eb needs both thumbs and fourth fingers; deceptively hard [?] (starting note of
  the cited move cut off on the scan).

**Agent applicability**
- Keep fast slurred bassoon figures that cross the low thumb-key notes (Bb1–D2) or the Eb3 fork simple and
  not too long (inferred).

## Ch.10 — Registers (registros) (scan s096L–s098L, printed p.192–196)

**Concepts**
- Fig.59 register map (concert = written): low Bb1–F#2; fundamental scale G2–F3; harmonic 2 (octave)
  F#3–C4; harmonic 3 (sharp) ~C#4–E4; harmonic 4 (sharp) ~F4–A4 [?]; harmonic 5 (flat) ~Bb4–B4 [?];
  harmonic 6 ~C5–E5 [?]. (Box edges read from the figure; approximate.)
- No sharp timbral break between registers: low merges into middle, middle into high.

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] Lowest fifth or sixth (Bb1 to about F2/G2, MIDI 34–41/43): sonorous, vibrant, slightly rough,
  rich in audible overtones.
- R2 [AVOID] Expecting a true pianissimo in the low register: hard to attack softly, no bass-clarinet-like
  pp. Low Bb1 gives a softer piano than low B1. A handkerchief in the bell is sometimes used to soften it.
- R3 [AVOID] Low A1 (MIDI 33): appears in Wagner and Mahler, needs a special longer bell joint; outside the
  normal range. [era: Wagner/Mahler]
- R4 [PREFER] Middle ("baritone") register G2–F3 (MIDI 43–53): smoother, more subdued than the low range;
  easily covered — accompany it thinly.
- R5 [PREFER] Gentlest, most delicate: upper-middle F#3–D4 (MIDI 54–62), the first overblown notes: least
  intensity and carrying power, soft and expressive. For tender solos.
- R6 [MUST] Louder solos passing through F#3–D4 need light accompaniment.
- R7 [ALLOWED] High register D4 up to about C5 (MIDI 62–72): tone grows tense and penetrating, but remains
  well modulated up to A4/Bb4 (MIDI 69/70).
- R8 [ALLOWED] Extreme top: E5 (MIDI 76). [AVOID] approaching it by skip (hazardous, uncommon); approach the
  highest notes by step. Unison doubling (e.g. violas and cellos) makes it safer.
- R9 [AVOID] F5 (MIDI 77) and above: written by Berg (*Lulu*) and can be forced, but strained, pinched and
  unreliable. The French bassoon's smaller bore favours high notes.
- R10 [ALLOWED] Notation: tenor clef for high notes; treble clef rarely.
- R11 [ALLOWED] French and German bassoons sound quite different but both are accepted as "bassoon";
  choice is the player's taste; sometimes both in one orchestra.

**Examples**
- Milhaud, *Second Symphonic Suite* (Ex.218): Bn I and III, "Très animé", p, accented low notes around
  Bb1–Bb2 — the vibrant low register.
- Mussorgsky–Ravel, *Pictures* (Ex.219, "The Old Castle" opening): Bn I melody in 6/8 over Bn II sustained
  pedal, p, espressivo, portato — the two bottom octaves in a soft, sombre duet.
- Hindemith, Concerto for Orchestra (Ex.220): solo bassoon, f, "Mit Kraft", trills, wide-ranging line ending
  p poco a poco cresc. down to low Bb1 — the two bottom octaves in three-part melodic writing with solo
  violin and oboe.
- Brahms, Symphony no.3 (Ex.221): Bn I, Andante, p espr. dolce, gentle melody in the upper-middle register.
- Stravinsky, Symphony in C (Ex.222): Bn I solo, f marcato, staccato with shifting meters (3/8, 7/16, 2/8 …),
  tenor clef, over soft staccato chords of three trombones in their low register — a loud solo through the
  upper-middle register kept clear by light accompaniment.
- Beethoven, Symphony no.9 (Ex.223, slow mvt, Andante moderato): Bn I pp cresc. … morendo, slurred line in the
  high register — high bassoon well modulated.
- Wagner, *Tannhäuser* Overture (Ex.224): Bn I–II a2, ff, rising scale to the top E5 — top note reached
  with unison viola/cello support.
- Berg, Violin Concerto (Ex.225): Bn I–II a2, f→ff, chromatic melody to C#5, doubled at unison by bass
  clarinet; cued for alto sax and two clarinets in case the "purposely exposed" passage can't be played.

**Agent applicability**
- Bassoon MIDI range for generated parts: Bb1–E5 (34–76); normal working top C5 (72), expressive top Bb4
  (70); reserve D5–E5 for climaxes reached by step, ideally doubled.
- Low register (34–43): don't write pp entrances; use p minimum (low CC/velocity floor) and expect edge.
- For a tender bassoon solo, put the melody in F#3–D4 (54–62) and thin the accompaniment around it.
- Middle-register bassoon lines (43–53) disappear under thick textures: reduce surrounding density or
  double.

## Ch.10 — Embouchure (embocadura) (scan s098R, printed p.197)

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] Players make their own reeds; technique similar to the oboe, with a larger, heavier reed.
- R2 [ALLOWED] Unlike the oboist, the bassoonist has no problem of excess breath; low notes need a lot of
  breath, especially loud, long tones — leave breathing room after long loud low notes (inferred).
- R3 [PREFER] Wide slurs ascending; [AVOID] wide slurs descending (risky). *Why:* true for all winds.

## Ch.10 — Tonguing (articulación) (scan s098R–s099L, printed p.197–198)

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] Tonguing like the oboe's, a little less light and active. The bassoon staccato is especially
  effective.
- R2 [AVOID] Long unbroken staccato passages for one player: breathing problems; a quick breath disturbs
  the rhythm. [PREFER] Insert a musical break, or split the line between two bassoons.
- R3 [ALLOWED] Single tonguing can be remarkably fast, but tires the tongue if unrelieved by slurs or rests.
- R4 [ALLOWED] Double and triple tonguing only as a last resort.
- R5 [AVOID] Very fast continuous single-tongued passages in a tutti: players will slur or drop notes.

**Examples**
- Beethoven, Symphony no.4 (Ex.226, finale): Bn I–II a2, Allegro vivace, pp staccato, fast continuous eighth/
  16th motion — classic bassoon staccato.
- Prokofiev, Symphony no.5 (Ex.227): Bn I–II, Allegro marcato, f > p, the two bassoons interlock (one on
  low downbeat notes, the other on the off-beat figure) — staccato pattern shared so each can breathe.
- Mozart, *Così fan tutte* Overture (Ex.228): Bn I–II a2, Allegro molto, f, rapid running notes in a tutti —
  probably never single-tongued as written.

**Agent applicability**
- For long staccato ostinati, write two bassoon tracks alternating/interlocking instead of one continuous
  part; or insert rests every few bars.
- Very fast repeated-note bassoon passages: add slurs in pairs or thin the notes.

**Tags:** #orchestration/woodwinds/bassoon #orchestration/articulation/staccato #orchestration/breathing

## Ch.10 — Trills and tremolos (trinos y trémolos) (scan s099L–s099R, printed p.198–199)

**Rules**
- R1 [PREFER] Trills moved by a single finger (best). Two fingers: less easy. More fingers, especially some
  rising while others fall: harder.
- R2 [NEVER] Trills where the fourth finger must move back and forth between two keys, or where both notes
  are played with the left thumb (e.g. low Bb1–C2).
- R3 [PREFER] Check each trill against the fingering chart or a player; special trill keys on individual
  instruments may help.
- R4 [AVOID] Bassoon tremolos wider than a perfect fourth (5 semitones); same feasibility limits as trills.

**Examples**
- Fig.60: (a) single-finger trill, easy; (b) two-finger trill around G#2–A2 [?]; (c) fingers moving in
  opposite directions in the tenor-clef register [?]; (d) right fourth finger between two keys, impossible
  (around F#2–G#2 [?]); (e) both notes on left thumb, Bb1–C2, impossible.

**Agent applicability**
- Avoid generated trills below D2 (thumb-key zone) (inferred from R2); keep tremolos ≤ 5 semitones.

## Ch.10 — Orchestral uses of the bassoon (usos orquestales), start (scan s099R, printed p.199)

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] Bassoons are the natural bass of the woodwind section, with or without bass clarinet.
- R2 [PREFER] Double the string bass line at the unison or octave with bassoons: reinforces cellos/basses and
  gives point and clarity to staccato and rhythmic figures without noticeably changing the string tone.
- R3 [ALLOWED] Bassoons often strengthen or take over inner harmonic and contrapuntal voices.

**Examples**
- Brahms, Piano Concerto no.1 in D minor op.15 (Ex.229, Adagio): violins and violas con sordino play the
  melody in octaves (p espress. e legato), cellos/basses the bass, and two bassoons play the two inner
  parts (p legato) of a four-part texture, blending perfectly with the string octaves; horn in D enters
  softly later.

**Agent applicability**
- Default: double cello/bass staccato and rhythmic bass lines with bassoon (unison or 8va) for definition.
- In soft 4-part string textures with the melody doubled in octaves, give the two inner voices to two
  bassoons (p, legato) for a warm blend.
