# Walter Piston, Orchestration — scans s070L–s079R (printed p.140–159)

The range starts in the closing pages of the flute chapter (Ch.7) and covers all of Ch.8 (oboe) up to the
first page of the English horn section. Pitches: flute and oboe are non-transposing (concert = written).
Piccolo sounds an octave above written, alto flute a perfect fourth (5 semitones) below written, English horn
a perfect fifth (7 semitones) below written. MIDI numbers below are **concert** unless marked written; where I
converted from written pitch, I say so.

## Ch.7 The Flute — flutes in combination (printed p.140)

**Examples**
- Mahler, Symphony no.4 (Ex.163, "Sehr zart und innig"): 4 horns sustained p→pp, Vn I divisi, bassoons and
  bass clarinet, cellos ppp, later harp arpeggio rising and double-basses 8va bassa. Shows sudden changes of
  mode in the progression, an unusual chord spacing (m.5), and a perfect fourth (P4, 5 semitones) held ppp
  by two flutes on top: an unexpected, "magical" colour.

**Agent applicability**
- A bare P4 in two flutes, high and ppp, over a soft low-string/horn chord is a usable "magical" colour
  for a quiet cadence.
- Piston's advice for flute combinations: study scores; no rules given beyond the example.

## Ch.7 — More than two flutes (más de dos flautas) (printed p.141)

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] A woodwind section "by threes" (a tres) often uses 3 flutes instead of 2 flutes + piccolo.
- R2 [PREFER] 3 flutes when a homogeneous colour is wanted (e.g. repeated soft chords).
- R3 [ALLOWED] A large orchestra may use 4 flutes.

**Examples**
- Verdi, *Aida* (Ex.164, Andante con moto): Fl I–II in thirds/sixths and Fl III below, repeated staccato
  eighths, p dolcissimo: a soft 3-part flute chord texture.
- Berg, *Wozzeck*, end of Act III (Ex.165, Langsam, 12/8): 4 flutes in two pairs, continuous alternating
  eighth-note figures pp, poco accel. … poco rit.; in the score the flutes are doubled at the unison by
  celesta.

**Agent applicability**
- For a pp repeated-chord ostinato in flutes, give it 3 (or 4) flute tracks rather than flute + piccolo.
- Celesta doubling flutes at unison adds a bell-like sheen to a soft ostinato.

## Ch.7 — The piccolo (flautín; Fr. petite flûte, It. flauto piccolo / ottavino, Ger. kleine Flöte) (printed p.141–144)

**Concepts**
- Standard vs auxiliary instruments (instrumentos auxiliares): each woodwind family has one standard type
  (flute, oboe, Bb clarinet, bassoon); the others are auxiliaries whose first purpose is to extend the
  family's range.
- An auxiliary built to play higher is smaller, favours the top of its range, and its low notes are weaker.
- Transposing principle: the piccolo is a half-size flute (12 5/8 in.) with the same fingering, so it sounds
  an octave above written.

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] Write piccolo an octave below sounding pitch (sounds 8va).
- R2 [MUST] Lowest piccolo note is written D4 → sounding D5 (MIDI 74). There is no foot joint, so no C/C#.
  (converted from written)
- R3 [ALLOWED] Top: sounding C8 (MIDI 108), the top of the piano. Top B7 (107) and C8 (108) are quite hard to
  produce. (Piston: "another octave C" above the flute.)
- R4 [AVOID] Writing the piccolo constantly in its highest octave. The top octave is bright and piercing,
  heard over the full tutti: use it sparingly.
- R5 [PREFER] The middle range: it blends more flexibly and adds less edge. Many piccolo solos sit there.
- R6 [PREFER] When the piccolo doubles the flute, especially when the flute is in its upper octave, write
  the piccolo an octave below (written) so both **sound in unison**.
- R7 [PREFER] High woodwind chords: give the three upper notes to 2 flutes with the piccolo on top.
- R8 [ALLOWED] In tutti the piccolo adds highlights and reinforces the upper partials of the harmony.
- R9 [AVOID] Treating the piccolo as a loud-only instrument. It can play delicately and does a lot with a few
  well-placed notes. Its effect is in inverse proportion to how much it is used.
- R10 [ALLOWED] The low register has a "hollow" sound, weaker than the flute's, but usable as a colour in
  the right surroundings.
- R11 [ALLOWED] Two piccolos for a movement or a passage; the 2nd or 3rd flutist plays the extra piccolo.

**Examples**
- Shostakovich, Symphony no.7 (Ex.166, quarter = 126): piccolo solo pp, lyrical line over sustained pp
  string chords (Vn I/II, Va, Vc, Db); continues in dialogue with Vn I pp.
- Milhaud, Symphony no.2 (Ex.167, half = 63): piccolo solo p in the medium register, slow lyrical line with
  dotted rhythms.

**Agent applicability**
- Piccolo MIDI range for generation: 74–105 as the safe default, 106–108 only for a climax.
- For a flute + piccolo unison doubling, put the same MIDI pitch on both tracks. Don't stack the piccolo an
  octave above a flute that is already high.
- In tutti, check the piccolo has rests; long stretches in its top octave = error.
- A quiet piccolo solo over pp sustained strings is idiomatic (Shostakovich model).

## Ch.7 — The alto flute (flauta en sol; Fr. flûte en sol, It. flauto contralto, Ger. Altflöte) (printed p.144–145)

**Concepts**
- Auxiliary flute, 34 1/4 in. long, pitched a P4 below the flute; "flute in G" (sometimes wrongly called bass
  flute). Same mechanism as the flute, with plates/axles and discs so the hands reach the keys.
- [era: 1955] Not yet a regular orchestral instrument; many orchestras did not own one; few scores use it
  (notably *Daphnis et Chloé* and *Le Sacre du Printemps*).

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] Alto flute sounds a P4 (5 semitones) below written.
- R2 [ALLOWED] The flute's written range up to high C is available. Taking the flute's low written C4,
  sounding range ≈ G3 (55) to G6 (91). (converted from written; the low limit is inferred from the flute's
  C4 foot)
- R3 [AVOID] Relying on the top half-octave (≈ sounding C#6–G6, 85–91): it lacks brilliance next to the flute
  at the same pitch. (converted, inferred boundary)
- R4 [PREFER] The low octave (≈ sounding G3–G4, 55–67), its most individual part: rich, warm and stronger
  than the flute's low notes.
- R5 [MUST] Accompany the low register lightly, or not at all, so it is heard.
- R6 [ALLOWED] It responds even to fast double- and triple-tonguing. It needs a little more breath than the
  flute.
- R7 [ALLOWED] In a 4-flute group the alto flute extends the range downward.
- R8 [ALLOWED] In the middle register it differs from the flute only subtly in timbre.

**Examples**
- Holst, *The Planets* (Ex.168, Andante, 3/2): 3 flutes repeat a soft chord pattern pp; the alto flute plays
  an oscillating figure below, pp, as the bass of the flute group.
- Ravel, *Daphnis et Chloé* (Ex.169): unaccompanied alto flute, a ff descending run (groups of 7 and 6) into
  a "très lent, très expressif" phrase in the low register.
- Stravinsky, *Le Sacre du Printemps* (Ex.170, Tempo giusto, half = 50): alto flute f melody in the middle
  register, with grace-note interjections from Fl II, mf.

**Agent applicability**
- Use an alto flute (or a sample patch) for warm solos in MIDI 55–67 with thin or no accompaniment. Keep the
  other parts out of that octave while it plays.
- In a flute choir texture, the alto flute takes the lowest voice.

## Ch.8 The Oboe (oboe; Fr. hautbois, It. oboe, Ger. Oboe/Hoboe) — construction and fingering (printed p.146–148)

**Concepts**
- Gently tapering conical bore in three joints (upper, lower, bell). The bore is 3/16 in. at the top and 5/8 in.
  at the bell joint. Double reed (caña doble, 1/4 in. wide) bound to a cork-covered tube (staple, tudel). Length
  incl. reed ≈ 25 1/2 in.
- Pitch can be adjusted only very slightly, by moving the reed tube in or out.
- Made of grenadilla, rosewood or cocus wood; ebonite resists warping.
- Fingering based on the six-hole D-major scale (Ch.6). Right 4th finger: low C, C#, D#. Left 4th finger:
  G#, duplicate low C#, low B, low Bb. Left thumb: octave key and Bb plate. Right thumb supports the
  instrument.
- Small tone holes. On some instruments the left 3rd-finger hole is split into two small holes (one
  closed = Ab, both = G). The perforated C# key works as a half-hole (medio agujero).
- Standard keys: levers for upper Bb and C, cross F-natural key, upper D and D# trill keys. The French
  Conservatoire system (13–16 keys) is considered the best. Many alternative fingerings exist (Fig.48 shows
  common ones only).

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] Oboe is in C (non-transposing). Lowest note Bb3 (MIDI 58).
- R2 [ALLOWED] The fingering chart (Fig.48) runs Bb3 (58) to A6 (93). For the practical top, see the
  register rules below.

## Ch.8 — Overblowing (sobresoplado), reed, embouchure (printed p.148–150)

**Concepts**
- Octave keys 1 and 2 and the half-hole vent the tube to bring out upper harmonics (Fig.49):
  half-hole → C#5–D#5 (2nd harmonic of C#4–D#4); 1st octave key → E5–G#5; 2nd octave key → A5 upward (some
  oboes have an automatic 2nd octave key). The top notes (≈ C#6 up) use harmonics 3, 4 and 5 plus extra
  venting.
- Those harmonics are sharp by at least a semitone (high F6 by a whole tone), so the player uses
  lower-pitched fundamentals to get them.
- 3rd harmonics (twelfth, duodécima) can be taken from first-octave fingerings up to Bb4. F#6 and G6 are
  normally played this way. Composers do not mark these harmonics, but oboists sometimes use them for a
  very fine pianissimo.
- Reed: players make and scrape their own. Too thin → poor tone. Too thick → hard staccato and the low notes
  don't speak. Stiff → harsh, strident. The sound is very personal to the player.
- Embouchure: the lips control the reed and keep the teeth off it. The lips alone can bend pitch
  considerably.

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] Give the oboe frequent rests. It uses the least wind of any wind instrument, so phrases can be
  long, but the player is always holding back air pressure and needs to rest the lips and tongue.

**Agent applicability**
- Long oboe lines are possible without breath marks, but insert regular rests (e.g. at least a beat or two
  every few bars) for endurance. (inferred quantity)

## Ch.8 — Tonguing (articulación con lengua) (printed p.150–151)

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] All the tongue articulations described for winds (Ch.6) work on the oboe, with a few reservations.
- R2 [AVOID] Double- and triple-tonguing (doble/triple picado) on the oboe. They are emergency resources for
  tempos too fast for single-tonguing and are not idiomatic (unlike the flute).
- R3 [ALLOWED] Light single-tongued staccato can be surprisingly fast.
- R4 [PREFER] The oboe for crisp staccato in wind combinations. Its staccato is the sharpest, driest and
  lightest of the winds.
- R5 [MUST] Every note starts with a definite tongued attack ("t"). A reed blown without the tongue speaks at
  an unpredictable moment, so breath-only "soundless" entries can't be controlled.
- R6 [ALLOWED] Flutter-tonguing (frullato, Flatterzunge): only moderately effective on the oboe.

**Examples**
- Rossini, *La Scala di Seta*, Sinfonia (Ex.171, Allegro, cut time): Ob I p, long run of fast staccato
  eighths and scale figures, single-tongued.
- Stravinsky, *Le Sacre du Printemps* (Ex.172, Vivo): 4 oboes, ff accented chord stabs, then
  flutter-tongued figures f cresc. passed between pairs I–II and III–IV.

**Agent applicability**
- Oboe entries always have an audible attack. For a "from nothing" swell, choose flute, clarinet or strings.
- Oboe staccato: short notes (≈ 40–50% of the grid value, inferred) at moderate velocities read well.
  Fast repeated notes beyond single-tongue speed suit flute better.

## Ch.8 — Timbre and registers (timbre y registros) (printed p.151–153)

**Concepts**
- The tone is called pungent, tangy, nasal, penetrating, biting, piercing, raspy. It differs sharply from
  the flute.
- National schools (French vs German) and individual players differ in tone.

**Rules**
- R1 [era: Baroque vs 1955] The oboe tone of Piston's day is much thinner and lighter than in Bach's time.
  For a Baroque sound, the oboe was fuller and more robust.
- R2 [MUST] Low register: unlike the flute, the oboe gets more intense, and can get coarse, as it descends
  through its lowest fifth (Bb3–F4, MIDI 58–65). Keep low notes in soft passages short or expect them to
  stand out.
- R3 [AVOID] Low Bb3 (58) in soft music: it can't be attacked softly. Use it only in robust music. B3 (59)
  is more manageable.
- R4 [NEVER] G#6 (92) and A6 (93) in orchestral writing: they are insecure and lack oboe quality.
- R5 [ALLOWED] F#6 (90) and G6 (91) are a normal part of the range but are rarely used.
- R6 [AVOID] Approaching F#6 and G6 by skip, or using them in active legato figuration.
- R7 [PREFER] Expressive oboe melody below A5 (81). Above A5 the tone grows thinner, and above D6 (86) it
  sounds less like an oboe.
- R8 [ALLOWED] Notes above A5 add brilliance when the oboe doubles the woodwinds or upper strings.

**Examples**
- Bach, *Brandenburg Concerto no.1*, Adagio (Ex.173): Ob I ornate cantilena with trills, in a minor key.
  Shows the melody written for the older, fuller oboe tone [era: Baroque].
- Prokofiev, *Chout* (Ex.174, Lento): Ob I pp dolce line descending into the bottom of the range, ending p
  on low notes (around B3/C4 [?]). A soft low-register nuance for which low Bb would be unsuitable.
- Ravel, *Daphnis et Chloé* (Ex.175, Lent): Ob I p→ppp solo in the upper register. Very hard but very
  effective when well played.
- Chausson, Symphony in Bb (Ex.176, Très animé): Ob I–II mf→f in the high register (up to about E6/F#6
  [?]) doubling the strings for brilliance.

**Agent applicability**
- Default oboe generation range: MIDI 59–86. Allow 87–91 only for stepwise approach and non-florid lines.
  Never use 92 or above; use 58 only at f or louder.
- For pp low-register oboe, prefer 60–65 with gentle velocity, or swap the line to English horn (see below).

## Ch.8 — Solo melody (printed p.153–154)

**Rules**
- R1 [PREFER] The oboe as a solo voice. Its distinctive colour and expressiveness make it a favourite.
- R2 [PREFER] Folk-song-like melodies suit its simple plaintive quality (lo plañidero).
- R3 [ALLOWED] The oboe evokes an exotic pipe (ornamented, narrow-range lines).

**Examples**
- Schumann, Symphony no.2, Adagio espressivo (Ex.177): Ob I cantabile p, long-breathed melody with fp
  accents, cresc./dim.
- Brahms, Symphony no.2 (Ex.178, Allegretto grazioso, 3/4): Ob I p graceful melody; later Ob II joins in
  thirds/sixths.
- Bartók, *Concerto for Orchestra* (Ex.179, Allegretto): Ob I p folk-style tune within one octave, changing
  meters (2/4, 5/8); repeated later an octave lower.
- Falla, *El Amor Brujo* (Ex.180, Poco moderato): Ob f, narrow-range ornamental figures in 32nds and
  quintuplets, ending morendo: an exotic pipe.

**Agent applicability**
- For a folk or plaintive tune, an oboe solo within one octave (e.g. MIDI 69–81) with light accompaniment is
  idiomatic. Restating it an octave lower is a classic variation.

## Ch.8 — Agility (agilidad) (printed p.154–155)

**Rules**
- R1 [AVOID] Fast slurred arpeggios and figuration on the oboe unless a special effect is wanted. The oboe
  can play them, but agility suits the double-reed tone less than flute or clarinet.
- R2 [PREFER] The oboe for neatly articulated rhythmic patterns.

**Examples**
- Ravel, *Le Tombeau de Couperin*, Prélude (Ex.181, Vif, 12/16): continuous pp 16th-note figuration in
  short slur groups with accents. Hard, but very successful oboe writing, because it is rhythmic rather than
  arpeggiated.

**Agent applicability**
- Give fast slurred arpeggios to flute or clarinet. Give the oboe repeated-pattern, neatly articulated
  16ths.

**Tags:** #orchestration/woodwinds/oboe #orchestration/agility

## Ch.8 — Trills and tremolos (trinos y trémolos) (printed p.155)

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] All major and minor trills (M2/m2, 2/1 semitones). A full table can't be given (players and
  instruments vary).
- R2 [AVOID] Tremolos (fingered alternations) wider than a P4 (5 semitones): they are undependable.
- R3 [AVOID] Tremolos on notes above the staff (≈ above F5/G5, MIDI 77–79): poor effect.

## Ch.8 — Orchestral uses of the oboe (usos orquestales) (printed p.155–158)

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] Usual groupings: 2 oboes; 2 oboes + English horn; 3 oboes + English horn; less commonly 3
  oboes. 4 oboes + EH (Stravinsky, *Symphony of Psalms*) is a special case.
- R2 [ALLOWED] Oboes reinforce violins or violas by doubling at the unison.
- R3 [PREFER] Oboes inside wind chords give them incisiveness and give rhythmic figures sharp definition.
- R4 [ALLOWED] Oboe + clarinet in unison is forceful.
- R5 [ALLOWED] 3 oboes + EH, all in their low registers, make an extremely pungent chord.
- R6 [ALLOWED] Two oboes in thirds or other close intervals have a "tangy" quality no other woodwind pair has.
- R7 [ALLOWED] Economy: a single simple oboe motive repeated in diminishing dynamics over changing harmonies
  can have maximum effect (Sibelius).
- R8 [ALLOWED] Bells up (Ger. *Schalltrichter auf!*, campana al aire) for oboes and clarinets gives a
  more strident sound (Mahler). The marking is more usual in brass. [era: late-Romantic, Mahler]
- R9 [ALLOWED] Muted oboes (con sordina): a handkerchief stuffed in the bell, for a veiled pp.

**Examples**
- Strauss, *Till Eulenspiegel* (Ex.182, Sehr lebhaft, 6/8): Ob I–II, Ob III and EH hold a low-register chord
  sfz then dim., under a "lustig" clarinet in D figure: the pungent low double-reed chord.
- Debussy, *La Mer* (Ex.183, Animé et tumultueux): Ob I–II in close intervals, sfz>p swells: tangy oboe
  pair.
- Sibelius, Symphony no.4, closing bars (Ex.184, Allegro): Ob I repeats a short motive spanning a major
  seventh (M7, 11 semitones), f→p→mf→p, while Fl I (high) and strings (p, fp) change the harmony beneath.
- Stravinsky, *Petrushka* (Ex.185, Lento, 3/4): 3 oboes con sordina, pp → pochissimo, sustained close
  chords: muted oboes.

**Agent applicability**
- To sharpen a woodwind chord or a rhythmic tutti figure, include the oboes.
- Oboe doubling Vn I or Va at the same MIDI pitch adds edge without changing the line.
- With sample libraries, emulate "bells up" with higher velocity/CC (brighter layer) and "muted" with low
  dynamics plus a high-cut. (inferred)

## Ch.8 — The English horn (corno inglés; Fr. cor anglais, It. corno inglese, Ger. englisch Horn) (printed p.158–159)

**Concepts**
- Same mechanism as the oboe but without the low Bb. 31 1/2 in. long incl. reed, with a bulb-shaped bell, a
  bent metal crook holding the reed, and a larger double reed. A neck cord carries its weight.
- Transposing instrument in F: the player reads C with oboe fingering and sounds F. All notes sound a
  P5 (7 semitones) below written.
- [era: 18th c. → mid-19th c.] Used in 18th-c. chamber music and divertimenti. It became a normal
  orchestral auxiliary of the oboe family from the mid-19th c. At first it was used only now and then for a
  featured solo, played by the 2nd oboist, who then went back to oboe. Later a specialist EH player became
  standard, sometimes also playing 3rd or 4th oboe.

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] Write the EH a P5 above sounding; it sounds a P5 lower than written.
- R2 [MUST] Lowest note: written B3 → sounding E3 (MIDI 52) ("E-natural below middle C"). (converted from
  written)
- R3 [ALLOWED] Top: by analogy with the oboe's practical top (written G6), ≈ sounding C6 (84). (inferred;
  Piston gives no top limit on these pages)
- R4 [ALLOWED] Its lowest fifth (≈ E3–B3, 52–59) is not coarse like the oboe's low fifth. The larger size
  favours the low notes.
- R5 [PREFER] Use the EH for the oboe's lower-fifth notes (≈ Bb3–F4) when a softer, less penetrating tone is
  wanted.
- R6 [PREFER] In a 3-part double-reed chord, replace Ob III with the EH for a smoother, more homogeneous
  sound.
- R7 [PREFER] The EH for singing melodies. Its colour is close to the oboe's but very individual, owing
  partly to the bulb bell. The literature has many EH solos.

**Examples**
- Bartók, *Second Rhapsody for Violin and Orchestra* (Ex.186, Moderato): EH pp→mp, slow line descending
  into its low register.

**Agent applicability**
- EH MIDI range for generation: 52–81 safe, up to 84 sparingly. (upper bound inferred)
- A soft double-reed line in MIDI 52–65 belongs to the EH, not the oboe.
- Write MIDI at concert pitch; transposition only matters for parts.
