## 15 Orquestación — introduction (scan p.548, printed p.547)

**Concepts**
- Orchestrating (orquestar): thinking *for* the orchestra; requires knowing each instrument's register,
  limits, and the timbre, power and texture of every part of its range, solo and in combination.
- Maroges' axiom (quoted): the greatest colorists get maximum brilliance and vibration with a minimum of
  color.

**Rules**
- R1 [AVOID] A constant flow of changing colors and effects: it endangers the piece's structure.
- R2 [AVOID] Endless tutti (all sections sounding together for long stretches): listeners tire quickly of
  that monotony. *Why:* a classic inexperienced-orchestrator trap.
- R3 [PREFER] Careful balance of colors and clear definition of musical elements; orchestration should
  clarify the musical organisation.
- R4 [MUST] In a long work, use orchestration to clarify the form (sections, returns, structural
  gestures — melodic, rhythmic, harmonic).
- R5 Footnote (Rimsky-Korsakov, quoted by Adler): orchestration cannot rescue badly composed music; only
  what is well written can be well orchestrated.

**Agent applicability**
- Plan a density/color map per section before writing parts; reserve full tutti for structural peaks.

## Tutti in unison and octaves (tutti al unísono en octavas) (scan p.549–557, printed p.548–556)

**Concepts**
- Tutti: simultaneous use of most or all instruments. Two types: **partial tutti (tutti parcial)** — only
  some of the available instruments; **grand tutti (gran tutti)** — all or almost all.
- Unison tutti (tutti al unísono): everyone on the same pitches. Rare, because of register limits.
- Octave tutti (tutti a la octava) / multi-octave tutti (tutti multi-octava): the line spread over two or
  more octaves.

**Rules — unison tutti**
- R6 [MUST] In a unison tutti, omit instruments whose range cannot cover the whole passage (D'Indy drops
  flutes and oboes: they can't reach its low part).
- R7 [ALLOWED] (modern option) Use an instrument for only part of a unison line to color one accent, e.g.
  oboes (maybe plus a muted trumpet) only on the *sf* note.
- R8 [AVOID] Giving a unison line to an instrument that can play it but is not in its best register
  (D'Indy's line would be possible on trumpets but poor).
- R9 [PREFER] Mark trombones much softer (D'Indy: *ppp* against *f* elsewhere) so they don't dominate a
  unison tutti.
- R10 [PREFER] Put violins on the G string (sul G, "4e corde") to add power to a low-register unison.
- R11 [ALLOWED] Drop double basses from a unison line even if they can reach it at pitch, when the high notes
  would be hard to keep in tune and weak in sound (little contribution).
- R12 [PREFER] Contrast articulations inside a unison tutti for tension: strings/trombones articulated
  diversely, violas alone on tremolo, against a smooth legato line in woodwinds and horns.

**Rules — octave and multi-octave tutti**
- R13 [PREFER] Build a climactic unison *crescendo* by adding brass and timpani to the note the rest of the
  orchestra already plays (Barber: D), then release into the main idea spread over several octaves.
- R14 [PREFER] Reserve an instrument family's most powerful register for the key gesture (Barber keeps the
  horns' highest register for the descending glissando instead of an earlier octave leap).
- R15 [MUST] Drop instruments from a descending tutti glissando/line when the goal note falls below their
  range (Barber removes piccolo and flutes; violins take over the Bb3 = MIDI 58 goal [?: text calls it the
  highest note of m.136]).
- R16 [AVOID] Low brass (trombones, tuba) in a fast tutti glissando: too heavy; also omit them where they
  can't reach the high note.
- R17 [PREFER] Mark the arrival/cadence with a fresh low entry (3rd trombone, tuba, timpani on low Bb) to
  reinforce the phrase.
- R18 [PREFER] Multi-octave unison is a standard way to introduce a new idea, recapitulate an old one, or
  state a melodic gesture before developing it contrapuntally.
- R19 [MUST] [era: natural horn, Classical] Omit natural horns from a chromatic/modulating unison theme they
  can't play (Mozart 40).
- R20 [PREFER] Vary the density per octave: Mozart's layout = vln 1+2 unison with flute + 2 oboes (top);
  octave below thin (violas + clarinets only); next octave denser (cellos + 2 bassoons) plus basses an
  octave lower. Later he thickens the middle register.
- R21 [PREFER] Put woodwinds an octave **above** a violin melody rather than at unison to add brilliance and
  cover the space better; it also keeps flute and oboe out of trouble at the line's low end.
  *Why:* at unison the flute and oboe would have to drop the final low note, and oboes sound too raucous
  on C#4 (MIDI 61).
- R22 [AVOID] Leaving the flute alone in its highest register in a tutti.
- R23 [PREFER] Use the octave layout to make the listener hear the theme exactly as intended (register
  placement of a theme is a compositional decision; orchestration clarifies it).
- R24 [era: Beethoven, natural brass + 2 timpani] Classical tutti is constrained: horns/trumpets play only
  some notes, timpani only tonic/dominant, violin top register barely used.
- R25 [ALLOWED] [modern] Re-orchestrating such a tutti today: piccolo on the high upbeat (D7 = MIDI 98),
  1st flute on the upper D, 1st violins an octave higher, trumpets and all four horns in octaves with the
  orchestra throughout, up to 5 timpani covering every melody note. (Adler: "not necessarily better".)
- R26 [PREFER] Turn instrument limits into structure: Beethoven's horns 3–4, trumpets and timpani hammer the
  final D (m.21) and restate the open 5ths/4ths of the opening.
- R27 [PREFER] Flutes doubled a2 to give weight to a high line (Beethoven 9 from m.18).
- R28 [ALLOWED] Leap a doubling instrument (2nd clarinet up a tenth = 16 semitones) to reinforce the register
  of the leading instruments rather than continue in its own lower octave.
- R29 [PREFER] For an energetic fast melodic tutti, give the agile eighth-note line to woodwinds + strings
  and let brass hold sustained harmony / punctuate with repeated chords (Smetana). *Why:* sustained brass
  harmony gives cohesion and brightness while keeping the line light.
- R30 [AVOID] Doubling a fast eighth-note melody in trumpets/horns: it makes the line sound clumsy and
  removes lightness.
- R31 [AVOID] Removing brass entirely from such a tutti: it sounds fragile.
- R32 [era: mid-19th-c. orchestra] Adding xylophone for "ping" on woodwind eighths is a modern option that
  would be out of style for Smetana.

**Examples**
- Ex.15-1 D'Indy, *Istar*, mm.206–216 (Moderato): partial unison tutti — EH, clar a2, bass clar, bsn, horns
  a2, trb *ppp*, vln I/II on G string *f et bien chanté*, cellos; violas tremolo; DB pizz; harp/timp light.
- Ex.15-2 Barber, Symphony No.1, mm.128–136 (q = 116): grand tutti on D — fl/clar 9-tuplet runs, sustained
  winds with tremolo, three trumpets in independent rhythms, trb *f marcatissimo*, strings repeated D;
  timpani solo (m.133); fermata *ff*; high horns glissando, harp glissando, strings 16-note descending runs
  (m.135); low entry on Bb (m.136).
- Ex.15-3/15-4 Mozart, Symphony No.40, iv, mm.125–132 (Allegro assai, *f*): partial multi-octave tutti
  without horns; 15-4 shows the intended theme in its intended octaves.
- Ex.15-5 Beethoven, Symphony No.9, i, mm.16–21 (Allegro ma non troppo, *ff*): grand tutti limited by D/Bb
  natural horns, D trumpets, 2 timpani; flutes an octave above vln/ob/cl.
- Ex.15-6 Smetana, *The Bartered Bride*, overture, mm.1–12 (Vivacissimo, *ff*): picc/fl/ob/C clar/bsn +
  strings melodic tutti; brass + timp sustain then punctuate (*f*→*ff*); strings *sf non legato*.

**Agent applicability**
- For a unison tutti, check every doubling instrument's range against the whole line (`instruments.json`);
  drop any that would break off mid-line.
- Brass in a unison tutti: CC1/velocity 20–30 lower than strings/woods so they reinforce but don't cover.
- Octave tutti: woods one octave above violins; violas + clarinets thin middle octave; cellos + bassoons
  denser below, basses an octave under the cellos.
- Fast tutti melody: woods + strings on the line; brass long chords or short stabs on strong beats.

## Foreground / middleground / background distribution (Distribución de primer plano, plano medio y plano de fondo) (scan p.559–570, printed p.558–569)

**Concepts**
- Foreground (primer plano), middleground (plano medio), background (plano de fondo): layers used to
  describe melody-with-accompaniment, strictly homophonic chord textures, and polyphonic textures (two or
  more ideas, one usually standing out).
- Homophonic layouts studied: (1) melody with accompaniment; (2) voicing of isolated chords (spacing and
  doubling); (3) doubling and voice-leading in a chordal texture.
- Three chord layouts across sections (from earlier chapters): juxtaposed / superimposed (yuxtapuesto,
  sobreimpuesto — sections stacked in separate registers), interlocked (intercalado — alternating notes),
  enclosed (incluido, encerrado — one section's notes inside another's). In full orchestra they are
  normally combined.
- Horn fifths (5as de trompa, quintas de trompa) [era: natural horns/trumpets, 18th–19th c.]: two-voice
  harmonisation of melody degrees 1–2–3 as unison-or-sixth, fifth, third (only the middle interval is a
  real fifth). See also Ex.9-9.
- Klangfarben / pointillist orchestration: nearly every note colored by a different instrument.

**Rules — melody with accompaniment**
- R33 [PREFER] Give the solo melody an accompaniment color that contrasts with it (sometimes strikingly);
  alternatively accompany with instruments of the same or similar type.
- R34 [ALLOWED] String pizzicato under a clarinet solo to make the solo sound warmer (Tchaikovsky).
- R35 Mutes (con sordino) have little effect on pizzicato.
- R36 [PREFER] Introduce a new melodic element with a **new color at the start of the phrase (including its
  upbeat)**, or put a **silence before the new color** appears, so it is clearly perceived. Especially
  important in polyphonic textures.
- R37 [PREFER] When a melody passes to another section, contrast the layer below: muted violins in octaves
  over low strings, sustained bassoon/horn and timpani roll.
- R38 [PREFER] Switch a section's role from foreground to background one bar before the new foreground
  enters (Weber: violins to arpeggios, low strings to long notes, a bar before the horns).
- R39 [PREFER] Hold back a color (e.g. horns) so its first entry sounds fresh.
- R40 [PREFER] When orchestrating a piano texture, add the implied elements explicitly (e.g. the
  pedal-sustained bass D2 = MIDI 38 becomes a 4th-horn and bass-clarinet pedal).
- R41 [ALLOWED] Distribute the parts of a two-layer texture among many instruments so no two play identical
  material for more than a few notes (Klangfarben effect).
- R42 [ALLOWED] Treating the large orchestra as a chamber ensemble (soloistic colors) is valid; so is the
  full-orchestra sound. Adler accepts both if done with imagination.

**Rules — chord layout for full orchestra** (general rules, p.563)
- R43 [MUST] Melody notes more prominent than harmony notes.
- R44 [MUST] Assign notes to instruments in their best register so they can play at the intended dynamic.
- R45 [PREFER] Double with acoustically related instruments, especially at the unison.
- R46 [PREFER] For an "open", resonant tonic sound, double the root heavily (all strings, trumpets, horns,
  timpani on D, flutes an octave higher) and give the other chord tones to a few winds only; the effect is
  of reinforcing the root's lower harmonics (Beethoven m.1).
- R47 [PREFER] For a fuller sound, have every section play all chord tones (Beethoven m.21).
- R48 [PREFER] Double the lower-middle octave (D3–D4 = MIDI 50–62) with many instruments and the upper
  octave (D5–D6 = MIDI 74–86) with woodwinds only: emphasis sits in the middle.
- R49 [PREFER] Brilliant tutti chord (Weber m.284): strings juxtaposed from C3 to G6 (MIDI 48–91), brass
  juxtaposed, only oboes/clarinets interlocked; leave instruments undoubled where they sit well (ob 1,
  hn 1 and 4, trb 2, bsn 2). Use as a model for a brilliant chord.
- R50 [ALLOWED] A single instrument may hold the lowest note alone (Weber: 2nd bassoon alone on C2 =
  MIDI 36) [?: as stated by Adler].
- R51 [PREFER] Doubling choice by affinity: Weber treats 2nd oboe + 1st trumpet as closer than clarinet +
  trumpet.
- R52 [ALLOWED] Non-divisi double/triple stops in strings for dramatic, resonant chords (Schumann).
- R53 [PREFER] Double every chord tone at least once; double string notes in juxtaposed woodwinds.
- R54 [era: natural brass] Horns/trumpets in Bb play only horn-fifth patterns in such progressions.
- R55 [MUST] Keep correct voice-leading inside orchestral chords: sevenths resolve down (V4/3), tonic and
  dominant common tones held where possible (except inside horn fifths).
- R56 [PREFER] Withhold trombones and timpani from one phrase to make the next one heavier.
- R57 [PREFER] Romantic ending: give the top notes of the final chord to woodwinds/flutes (Brahms 3).
- R58 [AVOID] Very high, undoubled flute notes in a soft final chord (F6 = MIDI 89 at *p*): hard to articulate
  softly and the chord sounds louder than written; flutes dominate because nothing neutralises their
  piercing timbre.
- R59 [ALLOWED] Easier-to-control alternative: strings on the top octave, woodwinds doubling an octave below.
- R60 [PREFER] Clear high/low separation (only flutes high, everything else middle/low) gives a luminous,
  warm and sweet chord.
- R61 [PREFER] Violin melody in its best register, flutes reinforcing only the melody notes at the start of
  each bar; chords fully represented in every section and octave from C2 to F6 (MIDI 36–89) (Brahms 3,
  mm.183–187).
- R62 [PREFER] Very brilliant final chord: all upper octaves contain all chord tones; the lowest octave
  (cellos, basses, tuba, 3rd trombone, timpani) only the tonic; brilliance from high brass doubled one and
  two octaves above by violins, flutes, piccolos; string tremolos, timpani/triangle rolls, a cymbal crash
  (Mahler 1).
- R63 [MUST] For an *ff → pp* chord, give each note to instruments that can manage that dynamic on that
  note.
- R64 [ALLOWED] Very high notes at *pp* are fine on violins (they can play any note softly) and on piccolo in
  an easy register (Debussy: top A#6 = MIDI 94 on vln I + piccolo [written A#5] + harp).
- R65 [ALLOWED] Harp "pings" on the top note fading over four bars.
- R66 [ALLOWED] Leave a large gap between the low bass (C#2 = MIDI 37) and the rest of the chord.
- R67 [PREFER] In a fading chord, double at unison only the essential notes (bass C#, trumpets' melody note
  E#5 = MIDI 77 doubled by ob 2 at unison and English horn an octave lower, top A#); all other notes single
  so they stay soft and keep their color. [?: text writes the top note once as LA#6 and once as LA#5.]

**Rules — doubling in a chordal texture** (begins p.569)
- R68 Reasons to double: (1) raise the dynamic level; (2) subtle color shades.
- R69 Octave doublings normally lose the individual color of the instruments involved.
- R70 [AVOID] Constant repeated doublings: they become tiresome.
- R71 Octave doublings often give a much clearer, brighter result and can raise the dynamic level.
- R72 What and how to double is a matter of taste and conscious decision. Beethoven often used octave
  doublings to raise dynamics; Mozart, Mendelssohn, Debussy and Ravel preferred pure, unmixed, soloistic
  melody sounds, but also used octave doublings at times (continues on next page).

**Examples**
- Ex.15-7 Tchaikovsky, *Francesca da Rimini*, mm.325–349: A-clarinet solo (8-bar upbeat-like intro, then
  *p cantabile*) over muted string pizz *p*; from m.340 vln I/II arco take the melody in octaves, violas
  sustained, bsn pedal, horn line *p un poco marcato*, timpani roll *ppp*.
- Ex.15-8 Weber, *Der Freischütz*, overture, mm.1–19 (Adagio): winds + strings unison C *pp<f*, string
  phrases; from m.10 vln I/II eighth arpeggios *pp*, violas/celli/basses long notes; four horns soli (2 in C,
  2 in F) as foreground.
- Ex.15-9 Mahler, *Kindertotenlieder*, "Nun will die Sonn'", mm.58–61: piano version (RH eighths foreground,
  LH eighths background) vs orchestral Klangfarben version (fl, ob, clar, bass clar, bsn, horn pedal, harp,
  strings, each fragment differently colored).
- Ex.15-10 Beethoven, *Missa Solemnis*, chords m.1 (root-heavy, open) and m.21 (all sections, full), D major.
- Ex.15-11 Weber, *Der Freischütz*, overture, m.284: brilliant *ff* climax chord (with string tremolo and
  timp roll in the real score).
- Ex.15-12 Schumann, Symphony No.1, i, mm.3–4: I–IV–V4/3–I in Bb, woodwinds juxtaposed over strings,
  horns/trumpets in horn fifths.
- Ex.15-13 Horn fifths for 2 C trumpets and 2 horns (degrees 1–2–3).
- Ex.15-14 Brahms, Symphony No.3, i, final chord (*p*, F major): flutes alone on top.
- Ex.15-15 Brahms, Symphony No.3, i, mm.183–187 (Allegro moderato, *sf*): reduction + full score; violin
  melody, viola/cello repeated-chord pulse, brass/wind sustained chords, timpani trills.
- Ex.15-16 Mahler, Symphony No.1, iv, mm.723–727: *fff* D-major final chord.
- Ex.15-17 Debussy, *La Mer*, ii "Jeux de vagues", 4 bars before reh. 29: *ff → pp* chord over C#.

**Agent applicability**
- Chord voicing check per tutti chord: list each pitch and its doubling count; root/bass most doubled,
  third and seventh doubled sparingly; middle octave (≈ MIDI 50–74) carries the most instruments.
- Soft high top notes: put them on violins (and piccolo only in its easy range), not on undoubled flutes
  near MIDI 89+; or swap: strings on top, woods an octave below.
- Fade chords (*ff→pp*): CC1/CC11 ramps on sustaining instruments; double only bass, melody note and top.
- New melody entry: change instrument at the phrase start (incl. upbeat) or leave a rest before it.
- Hand a melody between sections: one bar earlier, turn the outgoing section into background (arpeggio /
  long notes at lower CC1).
- When orchestrating from a piano sketch, add the implied pedal bass as an explicit sustained part.
