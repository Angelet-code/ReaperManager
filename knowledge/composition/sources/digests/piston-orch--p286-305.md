# Piston, *Orchestration* — scans s143L–s152R (printed p.286–305)

## The Tuba, end of chapter (scan s143L–s147R, printed p.286–295)

**Concepts**
- Contrabass tuba in Bb (tuba contrabajo en Sib, double Bb bass, BBb; 18-foot Bb): the largest tuba. The author calls it mainly a military-band instrument that orchestras sometimes use.
- Tenor tuba (tuba tenor, euphonium/bombardino): a 9-foot Bb tuba. It is lighter and more flexible than the bass tuba.
- Wagner tubas (tubas wagnerianas, Bayreuth tubas, *Waldhorntuben*, It. *corno tube*): tenor tuba in 9-foot Bb and bass tuba in 12-foot F. They are horn-like instruments with a bore wider than the horn's and narrower than the tuba's. They take horn mouthpieces and have 4 left-hand valves (the 4th corrects low-valve pitch, as the horn player's hand does). The bell is elliptical and points up and to one side.
- Ophicleide (oficleido): a keyed brass bass shaped like a metal bassoon, with a wide conical bore, padded key-covered holes and a cupped mouthpiece. It came in 8-foot C or 9-foot Bb, with a range like the bassoon's. Reportedly it sounded like a euphonium.
- *con sord.* / tuba mute (sordina): a large cone, over 2 ft tall, made of cardboard or similar.

**Rules**
- R1 [AVOID] On the tuba covered just before this range (identity [?]): the pedal tone is very hard to produce, and the valve notes just above it are unreliable. NEVER write above its 8th harmonic.
- R2 [ALLOWED] Contrabass tuba in Bb range (Fig. 91, sounding): pedal Bb0 (MIDI 22), "barely possible". The chromatic range runs from about E1 (28) [?] to Bb3 (58, the 8th harmonic). Read from the figure: the harmonic points are Bb1, F2, Bb2, D3, F3, (Ab3), Bb3.
- R3 [AVOID] Contrabass tuba in combination: its very heavy sound is unwieldy. It does have a fine pianissimo.
- R4 [MUST] Breath: tubas, especially the large ones, use enormous air. Players may breathe between single notes in slow non-legato low basses. Use long slurs and long held notes sparingly; leave breathing gaps in sustained low lines.
- R5 [PREFER] For beauty of tone, give the tuba soft, quiet legato phrases in the middle register (Ex. 313).
- R6 [ALLOWED] Low notes can be soft and unobtrusive. *Why:* they blend under the texture.
- R7 [AVOID] Asking for soft playing in the high register. The high register tends to shout and is hard to subdue.
- R8 [ALLOWED] Loud tuba is hearty and robust and carries over the full tutti.
- R9 [ALLOWED] Mute: mainly reduces volume and gives a muffled sound. AVOID muted high register, which sounds strained.
- R10 [ALLOWED] Agility: if it were not for breath, the tuba would count as agile. Active figures in the low register work (Ex. 316, 317).
- R11 [PREFER] Wide skips are fairly easy and add interest to a bass line. They can be slurred (Ex. 314) or detached (Ex. 318).
- R12 [ALLOWED] Trills are surprisingly good on tuba but rarely used in symphonic music (Ex. 319).
- R13 [ALLOWED] Single, double and triple tonguing all work. Flutter-tonguing is very unusual (Ex. 321).
- R14 [ALLOWED] Tuba pp staccato works like a double-bass pizzicato and can stand in for one (inferred use).
- R15 [AVOID] Long passages of sharp rhythmic tonguing, especially forte. They get heavy and tend to drag.
- R16 [PREFER] Main role: add massive solidity to the tutti bass by doubling other bass instruments at the unison or the octave below.
- R17 [PREFER] The tuba acts as bass of a quartet with 3 trombones. As bass of the whole brass section it doubles the bass trombone or perhaps the 4th horn.
- R18 [ALLOWED] The tuba alone can be the bass of a wind group, e.g. woodwinds plus horns (Ex. 323).
- R19 [ALLOWED] Use the tuba for broad, expressive melodic basses (Ex. 324). It is indispensable in ponderous, melodramatic passages (Ex. 325).
- R20 [MUST] For a tuba solo, say in the score which tuba you mean. Example: Ravel's *Pictures* solo ("Bydlo") suits the euphonium (small Bb tuba), while the rest of that score needs a larger tuba. Players often use two tubas in that work.
- R21 [PREFER] The F tuba is probably the best all-round orchestral tuba. Treat the smaller tuba as an accessory instrument and name it in the score.
- R22 [MUST] Safe bottom limit for all bass tubas: Eb five spaces below the bass staff (14-foot Eb) = Eb1 (MIDI 27). Upper limits differ by instrument; in general the top is the 8th harmonic of the tube length.
- R23 [PREFER] Write tenor tuba parts at concert pitch, which is the usual custom. Strauss's *Don Quixote* writes the tenor tuba in Bb, sounding a M2 (2 semitones) lower, and Piston calls this a departure from custom.
- R24 [ALLOWED] The tenor tuba plays lively figures with more lightness and flexibility than the bass tuba.
- R25 [ALLOWED] Wagner tubas: their ranges are like horns of the same pitch, Bb alto and F. The tone is strange and individual: broader, less concentrated and less flexible than the horn's. `[era: Wagner–Bruckner–Strauss, rare after]`
- R26 [PREFER] Wagner tubas are doubles for horns 5–8. Wagner's design was a family of 2 tenor and 2 bass Wagner tubas plus a contrabass tuba in 16-foot C. `[era: Wagner's Ring, 1870s]`
- R27 [AVOID] Writing for Wagner tubas without confirming the orchestra has them. Few orchestras own them and few composers after Wagner used them. `[era: 1955]`
- R28 [ALLOWED] Wagner tuba notations vary from score to score. Agents write MIDI at concert pitch, so this matters only when reading these scores:
  - Wagner, first notation: written like horns. The tenor in Bb sounds a M2 (2) lower. The bass in F sounds a P5 (7) lower, with bass clef in the horn "old notation".
  - Wagner, later notation: tenor in Eb sounds a M6 (9) lower; bass in Bb basso sounds a M9 (14) lower.
  - Bruckner 7 and Strauss *Elektra*: tenor in Bb sounds a M9 (14) lower; bass in F sounds an octave plus a P5 (19) lower.
  - Bruckner 9: tenor in Bb sounds a M2 (2) lower. The bass tubas are written in bass clef and sound a P4 (5) **above** written.
- R29 [ALLOWED] The ophicleide is obsolete. Mid-19th-c. bass parts written for it (Mendelssohn, Schumann, Meyerbeer, Verdi, Wagner *Rienzi*) are now played on tuba. `[era: mid-19th c.]` For a period sound, give it a bassoon-like range and a euphonium-like tone (inferred).

**Examples**
- Ex. 313 Wagner, *Eine Faust-Ouvertüre*: solo bass tuba, pp, slow legato descending line. Shows the soft legato tone.
- Ex. 314 Strauss, *Also sprach Zarathustra*: 2 bass tubas a2, ff, rising from the low register with wide slurred leaps to a high sustained line.
- Ex. 315 Stravinsky, *L'Oiseau de feu*: muted tuba (con sord.), mf, chromatic melodic line.
- Ex. 316 Hindemith, *Symphonic Metamorphosis*: tuba, f, lively running low-register figures with some slurs.
- Ex. 317 Shostakovich, Symphony 7: rapid 16th/32nd scale runs and turns low in the tuba, changing meters.
- Ex. 318 Ravel, *La Valse*: detached wide-leap waltz bass with sff accents on low notes.
- Ex. 319 Wagner, *Meistersinger* Prelude: bass tuba, mf cresc., stepwise climb ending on a trill.
- Ex. 320 Strauss, *Don Quixote* (Sancho Panza): tenor tuba in Bb with bass clarinet; 3 bassoons hold chords. Light, chattering figures.
- Ex. 321 Schoenberg, *Erwartung*: 4 trombones plus bass tuba, all muted and flutter-tongued, pppp, chromatic chords.
- Ex. 322 Mahler, Symphony 8: bass tuba, f, staccato figures leaping by octave in the low register.
- Ex. 323 Stravinsky, *Jeu de cartes*: tuba alone is the bass under flutes, oboes, clarinets and horns (f, accented), then plays secco. Trombone and bassoons are added later.
- Ex. 324 Prokofiev, Symphony 5: tuba, f espressivo, broad chromatic melodic bass across a wide range.
- Ex. 325 Respighi, *Fontane di Roma*: tuba, fff, heavy accented low line. Melodramatic weight.
- Ex. 326 Mussorgsky–Ravel, *Tableaux* ("Bydlo"): tuba solo, pp poco a poco cresc., lying high (about G#4 [?]). This is why the euphonium is preferred for it.
- Ex. 327 Bruckner, Symphony 7: 2 tenor Wagner tubas (Bb), 2 bass Wagner tubas (F) and contrabass tuba in a solemn chorale, p cresc.–dim. The book omits the string unison doublings.

**Agent applicability**
- Tuba MIDI floor: 27 (Eb1). Go below only for a deliberately named contrabass effect (BBb, chromatic down to about 28; pedal 22 barely possible).
- Keep loud solo tuba lines in the middle register. Keep high notes for forte. Use the low register for soft support.
- Leave a rest or a breath (≥ about 1/8 beat) every few bars in sustained tuba parts. Do not tie long slurred low notes over many bars.
- Default brass bass: tuba doubles the bass trombone at the unison, or the bass line an octave below in tutti. Avoid long fast repeated tonguing at f.
- For a soft pizz-like pulse, tuba pp staccato can replace or double basses pizz.

## Percussion Instruments: introduction (scan s148L–s148R, printed p.296–297)

**Concepts**
- Percussion: instruments that sound when one object strikes another. Piston also counts some non-struck instruments because percussionists play them. Keyboards other than mallet instruments are left out (the piano is not part of the section).
- The classifications: definite pitch versus indefinite pitch; membranophones (membranófonos) versus idiophones (idiófonos). The practical one is by how often the instrument is used:
  - (a) Standard section: timpani, snare drum, bass drum, cymbals, triangle, tambourine, glockenspiel, wood block, tam-tam.
  - (b) Auxiliary: castanets, xylophone, tenor drum, *tambour de Provence*, bells (chimes), antique cymbals, vibraphone.
  - (c) Sound effects: an open-ended list, e.g. sleigh bells, sandpaper blocks, wind machine, rattle, whip, cowbells, anvil, siren.
  - (d) Exotic, mostly Latin American at the time: maracas, claves, güiro, bongós, timbales, tom-tom, temple blocks. `[era: 1955]`

**Agent applicability**
- A default orchestral template needs only group (a). Add (b)–(d) only for colour or a programmatic reason.

## Kettledrums / Timpani (timbales; It. *timpani*; Ger. *Pauken*) (scan s148R–s152R, printed p.297–303)

**Concepts**
- Construction: a copper hemispherical kettle on a stand with a calfskin head `[era: pre-plastic heads]`. The head's tension is set by screws around the rim.
- Pedal (mechanical) timpani (timbales de pedal): a foot pedal sets the head tension, so the pitch can change while playing. Some French and Italian drums tune by rotating the drum instead `[era]`.
- Roll (redoble): the drum's sustained tone, made by rapidly alternating single strokes. It is written as a trill (`tr~`).
- *coperti* (covered): muted with a cloth on the head opposite the striking point.
- *secco* (dry): a staccato note damped at once.
- Sticks (baquetas): wooden handles with heads of felt, flannel, cotton thread, wood, cork or sponge, in small, medium and large sizes. Soft sticks: Fr. *baguettes d'éponge*, It. *bacchette di spugna*, Ger. *Schwammschlägel*. Hard sticks: Fr. *baguettes en bois*, It. *bacchette di legno*, Ger. *Holzschlägel*.

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] Classical practice used a pair of timpani tuned to tonic and dominant. `[era: Classical, c.1750–1820]` By 1955, 3–4 drums with retuning within a movement were normal.
- R2 [ALLOWED] Most players have at least 2 pedal drums. There is no need to state the number of drums and initial tuning unless the part is unusual (5+ drums, or more than one player). `[era: 1955]`
- R3 [MUST] Know which drum plays each note and what retuning each passage needs. Retuning takes time, and the player often retunes while the orchestra plays in another key.
- R4 [MUST] Use these drum sizes and ranges (Fig. 93, sounding):

  | Drum | Low | High |
  |---|---|---|
  | 30″ | D2 (38) | A2 (45) |
  | 28″ | F2 (41) | C3 (48) |
  | 25″ | Bb2 (46) | F3 (53) |
  | 23″ | D3 (50) | A3 (57) |

  Other sizes:
  - 32″ drum: down to C2 (36).
  - 23″ (sometimes 22″): can reach Bb3 (58).
- R5 [ALLOWED] Small extension drums. They lack true timpani resonance, so use them rarely:
  - Stravinsky's *timpano piccolo* (*Sacre*): B3 (59).
  - Ravel's *petite timbale en ré* (*L'Enfant et les sortilèges*): the D above the staff, D4 (62) [?].
- R6 [PREFER] Within F2–F3 two drums overlap, so choose the drum that puts the note mid-range: the best tone comes when the head is neither slack nor tight. Example: low F2 and F#2 sound much better on the 30″ than on the 28″.
- R7 [ALLOWED] Harder sticks are louder and give clarity to soft rhythmic figures. Softer sticks give a warmer, more resonant tone. Composers may specify soft or hard sticks.
- R8 [MUST] The low drums sit at the player's left. Each rhythm across drums has its own hand sequence (l/r, Fig. 94), so leave figures that alternate hands naturally.
- R9 [MUST] Timpani sound at written pitch (no transposition).
- R10 [MUST] Timpani ring long and the player damps by hand. Mark every short note staccato, and add *secco* to a single short note for safety.
- R11 [MUST] Notate the exact length of a roll. Three forms (Fig. 95):
  - (a) Separate accented rolls: a definite break with an accent on each new note.
  - (b) A roll tied across the barline: the change is unaccented, like a bow change on basses.
  - (c) A continuous roll ending on an unaccented short note.
- R12 [MUST] Mark percussion dynamics accurately. Every dynamic change stands out on percussion.
- R13 [PREFER] Main function: dynamic reinforcement of the tutti, especially of the bass, adding colour and buoyancy. Timpani also underline rhythmic outlines.
- R14 [ALLOWED] Soft timpani are effective in many ways, e.g. alone as an ostinato bass (Ex. 328).
- R15 [ALLOWED] One player can play a solo passage on 4 drums (Ex. 329).
- R16 [ALLOWED] Pedal glissando, two types (Ex. 330):
  - A single stroke, then a pedal move while the drum still rings.
  - A roll held through a pedal change, e.g. G# → C#.
- R17 [ALLOWED] Chromatic lines with a clear pedal change on each semitone (not a glissando, Ex. 331). These need pedal drums.
- R18 [ALLOWED] Muted (*coperti*) timpani. A tremolo can be shared between two drums (Ex. 332).
- R19 [ALLOWED] For stronger accents:
  - Tune two drums to the same note and strike them together, or
  - Strike one drum with both sticks (*con due bacchette*, Ex. 333).
- R20 [ALLOWED] Other effects: snare-drum sticks on timpani, striking near the rim, two different sticks at once.
- R21 [ALLOWED] Large timpani forces need several players. Berlioz, *Symphonie fantastique* "Scène aux champs": 4 players, 4 drums. Berlioz *Requiem*: 10 timpanists on 16 drums. Stravinsky *Sacre* (Ex. 334): 2 players, 5 drums including the piccolo.

**Examples**
- Ex. 328 Tchaikovsky, Symphony 4: timpani alone hold a pp ostinato bass in 9/8 under pp syncopated string chords, then p woodwind and horn chords.
- Ex. 329 Holst, *The Planets*: solo for 4 drums and one player, f cresc. to ff, ending in a roll.
- Ex. 330 Bartók, *Music for Strings, Percussion and Celesta*: mf single strokes followed by pedal glides, then a p roll gliding G# → C#.
- Ex. 331 d'Indy, *Jour d'été à la montagne*: chromatic timpani line with a pedal change on each half step, pp < mf > pp.
- Ex. 332 Debussy, *Nuages*: pp roll, then a tremolo between two drums a 5th/4th apart [?] in 6/4.
- Ex. 333 Mahler, Symphony 4: *con due bacchette*, ff, pesante, every note accented.
- Ex. 334 Stravinsky, *Le Sacre du printemps*: timpano piccolo plus timpani 1–2 and 3–4. Interlocking f triplet rhythms in 5/4 and 3/4 at ♩=126.

**Agent applicability**
- Clamp timpani notes to 36–58. For 1–2 drums classical style, keep to two pitches (tonic and dominant), each placed in the middle of a drum's range (R4/R6).
- Between pitch changes on the same drum, leave about 1–2 bars of rest unless the drum is a pedal drum and the change is small (inferred).
- Rolls in MIDI: use the library's roll articulation or fast 32nd-note single strokes at even velocity. A tie across a barline means no accent there; a new note means an accent (velocity bump). Most patches have a roll keyswitch; see AGENTS.md for Symphony Series Timpani.
- Use timpani to underline tutti bass hits and rhythmic outlines, not as a continuous layer. Soft ostinato timpani can be the sole bass under thin textures.

## The Snare Drum (scan s151R–s152R, printed p.303–305, continues)

**Concepts**
- Snare drum (caja, tambor): named for the snares, gut strings under the lower head. Side drum is a generic term (from the drum being hung on the right side for marching). Drum = Fr. *caisse*/*tambour*, It. *cassa*/*tamburo*, Ger. *Trommel*.
- *caisse claire*: the smallest, lightest and clearest snare drum. Stravinsky's *L'Histoire du soldat* specifies large and small sizes.
- Snare drum in the usual sense is slightly larger than the *caisse claire*: It. *tamburo piccolo*, Ger. *kleine Trommel*. In French scores *tambour* or *tambour militaire* (It. *tamburo militare*) means snare drum. English "military drum" (parade drum, field drum) means a drum larger than the common snare.
- Tenor drum (long drum; Fr. *caisse roulante*, It. *cassa rullante*, Ger. *Rührtrommel*): larger and deeper, about twice the size of the snare, longer in proportion, **no snares**.
- *Tambour de Provence* (tabor): the longest side drum, a regional drum. It is usually imitated by another instrument. Bizet, *L'Arlésienne* "Farandole" uses it for an ostinato.
- Standard snare (1955): 14–15″ diameter, about 6″ deep. The upper head is the batter head, the lower head is the snare head. The shell was formerly wood and is now brass; other side drums are usually wood `[era: 1955]`. Sticks: hard wood (hickory) with oval tips. Felt-tipped sticks are sometimes used on the tenor drum and always on the tambour de Provence.
- Without snares (It. *scordato*, *senza corde*): with the snares loosened the pitch drops about an octave and the drum sounds like a tom-tom.

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] If you use a side drum other than snare or tenor drum, explain it in a note in the score. Drum names are inconsistent between countries.
- R2 [PREFER] Normally play with snares on: a rattling, bright, dry tone with fairly high indefinite pitch.
- R3 [ALLOWED] Snares off for a dark, tom-tom-like colour (Ex. 335).
- R4 [PREFER] Write indefinite-pitch percussion on a single line without a clef. A five-line staff is acceptable.
- R5 [ALLOWED] Strokes combine singles and doubles (the stick rebounds and repeats). The main strokes are roll, flam and drag. A roll is rapidly alternating double strokes (rrll…).
- R6 [MUST] Roll notation (Fig. 96):
  - (a) Three tremolo slashes mean a roll, not literal 32nds.
  - (b) A trill sign (`tr~`) shows the roll's exact duration more clearly.
  - (c) The explanation of (c) continues on p.306.

**Examples**
- Ex. 335 Bartók, *Concerto for Orchestra*, II: snare drum alone without snares, mf → dim. → p. Syncopated 2/4 figures open and close the movement.

**Agent applicability**
- Snare drum MIDI: use the library's snares-on patch by default. Choose snares-off or tom-tom only for a deliberate muffled march colour.
- Tenor drum = no snares, deeper; it fits funeral or processional textures (inferred).
