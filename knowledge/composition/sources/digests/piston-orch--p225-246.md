# Walter Piston, *Orchestration* — scans s112R–s122R (Chapter 12, The Horn)

Chapter 12 runs printed p.225–245; Chapter 13 "The Trumpet" starts on p.246 (s123L) and is not covered here.

Pitch conventions in this digest: C4 = MIDI 60. "Written" = the horn part as notated; "sound" = concert
pitch (what MIDI must contain). **Converted by me:** every MIDI number below, every "sounds" value for horn in F
not printed in the book, and the per-crook table in §12.3. Fig. 68, 69 and 71 print sounding notes and were
read directly from the images; conversions agree with them.

Names: horn (Fr. *cor*, It. *corno*, Ger. *Horn*; Sp. *trompa*). Natural / hand horn (Fr. *cor simple*, It.
*corno naturale*, Ger. *Waldhorn*). Valve horn (Fr. *cor à pistons*, *cor chromatique*; It. *corno ventile*;
Ger. *Ventilhorn*).

## 12.1 Introduction and the true French horn (printed p.225)

**Concepts**
- "French horn" is an English name (18th c.); the modern horn was developed mainly in Germany.
- True French horn: small bore, piston valves, light/brilliant/open tone; used almost only in France.
  The German horn (rotary valves) has the warm, rich, rather dark tone and is used practically everywhere.
- Names *hunting horn / cor de chasse / corno da caccia / Jagdhorn* do not appear in scores of the period studied.

**Agent applicability**
- Default horn sample = German (rotary) horn colour: dark, warm. A "French" bright horn colour is a
  regional exception, not the default.

## 12.2 The natural horn: construction and crooks (printed p.225–226) [era: natural horn, 18th–19th c.]

**Concepts**
- Natural horn: open hoop of brass tubing, ~1/4 in bore at the mouthpiece, widening gently to ~3 in at the
  bell throat; bell ~11 in across. The wide bell favours the lower partials in the tone.
- U-shaped tuning slide inside the hoop (that part of the bore is cylindrical so it can slide).
- Crook (*Bogen*, Sp. *tonillo*): extra tubing inserted between mouthpiece and horn to change its key; later
  crooks were made with slides and replaced the tuning slide.
- Base instrument = horn pitched in 8-foot C. With crooks, total tube length ranges from ~9 ft 3 in
  (B♭ alto) to ~18 ft 6 in (B♭ basso).

**Rules**
- R1 [era: natural horn] Most-used crooks: B♭ alto, A, A♭, G, F, E, E♭, D, C, B♭ basso.
- R2 [era: natural horn] Crooks higher than B♭ alto are very rare; Haydn symphonies have horns in B♮ alto and
  C alto. C alto appears to be the horn without crooks.
- R3 [era: natural horn] "Horn in C" always means the C (basso) crook: sounds an octave (12) lower than written.
- R4 [era: natural horn] Low crooks seen: A basso (Italian opera), B♮ basso (Brahms Symphony 1), D[?] crook in
  Berlioz *Roméo et Juliette* (letter after D cut off at page edge; likely D♭ [?]).

**Examples**
- Fig. 68: written C5 (8th harmonic) sounds, per crook: B♭ alto B♭4, A A4, A♭ A♭4, G G4, F F4, E E4, E♭ E♭4,
  D D4, C C4, B♭ basso B♭3. i.e. every crook sounds *below* written by the interval from its key down from C.

## 12.3 Natural horn range, high/low players, factitious tones (printed p.227) [era: natural horn]

**Concepts**
- Harmonic series as written for horn (any crook), with harmonic numbers: 1 C2 (pedal, rarely usable),
  2 C3, 3 G3, 4 C4, 5 E4, 6 G4, 7 B♭4 (flat), 8 C5, 9 D5, 10 E5, 11 between F5 and F♯5, 12 G5, 13 A5 (flat,
  "A♭" by partial stopping), 14 B♭5, 15 B5, 16 C6. (Harmonics 1–5 printed in Fig. 69; 7, 11, 13 tuning
  described on p.228/237; the rest is the standard series the book refers back to, (inferred) for 9, 10, 14, 15.)
- High and low hornists (*cor alto / cor basse*): specialization of players by register.
- Factitious / artificial tones (*sons factices*): notes below the 2nd harmonic produced by bending it down
  with the lips, up to an augmented fourth (6 semitones).

**Rules**
- R5 [era: natural horn] Range = 2nd to 16th harmonic inclusive, with qualifications (R6, R7).
- R6 [era: natural horn] Shorter tubes (crook F and higher) speak with difficulty above the 12th harmonic;
  upper harmonics come more easily on longer (lower) crooks.
- R7 [era: natural horn] Pedal (1st harmonic): good on B♭ alto, poor on F, hardly ever on lower crooks.
- R8 [era: natural horn] For a time E, E♭ and D crooks were preferred, and 2nd (or 4th) horn parts were more
  interesting; in the 19th c. preference moved to the middle F horn, which became standard and received valves.
- R9 [MUST] In a horn quartet, horns 1 and 3 are high, 2 and 4 are low. (Principle persisted into modern practice.)
- R10 [era: natural horn] Low players used a larger mouthpiece and different embouchure, and added factitious
  tones: written B2, B♭2, A2, A♭2, G2, G♭2 below the 2nd harmonic C3 (Fig. 69).
- R11 [AVOID] Factitious tones: modern players do not make a practice of using them.

**Examples**
- Fig. 69 (horn in F), written → sound: E4→A3, C4→F3, G3→C3, C3→F2, factitious B2…G♭2 → E2, E♭2, D2, D♭2,
  C2, B1. Middle staff shows the same in old bass-clef notation (see 12.4): G3 written as G2, C3 as C2, etc.

**Agent applicability**
- To evoke Classical-era horns, restrict each horn line to the open harmonics of its crook (sound = written
  series minus transposition), avoid 7th/11th/13th harmonics except as passing/stopped colour, and keep
  most material between harmonics 3 and 12.
- Give horn 2/4 the low notes, horn 1/3 the high notes, even in modern writing (R9, R49).

## 12.4 Bass-clef notation (printed p.228)

**Rules**
- R12 [era: old notation, standard through early 20th c.] In bass clef, horn notes are written an octave
  too low. Horn in F: treble clef sounds a P5 (7) lower than written; old-notation bass clef sounds a P4 (5)
  *higher* than written.
- R13 [PREFER] Modern composers write bass-clef notes in the proper octave (sounding P5 lower like treble);
  when doing so, MUST put a note in the score: "horns in bass clef sound a fifth lower than written".

**Agent applicability**
- When reading/importing horn parts in bass clef, check which convention applies before converting to MIDI:
  old notation = written + 5 semitones; new notation = written − 7 semitones (horn in F).
- When exporting parts, use new notation and include the explanatory note.

## 12.5 Stopped notes on the natural horn (printed p.228–229) [era: natural horn]

**Concepts**
- Hand horn technique: right hand in the bell, partly closing the throat, controls tone quality, corrects
  out-of-tune harmonics, and lowers harmonics to make new notes.
- Stopped tone (*sonido tapado*) on the natural horn: dull, subdued compared with open tones.

**Rules**
- R14 [era: natural horn] Half to three-quarters closing lowers a harmonic a semitone; full stopping a whole
  tone. (Contrast modern full stopping, R27, which raises pitch; Piston describes different hand positions.)
- R15 [era: natural horn] Melodies mixing stopped and open notes need great embouchure/hand skill to sound even;
  such passages work in piano but are unconvincing in forte.
- R16 [era: natural horn] Composers were sparing with new stopped notes; Beethoven 9 Adagio 4th-horn part is
  exceptional.
- R17 [PREFER] Hand technique remains valuable on the valve horn for tone control and intonation.
- R18 [PREFER] Natural-horn idiom (melodic turns built from the harmonic series, "horn fifths") shaped
  melody and harmony for all instruments, even piano; simple horn melodies show it.

**Examples**
- Ex. 243 Schubert, Symphony 7 ("Great" C major) opening, horns I–II in C a2, Andante, p→pp: unison melody,
  stopped notes marked +. A is stopped from the already-flat 7th harmonic (needs less stopping than B, which
  is stopped from C); F is stopped from the 11th harmonic (lies between F and F♯). Shows why it works only piano.
- Ex. 244 Beethoven, Piano Concerto 5, horns I–II in E♭, Allegro, p dolce: two-part melody almost entirely in
  natural-horn intervals (thirds, sixths, horn fifths) — the "universal" horn-call quality.

**Tags:** #orchestration/brass/horn #era/natural-horn #stopped #idiom/horn-fifths

## 12.6 Choice of crooks (printed p.229–230) [era: natural horn]

**Rules**
- R19 [era: natural horn] Default crook = key of the movement (gives the most scale notes).
- R20 [era: natural horn] Exceptions: a planned modulation to a related key where horns have a prominent part;
  or low-pitched horns preferred when the key is high (e.g. E horns for a piece in A major).
- R21 [era: natural horn] Four horns were most often crooked in pairs in two different keys.
- R22 [era: natural horn, Berlioz] Berlioz often crooked four horns in four different keys to obtain
  specific note combinations.

**Examples**
- Ex. 245 Berlioz, *Roméo et Juliette*: horns I in E, II in E♭, III in G, IV in F (IV starting in bass clef),
  f; one melodic line passed between the four differently crooked horns, the composite sounding line given on
  a bass-clef staff (about F2 up to E♭4 [?]), with long held notes and fermatas — four crooks yield a nearly
  chromatic line no single natural horn could play.

**Agent applicability**
- To write "natural-horn" style in a modern mock-up: choose a crook per pair (R19–R21), generate each
  horn's notes from its crook's harmonic series (table §12.3), and only pass chromatic lines between pairs.

**Tags:** #orchestration/brass/horn #era/natural-horn #crooks

## 12.7 The valve horn: transition (printed p.231–232) [era: c.1830–1900]

**Rules**
- R23 [era: early valve horn] Transition to valve horns took longer for composers than players; natural horns
  were still used occasionally up to c.1900, and many composers wrote "as for natural horns" knowing valve
  horns would play.
- R24 [era: Wagner, 1840s] *Rienzi*, *The Flying Dutchman*, *Tannhäuser*: two valve horns + two natural horns.
- R25 [era: Wagner, Lohengrin only] Wagner's crook markings ("in G", "in E", "in D") mid-passage meant
  instantaneous key changes by valve on an F horn: "in E" = hold 2nd valve and play as natural horn in E;
  "in D" = 3rd valve or valves 1+2; "in G" impossible by valves (transpose up M2 and play with valves). The
  notation was abandoned after *Lohengrin*.

**Examples**
- Ex. 246 Wagner, *Tannhäuser* Bacchanale: Ventilhörner I–II in E play chromatic chords ff; Waldhörner I–II
  in E answer with accented notes that natural horns can manage (harmonics and stopped notes).
- Ex. 247 Wagner, *Lohengrin* Prelude to Act III: horns I–II and III–IV switching "in G" → "in E" → "in D",
  ff, triplet-pickup fanfare figures; shows the experimental crook markings.
- Ex. 248 Brahms, Symphony 3, III (Poco allegretto), horn I in C, p espressivo: hand-horn-style melody,
  playable on natural horn but better on valve horn because stopped notes are eliminated.

**Tags:** #orchestration/brass/horn #era/valve-transition #wagner #brahms

## 12.8 The modern horn: F, B♭ and double horn (printed p.232–235)

**Concepts**
- Valve horn in F: tube with valves ~17 ft; the standard horn through the 20th c.
- High B♭ horn (*trompa en Si♭ agudo*): tube with valves under 13 ft; brilliant, easier top; adopted by 1st and
  3rd players. To get the same sounds, notes are transposed down a P4, so the 16th harmonic of the F horn is
  only the 12th harmonic of the B♭ horn.
- Double horn (*trompa doble*, F/B♭): one instrument combining F and B♭ horns; duplicated valve tubes, one set of
  rotary valves, 4th (thumb) valve switches F↔B♭ at any time and in any part of the range. "Fast replacing" the
  single horn (1955).

**Rules**
- R26 [PREFER] The B♭ horn lacks the horn's prized warmth and dark beauty; the double horn is the workable
  solution (good qualities of both, defects avoidable).
- R27 [MUST] Double-horn music is always written as for horn in F (sounds P5 = 7 lower than written), whichever
  division is used; the choice of division is the player's and is not marked in the part.
- R28 [PREFER] B♭ division is used for upper-register facility, prompter response of low notes, and
  alternative fingerings everywhere; its tone has some hardness that good players equalize, with the right hand
  as the main tone control.
- R29 [AVOID] F horn above its 16th harmonic: top notes difficult and tiring to the embouchure.

**Range — double horn** (Fig. 71; read from image, MIDI converted by me)
- Written (new notation, horn in F): D2 (38) – C6 (84).
  - D2, E♭2, E2, F2 written = pedal tones on the B♭ division → sound G1 31, A♭1 32, A1 33, B♭1 34.
  - F♯2 written (42) = lowest note of the F division (2nd harmonic C3 lowered by valves) → sound B1 35.
  - C3 written (48) = 2nd harmonic on F division → sound F2 41.
  - C6 written (84) = 16th harmonic on F division = 12th harmonic on B♭ division → sound F5 77.
- Old notation of the same: D1, E♭1, E1, F1, F♯1, C2 in bass clef (octave too low), C6 in treble.
- **Sounding range: G1 (31) – F5 (77)**; B1 (35) and below need the B♭ division's pedals or valved F-side lows.

**Fingering** (Fig. 72) — on the double horn players learn a direct set of B♭-division fingerings for the
written F part rather than transposing mentally. Example: sound C4, B3, B♭3, A3 = written (F) G4, F♯4, F4, E4;
F-division fingering 0, 2, 1, 0; B♭-division (thumb down) fingering 1, 1+2, 0, 2 (same as a single B♭ horn
reading D4, C♯4, C4, B3).

**Agent applicability**
- Horn MIDI hard limits: 31–77; practical writing range much narrower (see R44); treat 31–40 as
  specialist pedals/low-horn territory (horn 2/4, doubled, soft — R46).
- Never expect a sample library or part to say "F" vs "B♭" division; write one part in F.

## 12.9 Stopped tones, mutes, brassy (printed p.235–237)

**Concepts**
- Modern stopped tone (*bouché*, *chiuso*, *gestopft*; Sp. *tapado*): hand closes the bell completely; muted
  quality; wind must be forced a little, giving the metallic vibrancy of muted brass.
- Stop valve / transposing valve: extra thumb half-tone valve that does the semitone correction automatically;
  common on B♭ horns, rare on double horns (weight).
- Mute (*sordina*, metal or cardboard): normally does not alter pitch; some metal mutes need the same correction
  as stopping.
- Brassy (*cuivré*, *schmetternd*; Sp. *metálico/cuivré*): metallic, blaring sound from lip tension and hard
  blowing; can be applied to open, stopped or muted notes; degree variable.

**Rules**
- R30 [MUST] Full stopping raises every note a semitone; the player transposes down a semitone to compensate.
  Write the desired sounding pitch; it is the player's job (inferred from text: notation shows the intended note).
- R31 [AVOID] Stopped notes below written middle C (written C4 → sound F3, MIDI 53): unreliable; use mutes for
  low notes instead.
- R32 [MUST] Use the terms correctly: muted ≠ stopped, though often confused. Stopped = *bouché/chiuso/gestopft*;
  cancel with open = *ouvert/aperto/offen, nicht gestopft*. Mute on/off directions are the same words as for
  strings.
- R33 [ALLOWED] Mark single stopped notes with + and cancel with o.
- R34 [PREFER] Stopped metallic colour is weak in soft playing and pronounced in forte; usual brassy marking =
  stopped + sfz or f (*bouché-cuivré*, *gestopft-stark anblasen*).
- R35 Fact: horn players often replace requested mutes with quicker hand stopping; composers who don't
  distinguish the effects encourage this.

**Examples**
- Ex. 249 D'Indy, *Istar*, horn in F, Très lent, *bouchées*, mf → dim → p: slow descending chromatic solo
  (written about D5 down to C4 [?]) all stopped — soft, veiled solo colour.
- Ex. 250 Falla, *El Amor Brujo*, horns I–II in F, Vivo 2/4, ff with sfz: fast repeated chromatic figure where
  one horn plays open (o) while the other plays the same figure stopped (+), then they swap — open/stopped
  echo as a rhythmic colour effect.
- Ex. 251 Mahler, *Das Lied von der Erde*, horns I/III and II in F: sustained chord p < sff (gestopft) > p —
  a stopped forte stab inside a swelling held chord.
- Ex. 252 Beethoven, Symphony 3, I, horn I in F, Allegro con brio, dolce…cresc. then sudden p on last note:
  rising arpeggio to written G5 then A♭5 (sound C5 72 → D♭5 73). Modern natural-horn practice: keep the G
  fingering and play A♭ fully stopped, getting the sudden piano and the semitone rise at once; in Beethoven's
  day the A♭ was probably partially stopped from the 13th harmonic.

**Agent applicability**
- For stopped horn in samples use a "stopped/bouché" articulation at f–sfz for snarl, or at p for distant
  veiled colour; keep stopped notes ≥ MIDI 53; for lower muted colour use a mute patch.
- Open/stopped alternation between two horns on the same figure (Falla) is a cheap, idiomatic colour device.

## 12.10 Trills (printed p.237–238)

**Rules**
- R36 [ALLOWED] Lip trills only where two harmonics lie a second apart (upper register); limited range and
  intonation, comparable to vocal trills.
- R37 [PREFER] Valve trills: half-tone trills (2nd valve, shortest tube) generally work better than whole-tone
  trills (exceptions exist); valve tube inertia slows response.
- R38 [AVOID] Relying on horn trills for brilliance: always somewhat sluggish compared with woodwinds; not
  among the horn's best features.

**Examples**
- Ex. 253 Mahler, Symphony 9, II (Ländler), horns in F, 3/4: horn I trills on short notes over II/IV chords,
  sf → p → dim → pp — trill used for a rustic-dance character.

**Tags:** #orchestration/brass/horn #technique/trill

## 12.11 Tonguing and endurance (printed p.238–239)

**Rules**
- R39 [PREFER] All tonguings (single, double, triple) are used, but articulation is never as sharp/crisp as
  trumpet (long tube).
- R40 [MUST] Give the tongue rest in long fast passages.
- R41 [PREFER] For rapid continuous figures, distribute the figure among several horns in alternation rather
  than one player — more rhythmic and playable.

**Examples**
- Ex. 254 Mussorgsky–Ravel, *Pictures at an Exhibition* (Limoges, Meno mosso), horns I & III in F, ff,
  unbroken 16th notes in two parts — cited as very difficult.
- Ex. 255 Strauss, *Don Quixote*, horns I–IV in F, Gemächlich, f: a triplet arpeggio figure passed in turn
  between horns II, IV, I, III (low horns in bass clef) — the playable way to get continuous motion (R41).
- Ex. 256 Ravel, *Rapsodie espagnole*, horn I in F, Assez animé, bouché, p, 6/8: repeated notes and
  turn figures in double and triple tonguing, stopped — delicate, effective.

**Tags:** #orchestration/brass/horn #technique/tonguing #endurance

## 12.12 Glissando and bell-up (printed p.239–240)

**Rules**
- R42 [ALLOWED] Horn glissando = quick upward slur over the harmonics of one valve position (a "rip"; upward,
  harmonic steps, not chromatic).
- R43 [ALLOWED] Bell up (*pavillon en l'air*, *campana in aria*, *Schalltrichter auf*): hand out of the bell,
  horn raised horizontally; lusty and blaring; intonation may suffer.

**Examples**
- Ex. 257 Stravinsky, *Le Sacre du printemps*, horns V–VI in F, Vivo, ff/sff: fast upward gliss. to a high
  accented note, then low staccato notes in bass clef.
- Ex. 258 Mahler, Symphony 4, horns I/III and II/IV in F, a2, ff *Schalltrichter auf!*: high accented
  pickup into a held note (I/III) against low bass-clef notes (II/IV), then dim to p and pp.

**Agent applicability**
- Horn rip in MIDI: fast run of notes from the harmonic series of one fingering (not a chromatic scale),
  ending on an accented long note.

**Tags:** #orchestration/brass/horn #technique/glissando #technique/bells-up

## 12.13 Melodic uses and doubling (printed p.240–242)

**Rules**
- R44 [PREFER] The horn is a favourite solo instrument; most comfortable range = 3rd or 4th harmonic to about the
  12th of the F horn: written G3/C4 – G5 → **sound C3 (48) / F3 (53) – C5 (72)** (converted by me).
- R45 [MUST] Double extreme high notes in loud passages for volume and secure attack.
- R46 [PREFER] Double extreme low notes even when very soft: pp low notes gain steadiness and tone and do not get
  louder with two horns.
- R47 [PREFER] Horn melodies in octaves are rich and sonorous.
- R48 [PREFER] Unison of the whole horn group (usually 4; all of them if more) for breadth and force.
- R49 [ALLOWED] Low horns (2, 4) may occasionally go to the top of the range, especially when doubling in
  unison; the high/low classification still stands, and all four can play the whole range.

**Examples**
- Ex. 259 Shostakovich, Symphony 5, I, horn I in F, ♩=84, p: long legato solo starting low (written A3 [?])
  and climbing; a score note says the high B (written B5 → sound E5, 76) only if it can be played piano. Horn
  in canon a 12th (octave + fifth) below the flute; harp chords and pulsating strings accompany. Needs fine
  breath control.
- Ex. 260 Copland, Symphony 3, horns I–II and III–IV in F a2, ♩/half=120, fff: high accented notes and chords
  with horns doubled; II and IV join ad lib. on the top note fff.
- Ex. 261 Mahler, Symphony 1, III, Feierlich und gemessen, sempre pp deutlich: three horns a3 (II/IV/VII,
  bass clef) on the low bass line, doubled an octave higher by three more (I/III/VI).
- Ex. 262 Debussy, *La Mer*, horns I–II and III–IV in F, Modéré 6/8, p expressif et soutenu: melody in
  octaves in each pair.
- Ex. 263 Holst, *The Planets* (Jupiter), 6 horns in F a6, Allegro giocoso, f: unison melody, then split into
  three-part chords (I–II / III–IV / V–VI).

**Agent applicability**
- Horn solo lines: keep the bulk in MIDI 53–72; reach 76 only soft and rarely.
- High fff notes (≥ ~72 sound): put ≥2 horns on them. Low pp notes (≤ ~45): also ≥2 horns.
- Big tune: all horns unison; lyrical tune: horns in octaves.

## 12.14 Agility (printed p.242–243)

**Rules**
- R50 [PREFER] Agility is not in the horn's nature; the best horn writing still follows natural-horn principles
  and "sounds as if" it could be played on a hand horn (arpeggios, harmonic-series turns, stepwise lines in
  the upper harmonics).
- R51 [AVOID] Fast chromatic runs/figures for horns except for a special effect: effective but not idiomatic.

**Examples**
- Ex. 264 Strauss, *Elektra*, 8 horns in F, Sehr schnell 3/4: I/III and II/IV in fast chromatic triplet
  chords, V–VIII on held chords cresc., then rapid chromatic 16th runs (V, and VI–VIII a3) to an sff chord —
  cited as successful but non-idiomatic.

**Tags:** #orchestration/brass/horn #agility #idiom

## 12.15 Harmonic uses, balance, section size (printed p.244–245)

**Concepts**
- "Blanket of horns": four-part horn harmony as a sustained foundation under the orchestra.

**Rules**
- R52 [era: mid-19th c. – early 20th c.] Four-part horn harmony as a constant foundation was common practice:
  unobtrusive, gives continuity and fullness. Interest in counterpoint and clarity made it unusual by 1955.
- R53 [ALLOWED] Horn harmonic backgrounds can use any figuration or rhythmic division and can be arranged with
  contrapuntal interest and transparency.
- R54 [PREFER] Horns blend with all instruments; they link brass and woodwinds (hence placed above trumpets in
  the score, next to the woodwinds).
- R55 [MUST] Balance: horns are weaker than heavy brass (trumpets, trombones, tuba) and stronger than woodwinds.
  With other brass, place them carefully and sometimes double them to keep balance.
- R56 Section size [era: 1955]: large orchestras have 8 horns; lesser ones 4 or 5. A 5th player ("assistant")
  takes the 1st horn's part in less important bars so the 1st can save himself for solos. Extra players for
  works needing 6 or 8. Eight horns also let players alternate and rest fatigued embouchures.

**Examples**
- Ex. 265 Stravinsky, *Divertimento* (*Le Baiser de la fée*), horns I–II and III–IV in F, Allegretto grazioso
  2/4, p dolce: four-part horn background with independent slurred lines — harmony with contrapuntal interest.

**Agent applicability**
- Pads: a 4-horn chord (1 and 3 on upper notes, 2 and 4 lower) is a valid Romantic foundation; for a modern
  texture make the inner horn lines move contrapuntally instead of sustaining blocks.
- In brass tutti at equal dynamics, a single horn will be covered by a trumpet or trombone; double horns or
  drop the heavy brass dynamic (inferred: about two horns per trumpet/trombone line).
- Long horn-1 parts: plan rests or hand passages to another horn (endurance).
