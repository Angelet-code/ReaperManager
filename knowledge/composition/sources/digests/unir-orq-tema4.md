## 4.2 Common elements (elementos comunes) (scan p.5–7)

**Concepts**
- Strings: the most important orchestral family; homogeneous colour; register changes between members are
  subtler than in other families.
- Each string part is played by a section seated in **desks (atriles) of two**; in divisi each player of a
  desk takes a different line; "unis." returns to unison.
- Open strings (cuerdas al aire): fixed pitch, marked "0" (not to be confused with the natural-harmonic
  circle). Stopped notes (notas pisadas): all others, fingered by the left hand.
- The same pitch can be played on different strings/positions with different tension and colour, e.g. E5:
  open E string, 1st position on A, or 5th position on D (much tenser on D).

**Section sizes**
- Modern symphony orchestra string section: **16 Vln I, 14 Vln II, 12 Va, 10 Vc, 8 Cb**.
- Mannheim / Classical orchestra (Haydn, Mozart, Beethoven): **6–8 Vln I, 4–6 Vln II, 4 Va, 4 Vc, 1–2 Cb**.

**Tunings (open strings, low→high)**
- Violin: G3 D4 A4 E5 (MIDI 55 62 69 76), in fifths.
- Viola: C3 G3 D4 A4 (48 55 62 69), in fifths, a P5 below the violin.
- Cello: C2 G2 D3 A3 (36 43 50 57), in fifths, an octave below the viola.
- Double bass: sounding E1 A1 D2 G2 (28 33 38 43), **in fourths**; written an octave higher (E2 A2 D3 G3).

**Range charts (Fig. 1–4, read from staff figures; extremes approximate)**
- Violin: low G3 (55). Top stopped note shown ≈ B7 (107) under 15ma; top harmonic ≈ D8 (110) [figure
  reading approximate]. (Practical orchestral limits: see apuntes-orq-sesion1: up to ≈ E7.)
- Viola: low C3 (48). Top stopped ≈ D7 (98) and harmonic ≈ G7 (103) under 8va [approximate; orchestral
  practice rarely above A5, see apuntes-orq-sesion1].
- Cello: low C2 (36). Top stopped note shown A5 (81); top harmonic shown under 15ma (≈ A7) [?].
- Double bass: low sounding E1 (28), with optional low C1 (24) in parentheses (5th string/extension).
  Top stopped written G4 → sounds G3 (55); harmonic written ≈ G6 → sounds ≈ G5 (79) [approximate].

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] Know the open strings: they determine the most comfortable keys and which double/triple/quadruple
  stops are possible.
- R2 [NEVER] Expect vibrato on an open string (it is impracticable there); open strings have a slightly
  different timbre, associated with some style periods.

**Agent applicability**
- Hard low limits per track: Vln ≥ 55, Va ≥ 48, Vc ≥ 36, Cb ≥ 28 (≥ 24 only if 5-string/extension patch).
- Prefer keys near open strings (D, G, A, E, C major) for string-heavy, resonant writing.

## 4.3 The bow (el arco) (scan p.7–8)

**Rules**
- R3 [ALLOWED] Bowing closer to the bridge gives a more powerful, brighter sound.
- R4 [ALLOWED] Playing at the tip (a la punta) = less bow weight, lighter; at the frog (al talón) = heavier.
  The difference is very audible with a full orchestral section.
- R5 [ALLOWED] Down-bow (arco abajo, frog→tip) has a natural diminuendo tendency; up-bow (arco arriba,
  tip→frog) a natural crescendo tendency (as stated by the source: pressure increases toward the frog).
- R6 [PREFER] Attack an anacrusis (upbeat) with up-bow and a strong beat with down-bow, where possible.
- R7 [AVOID] Changing bow direction inside long notes, except for very long notes.
- Note: bow directions are mostly marked in solo music, less in orchestral parts.
- Schema also lists sul ponticello – sul tasto (bow position) and jeté, without further text.

**Agent applicability**
- Upbeat notes: slight crescendo shape (CC1 rising) into the downbeat; long held notes: one bow = one
  dynamic gesture; for very long pads, staggered bowing is implied (no audible gap) (inferred).

## 4.4 Pizzicato (scan p.8–10)

**Concepts**
- Pizzicato (pizz.): strings plucked with the fingers until "arco" appears.
- Sound: dull/dry and short, decays fast. Longer, heavier strings sustain better; open strings resonate more;
  stopped pizz can use vibrato.

**Rules**
- R8 [MUST] Leave time to switch arco→pizz; more time is needed after a down-bow than after an up-bow
  (per the source).
- R9 [MUST] Mark "pizz." and "arco" in every part that needs them.
- R10 [AVOID, style] Orchestral pizzicato before the 19th c. was exceptional (too little volume in the
  ensemble); it was more a solo effect. (Example cited: Bach, Magnificat.)
- 20th-c. pizz types (e.g., Bartók pizz) are only named, deferred to another course.

**Examples**
- Ravel, *Le Tombeau de Couperin*, Rigaudon, middle section: pizzicato with string divisi.

**Agent applicability**
- Switching between pizz and arco patches on one string section: insert a rest/gap (≥ about a beat at
  moderate tempo, inferred) between the last arco note and the first pizz note.
- Pizz in low strings sustains longer than in violins; don't expect violin pizz to carry against winds.

**Tags:** #strings/pizzicato #articulation/strings

## 4.5 Articulation (articulación) (scan p.10–14)

**Concepts**
- Two families: bow drawn on the string (frotado) with three main articulations ordered as a progression:
  legato → non legato (détaché) → staccato; and bow struck on the string (golpeado), with an unmistakable
  sound+noise component.
- Legato: no gaps between notes; on strings = several notes in one bow direction under a slur. Without a slur
  each note takes a new bow direction. Example (Fig. 8): 4 slurred 8ths down-bow (G A B C), 4 slurred 8ths
  up-bow (D C B A), final quarter down-bow.
- Non legato / détaché: between legato and staccato; notes slightly separated, lighter, allows **more
  speed**. Probably the most common stroke. Played around the middle or upper third of the bow; at the tip in
  fast passages. **No marking**: no slur and no dot means détaché.
- Staccato: each note fully separated. At moderato tempo and p–mf, the bow is dropped on its middle in a
  semicircular motion and bounces (wrist); also called saltando / spiccato; marked with a dot.

**Rules**
- R11 [PREFER] Fast passages in strings: détaché (separate bows), not legato. *Why:* in Rimsky-Korsakov's
  *Flight of the Bumblebee* the strings are détaché while the winds play legato; string legato would blur at
  that speed (flute is more agile than violin).
- R12 [ALLOWED] Spiccato/staccato for light, bouncing figures at moderate tempo, p–mf (virtuoso example:
  Paganini Caprice 1).

**Agent applicability**
- Patch choice: slurred phrase → legato patch; unslurred moderate/fast notes → détaché/sustain-short;
  dotted notes at moderate tempo, p–mf → spiccato patch.
- Fast runs in strings (16ths at high tempo): use détaché/spiccato, not legato transitions.

## 4.6 Mute (sordina) (scan p.14)

**Rules**
- R13 [ALLOWED] Mute (sordina, sourdine, Dämpfer, sord.): clipped to the bridge, attenuates the sound. More
  common in orchestra than solo; popular only from the early 19th c.
- R14 [MUST] Mark where the mute goes on and where it comes off, leaving the player time to fit and remove it.

**Agent applicability**
- Con sordino patch changes need a rest of a few beats before/after (inferred from R14).

## 4.7 Violin (violín) (scan p.15–21)

**Concepts**
- Fingers 1 (index) to 4 (little); thumb only supports; 0 = open string.
- First position (Fig. 12): G string A3 B3 C4 D4; D string E4 F4 F#4 G4 A4; A string B4 C5 C#5 D5 E5;
  E string F#5 G5 A5 B5. Each higher position moves the hand up a step (Fig. 13: 1st–3rd positions).
- Open vs stopped: open strings have richer harmonics but no finger control of intonation (and no vibrato).
- String characters:
  - E (mi, steel): most brilliant and strongest; vigorous, sometimes strident; ethereal and luminous when
    played softly.
  - A (la, gut or metal): considerably powerful in 1st position; loses brilliance and power higher up.
  - D (re, gut or aluminium-wound gut): the weakest; gentle, grey; contrasts especially with G.
  - G (sol, wound): excellent power; ideal for broad melodies up to 7th–8th position; upper limit about
    **D5** (octave + fifth above open G3).

**Rules**
- R15 [AVOID] Open strings in expressive melodic phrases; [ALLOWED] in fast scales and ornaments.
- R16 [ALLOWED] Broad G-string melodies up to ~D5 (MIDI 74) for a dark, powerful violin colour; melody may
  move from G to D string (Mahler 3/VI example).
- R17 [MUST] Double/triple/quadruple stops: to be sure they are playable, **at least one note should be an open
  string** (source's safety rule). Comfortable keys for multiple stops (related to open strings): D, G, A, E,
  C major.
- R18 [AVOID] Overusing multiple stops in orchestral writing, especially in passages with many accidentals:
  individually possible but the section result gets dirty from accumulated awkward positions.
- R19 [ALLOWED] Use multiple stops as the string equivalent of a piano/guitar/harp arpeggiated chord
  (harmonic effect).
- Violin double-stop possibilities are much larger than for the other strings (Adler chart, Fig. 16: 2nds to
  octaves, many chromatic).
- R20 [MUST] Mark concertmaster solos "solo" to distinguish them from the section (Debussy *Faune* example).

**Orchestral role**
- Until about the 2nd half of the 19th c. the violin dominated the orchestral melody (range + agility); later
  the growing brass mass required more strings to balance.
- 1st and 2nd violins originally seated facing each other (antiphonal) for a "stereo" effect (Beethoven 7/I).

**Examples**
- Fig. 15 typical violin chords (all use open strings; read from figure): C major: D4–B4–G5, G3–D4–B4–G5,
  G3–E4–C5, G3–E4–C5–E5, G3–E4–C5–G5; G major: G4–D5–G5 [approx.], G3–D4–B4–G5, D4–A4–A5;
  A major: A3–E4–C#5–A5, E4–B4–E5.

**Agent applicability**
- Violin triple/quadruple stops in MIDI: include an open-string pitch (G3, D4, A4, E5) and keep adjacent notes
  ≤ about a 6th apart (inferred); in sections prefer divisi over chords.
- Antiphonal seating option: pan Vln I left, Vln II right for call-and-response writing.

## 4.8 Viola (scan p.21–26)

**Concepts**
- Size not standardised (measured in cm); even large violas are acoustically too small for a fifth below the
  violin, which Piston links to its "monochrome" sound; larger = harder in high positions.
- Fingering identical to violin; tuned a P5 lower. Clef: alto (C on 3rd line); treble in high register.
- String characters:
  - A (metal or gut): contrasting; nasal, penetrating, high, unstable.
  - D (gut): discreet and gentle, more powerful than the violin's D; for accompaniment or melodies.
  - G (wound): secondary next to A and C, but richer and warmer than the violin's G.
  - C (wound): the only string below the violin range; Piston: "full of omens and threats".

**Rules**
- R21 [AVOID] Triple/quadruple stops in orchestral viola writing (rare); double stops play a smaller role than
  on violin; use an open string to secure them.
- R22 [PREFER] Write viola double notes in accompaniment as **divisi** for concision and cleanliness
  (in Mozart 40/I the viola 8th-note double notes are implicitly divided, unmarked per period custom).
- R23 [MUST] From the 19th c. mark every divisi and the return with "unis.".
- R24 [ALLOWED] Divisi lightens the viola's heavier sound.

**Orchestral role**
- Traditionally harmonic accompaniment (alto voice of the string choir); increasingly melodic from the early
  19th c. as an alternative to the violin's high sound (Mendelssohn *Scottish*/I).

**Agent applicability**
- Viola filler: repeated 8th-note dyads split into two viola tracks (Va div. a/b) rather than one chord track.
- Use the C string (C3–G3) for dark, ominous colour.

**Tags:** #strings/viola #strings/divisi #texture/accompaniment

## 4.9 Cello (violoncello) (scan p.26–32)

**Concepts**
- An octave below the viola; standard sizes 3/4 or 4/4; held between the knees on an endpin.
- **Chromatic fingering**: the hand spans about a **third** between fingers 1 and 4 (e.g., A string 1st
  position: B3 C4 C#4 D4, with Bb3 extended), so position shifts are more frequent than on violin/viola.
  C major scale is playable in 1st position with open strings.
- Left hand is freer than on violin/viola (it doesn't hold the instrument). From 7th position the thumb leaves
  the back of the neck and can stop notes (thumb position).
- Clefs: bass; tenor for high passages (to save ledger lines); treble only for very high passages.
- Strings: A and D usually gut, G and C wound (metal also used). **A string: expressive and powerful** (most
  melodies); D: soft, discreet, less nasal than A; G and C: little timbral difference between them.
- Pizzicato: especially sonorous and vibrant; good quality up to about an **octave above each open string**.
  Pizz chords are arpeggiated low→high with the thumb unless marked otherwise.

**Rules**
- R25 [ALLOWED] Double stops: most intervals within the octave except seconds (Adler, Fig. 27: open string +
  stopped note, and stopped pairs).
- R26 [MUST] Mark principal-cello solos "solo".
- R27 [PREFER] Cello pizz within an octave above the open strings (C2–C3, G2–G3, D3–D4, A3–A4) for best tone.

**Orchestral role**
- Very versatile: harmonic accompaniment, main or secondary melody, bass. In the Baroque it played the
  continuo bass (with keyboard); after separating from the double bass part (19th c.) it became filler and
  melodic instrument (Brahms 3/III melody).

**Agent applicability**
- Cello melodies: favour the A string (A3–A4 and up) for expressive lines.
- Pizz chords on cello: roll notes bottom→top by a few ms (strum) unless "non arpeggiato" (inferred from R).

**Tags:** #strings/cello #strings/pizzicato #strings/double-stops

## 4.10 Double bass (contrabajo) (scan p.32–40)

**Concepts**
- Supported by the body and left knee; 3/4 size preferred in orchestras.
- 5-string bass reaches low **C1** (MIDI 24); not universal even today, used from the late 19th c.;
  some 4-string basses have a mechanical **C extension** on the E string.
- **Written like the cello but sounds an octave lower.** Until the 19th c. cello and bass shared one staff
  (bass = lower-octave doubling of the cello); separate bass lines (e.g. Mozart "Jupiter"/I) were exceptions
  in the 18th c.
- Hand span: a **major 2nd** between fingers 1 and 4 (fingering chart uses fingers 1, 2, 4 in lower positions:
  e.g. half position on E string: F, F#, G).
- Scales with open strings need fewer shifts. Clefs: bass, tenor, treble (same as cello).
- Bows: German bow (held underhand, like a saw) vs French bow (overhand, like a cello bow).
- All bow strokes possible but the response/sound is much slower.
- Pizzicato used more habitually than in other strings.

**Rules**
- R28 [ALLOWED] Pedal notes are common; players stagger bow changes to avoid gaps in long sustained notes.
- R29 [ALLOWED] Basses often double the cellos' arco line in **pizzicato**.
- R30 [ALLOWED] Double stops: 3rds, 4ths, 5ths, and any interval with an open string; rarely used orchestrally.
- R31 [PREFER] Default role = bass line (octave below cello); independent line from the 19th c.; occasional
  melodies (Beethoven 9/IV recitative; Mahler 1/III "Frère Jacques" solo).

**Agent applicability**
- Bass MIDI is concert pitch: write the sounding note (cello line −12 when doubling at the octave).
- For long held bass notes use a sustain patch without retriggers; for light pizz doubling, copy the cello
  line −12 to a bass pizz track.
- Low notes below E1 (MIDI 28) only with a C-extension/5-string patch (down to MIDI 24).
