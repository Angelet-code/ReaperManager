## I. Generalidades respecto de la Forma §1-8 (scan p007-p009, printed 3-6)

**Concepts**
- Form (forma, estructura, arquitectura; "morfología musical" = study of form) (§1): the organisation of a set of musical ideas; a composer may invent one or adopt an established one.
- Classified types (tipos clasificados) (§4): few prototypes with endless variants that keep their traces.
- Genres (§8): pure (pura) vs dramatic (dramática, tied to words; incl. programmatic, descriptive, impressionist); religious/liturgical/sacred vs profane; vocal / instrumental / vocal-instrumental; a cappella; chamber (cámara) vs concert (concierto, virtuoso or large forces).

**Rules**
- R1 [PREFER] Select ideas: too few ideas and too many ideas are the composer's two enemies; discard unnecessary ideas even if good (§2).
- R2 [ALLOWED] Every formal type has an essential core and a variable remainder that can change freely (§3).
- R3 [AVOID] Expecting modern works to match the Bach-Beethoven moulds: types evolve (§6). The great classical types flourished Bach→Beethoven; earlier vocal polyphony was organised by cantus firmus and text; post-classical music added little formally; modern music tends to "aformalism" or a new form per work (§7).

**Tags:** #form/general #genre

## I. Los elementos formales §9-15 (scan p009-p012, printed 6-12)

**Concepts**
- Rhythm, melody, harmony as elements (§9): rhythm can appear alone; melody can be unaccompanied; rhythm interest can outweigh the pitch line or vice versa; a repeated melody need not (but may) repeat its harmonisation; harmony can be the protagonist.
- Theme (tema) (§10): brief fragment, complete in sense, strong personality, not sectioned by internal cadences; basic element of a work and **subject to later repetition and development** (the decisive criterion — any fragment so treated is called a theme, even a full phrase).
- Motif (motivo) (§10): for the author = the head (cabeza) of the theme, its first elementary group, the generating cell where the theme's profile is most salient. Example (Eb major, 3/4): 2-bar theme, motif = its first beat-group.
- Figure/design (diseño) (§10): a melodic pattern of uniform rhythm that is insisted upon and lacks the precision of a theme (e.g. a 16th-note broken-chord figuration in C).
- Repetition vs transformation (§11): a theme can be repeated (same key or transposed, mode kept or changed) or transformed (modified to change its expression); harmony almost always plays the main role in transformation. Bach WTC Fugue 1: theme reharmonised to fit the modulating process (pure-musical reason); Wagner *Tristan*: leitmotiv transformed for extra-musical meaning.
- Thematic development (desarrollo temático) (§12): extracting elements from a theme to build new ideas. Melodic unfolding (desenvolvimiento melódico): free, unconstrained melodic line, which normally takes shape as phrases (WTC 8: prelude unfolds freely, fugue dominated by theme).
- Phrase (frase) (§13): the complete cycle of a melodic idea, built from partial ideas forming sections of decreasing rank. A phrase can contain the theme; not the reverse.
- Hierarchy (§13): phrase → periods (períodos) → subperiods (subperíodos) → members of subperiod (miembros de subperíodo). Larger: groups of periods (grupos de períodos); smaller: subdivisions of members. Note: other authors call our "period" a "phrase" and vice versa — what matters is where units begin and end.
- Example (F major, 4/4): 8-bar phrase = 2 periods × 4 bars; each period = 2 subperiods × 2 bars; members ≈ 1 bar.
- Ictus (§14): every phrase/period/subperiod is bounded by two strong beats, initial ictus and final ictus; a subperiod member has a single ictus (initial = final). Lower-level ictus don't count at higher levels. (Strong = beat 1; semi-strong = beat 3 in 4/4.)
- Written vs real bar (compás escrito / real) (§15): the written bar may equal 2+ real bars, or ½, ⅓, ¼ of one. Slow quadruple meters often = 2 real bars (Beethoven Sonata 8 op.13: written 4/4, real 2/4); fast binary/ternary often = fractions (Strauss *Blue Danube*: real bar 6/4 = two written 3/4 bars, and the first written bar is an anacrusis).

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] Locate ictus using the real bar, not the written one; use cadences, bass attacks and chord placement to find the real bar (§15).
- R2 [PREFER] Place chord changes on strong beats; prolonging a strong-beat chord into the weak beat is normal, the reverse (attack on weak, hold over strong) is not (§15 fn).
- R3 [ALLOWED] The same melody can have its ictus placed differently by different harmonisations (§15 ex.).

**Agent applicability**
- For a waltz in 3/4, plan phrase lengths in real 6/4 units (2 written bars); a "4-bar subperiod" = 8 written bars.
- Generate themes with a distinctive head (motif) in the first beat-group; derive later material from it.

## I. Rhythmic types, extension, unity/variety §16-18 (scan p012-p013, printed 13-15)

**Concepts**
- Start types (§16): thetic (tético) — attack on initial ictus; anacrustic (anacrúsico) — attack before it (pickup = anacrusa); acephalous (acéfalo) — attack just after it.
- End types (§16): masculine (masculino) — ends on final ictus; feminine (femenino) — continues past the final ictus (most typical: an appoggiatura on the ictus).
- Rhythm-letter analysis (§18): label member/subperiod rhythmic formulas a, b, c…; same letter if the rhythmic formula repeats. Converting masculine↔feminine ending does NOT justify a new letter.

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] Count a unit's length in bars from initial ictus to final ictus; the bar containing the final ictus counts as fully occupied (§17).
- R2 [MUST] Bar 1 of a piece = the bar of the first initial ictus; an opening anacrusis is not counted (§17).
- R3 [MUST] Feminine endings never extend into the next unit's bar; anacrustic starts DO borrow space from the preceding unit's bar (§17 fn). Example: 4/4 subperiods each count as 2 bars though they hold 10 or 6 quarter-values (§17).
- R4 [AVOID] Insisting on one rhythmic formula (→ monotony) or varying every formula (→ incoherence); repeat or vary deliberately (§18). Poverty in one parameter can be compensated by richness in melody/harmony (§18 fn).
- R5 [ALLOWED] Period rhythm schemes (F major 3/4, same pitch line): members a-a-a-a (subperiods A-A); a-b-a-b (A-A); a-b-a-c (A-B); a-b-c-d (A-B) (§18).
- R6 [MUST] A 2-period phrase is labelled A-A if the 2nd period repeats the 1st period's rhythmic formula, else A-B (§18).

**Agent applicability**
- When generating a 4-bar period, choose a member rhythm plan from {aaaa, abab, abac, abcd}; abab or abac are the safest balance of unity and variety.

## I. Suspensive/conclusive formulas and harmony §19-20 (scan p013-p014, printed 15-17)

**Concepts**
- Suspensive / interrogative formula (fórmula suspensiva/interrogativa) (§19a): no sense of absolute end; provisional rest needing continuation. Melodically: **ends on a note NOT in the tonic chord**.
- Conclusive / affirmative formula (conclusiva/afirmativa) (§19b): sense of absolute ending (not necessarily the actual end). Melodically: **ends on the tonic of the main key with masculine rhythm**.
- Indeterminate formula (inconcreta) (§19c): sounds suspensive next to a fully conclusive one and vice versa: ends on the tonic with feminine rhythm, or on another tonic-chord note (3rd or 5th).
- "Tonic" always means the main key's tonic; a tonic reached after modulating has suspensive effect (§19 fn, §24).

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] Melody and harmony may agree or disagree in character (§20).
- R2 [MUST] Harmony decides whether a melodically conclusive phrase stays conclusive: it can be fully conclusive, relatively conclusive or suspensive depending on whether there is a cadence and its type (§20). Example: one F-major 3/4 conclusive melodic period (ends on F) harmonised 4 ways: plenamente conclusivo (authentic V–I, bass C→F), relativamente conclusivo, and two suspensive versions (bass not ending on F root-position tonic; one bass ends on D) (§20).

**Agent applicability**
- To end a period openly: put the last melody note on 2, 4, 6 or 7 (not 1-3-5) or harmonise under a half cadence. To close: melody on 1 on a strong beat + V–I root position.

**Tags:** #harmony/cadence #form/period #melody/ending

## I. Repetition and reexposition §21 (scan p014-p015, printed 17-19)

**Concepts**
- Melodic formulas (rhythm + pitch line) can be repeated at member, subperiod or period level (Verdi *Traviata*: member repeated; Bach violin sonata Adagio: subperiod repeated exactly; Beethoven Sonata 22 op.54 opens with a 24-bar phrase almost all repetitions).
- Exposition / reexposition (exposición / reexposición): a non-immediate repetition; the model is the exposition, each return a reexposition. Both repetitions and reexpositions may be total or partial.

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] Slight modifications in repeats/reexpositions provided the characteristic rhythm and line survive (§21). Commonest: (a) ornamenting the melody or removing accessory notes (harmonic changes don't count); (b) converting suspensive→conclusive or vice versa; (c) change of key or mode; (d) amplifying the tonal process.
- R2 [PREFER] Use reexposition (total or partial) of a period as the main means of making an idea stick — but avoid too many (monotony) (§21).
- R3 [PREFER] Very common: a suspensive first period (ending on a half cadence) immediately repeated as conclusive (Sonata 9 op.14/1 II: 8-bar period) (§21 b).
- R4 [ALLOWED] State a formula, repeat it, then repeat only its last fragment twice (Sonata 1 op.2/1 I: bars 1-2 formula, 3-4 repeat, 5 and 6 repeat its last bit) (§21 fn).
- R5 [ALLOWED] Repeat a period a step away in the opposite mode: Sonata 21 op.53 I, 2nd period (bars 5-8) = 1st a major 2nd lower; 4th period (bars 18-21) = a major 2nd higher; Sonata 23 op.57 I, 2nd period (bars 5-8) = 1st a minor 2nd higher (opposite mode) (§21 c).
- R6 [ALLOWED] Reexpose with amplified tonal process: Sonata 2 op.2/2 II 4th period (bars 13-19) reexposes the 1st conclusively with 3 extra bars, modulating to the keys of degrees II and IV (subdominant function) to reinforce closure (§21 d, §41).

**Examples**
- Sonata 8 op.13 II: 8-bar principal phrase immediately repeated an octave higher with the accompaniment spread over both hands; reexposed at bar 51 with triplet-16th accompaniment — still counts as partial reexposition (§21 fn).

**Agent applicability**
- When a section returns, vary at least one of: ornament, accompaniment figure, register, ending type; keep rhythm profile and contour intact so it is recognised.

**Tags:** #form/repetition #form/reexposition #development/variation

## I. Binary/ternary, period combination, tonal & modulating process §22-25 (scan p015-p017, printed 19-22)

**Concepts**
- Binary (binaria) = 2 main divisions; ternary (ternaria) = 3. Applies independently at each level (a binary period may have ternary subperiods) (§22). Phrases of 4+ periods always resolve into 2 or 3 groups of periods (§22 fn).
- Tonal classes of phrase (§24): (a) begins and ends in main key; (b) does not begin in it but ends in it; (c) begins in it, ends elsewhere; (d) neither.
- Modulating phrase stages (§25): alejamiento (moving away to a target key) and regreso (return). Modulation can be direct or indirect (through other keys).

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] The last period defines the phrase: conclusive phrase ⇔ conclusive last period; other periods combine freely (§23).
- R2 [MUST] Only a phrase ending on the tonic chord of the main key is conclusive; ending in another key is never conclusive, even with an authentic cadence there, because the return is missing (§24). Exception: some popular songs/dances end on the dominant chord (§24 fn).
- R3 [PREFER] Modulate to neighbouring keys (tonos vecinos) (§25). Older composers modulate less; non-modulating phrases are common in early music.
- R4 [PREFER] Classical targets of the "away" stage (§25): (a) the dominant key — major if the main key is major; major or minor if the main key is minor (stopping on the major dominant key's tonic acts as a "superlative half cadence" relative to the main key); (b) the relative major when the main key is minor. The dominant key is the most frequent (§25 fn).
- R5 [PREFER] Strengthen the stages by: (1) establishing the main key before leaving; (2) a digression in the reached key, or continuing to another key; (3) re-establishing the main key at the end of the return (§25).
- R6 [ALLOWED] Stages need not coincide with period/phrase boundaries (§25).

**Schemes (§25)**
- Binary 1: [main key + modulation away] | [return to main key]. Ex. Sonata 8 op.13 II bars 1-8: Ab→Eb | back to Ab.
- Binary 2: [main key only] | [modulation away + immediate return]. Ex. Sonata 3 op.2/3 II: E (ends on half cadence) | f#, B, A, E (twice). Sonata 2 op.2/2 II bars 1-19: D (conclusive) | A, D, e, G, D. Sonata 12 op.26 bars 1-34: Ab (16 bars) | bb, Ab, f, eb, Ab.
- Ternary 3: [main + move away] | [digression/continuation to other key, ± start of return] | [main key]. Ex. Sonata 1 op.2/1 III bars 1-40: f→Ab (14 bars) | Ab, bb, C | f (12 bars, last 9 = coda).
- Ternary 4: [main key only] | [move away ± start of return] | [main key]. Ex. Sonata 9 op.14/1 II bars 1-51: e (15 bars) | C, G, C + start of return | e, a, e (ending in E major).
- Non-modulating ex. Sonata 1 op.2/1 II bars 1-16 (F): subperiod 1 ends bar 2 on half cadence; subperiod 2 (bar 4) closes period 1 with more harmonic motion, wider curve, ending on the chord 5th (not 3rd); subperiod 3 ends on IV (bar 6, first IV after only I and V so far → maximal weight); subperiod 4 = full cadence formula; period 3 (bars 9-12) sits on V, finally V7; period 4 (bars 13-16) repeats period 2 almost exactly.

**Agent applicability**
- Default classical 8-bar major-key phrase: bars 1-4 in I ending on V (HC) or modulating to V; bars 5-8 back to I with PAC. Minor key: aim for III (relative major) or v/V.
- Hold back IV/ii until late in the phrase to give the pre-cadential subdominant maximal weight.

## I. Principal and secondary periods; Intro, Coda, Soldadura §26-28 (scan p017-p018, printed 22-24)

**Concepts**
- Principal vs secondary periods (períodos principales / secundarios) (§26).
- Introduction (Introducción) and Coda (§27): non-essential, removable fragments before (prepare entry) or after (round off) a phrase; from a few notes to extensive; important when attached to a whole form; may be integrated as first/last period or clearly "add-ons" (postizos).
- Weld (soldadura) (§28): filler notes inserted between phrases/periods/subperiods WITHOUT adding bars, to avoid an unwanted rhythmic gap ("frío rítmico"); often looks like an anacrusis. Ex. Sonata 16 op.31/1 II (C, 9/8): a 16th-note run on the last beat linking two subperiods.

**Rules**
- R1 [PREFER] One principal period per phrase, usually the first (§26a).
- R2 [MUST] A period that is reexposed is by definition principal (§26b).
- R3 [MUST] Secondary periods exist for the principal: a principal lacking personality is a defect; a secondary with too much personality is a defect (it outshines the principal) (§26d).
- R4 [ALLOWED] Secondaries may precede (prepare), follow (complete), or mediate between exposition and reexposition as transition or episode (§26f).
- R5 [ALLOWED] Coda: short tonic-affirming tail, e.g. 4-5 bars of repeated cadence on I after a conclusive phrase (§27 ex.).

**Agent applicability**
- If a gap of rest would open between two sections in a continuous texture, fill it with a pickup run in the last beat (soldadura) instead of adding a bar.

**Tags:** #form/coda #form/introduction #form/transition

## I. Delimitation of phrases, periods, subperiods §29 (scan p018-p019, printed 24-26)

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] A masculine ending followed by a thetic start leaves a clear "dead point" marked by rests (unless the final note fills the bar); only rests BETWEEN units mark a dead point (§29).
- R2 [PREFER] Otherwise find boundaries by: (1) mentally converting feminine→masculine endings and anacrustic→thetic starts (skeleton "osatura" with rests); (2) salient points in line or rhythm creating symmetries, repetitions or sequences — where the model restarts, a unit has ended; (3) reexpositions — the start of a reexposition is the start of a unit; (4) cadences — in classical harmonised phrases many periods end with a cadence (some theorists require a cadence for a period); (5) periods of square phrases and many others are 4 real bars (§29).

**Examples**
- Sonata 2 op.2/2 II: phrase 1 cadences at the ends of periods 1, 2, 4 (bars 4, 8, 19); phrase 2 at ends of its 1st and last of three periods (bars 23, 31).
- Sonata 1 op.2/1 II: bar 13 = (varied) reexposition of period 2 → starts period 4; bar 32 = reexposition of phrase → preceding phrase has ended.

**Tags:** #form/analysis #form/period

## I. The 2-period phrase §30 (scan p019-p021, printed 26-28)

**Concepts**
- Shortest binary phrase. Conclusive-phrase types (only conclusive phrases considered until §42):
  1. A suspensive | A conclusive
  2. A conclusive | A conclusive
  3. A suspensive | B conclusive
  4. A conclusive | B conclusive

**Rules**
- R1 [PREFER] Melodic suspensive→conclusive (types 1 or 3) is the most common; if harmonised, period 1 almost always ends on the **dominant chord in root position**, either as a half cadence or by modulating to the dominant key (ex. modulation: Sonata 7 op.10/3 IV bar 4; Sonata 8 op.13 II bar 4) (§30).
- R2 [AVOID] Melodic A-A conclusive-conclusive with the same harmonic type (too poor); less rare if harmony makes the first suspensive and second conclusive. Logically acceptable only if the 2nd period is more conclusive than the 1st (§30 + fn).
- R3 [PREFER] When both periods are conclusive, make the 2nd more conclusive (e.g. ending an octave higher on the tonic / stronger cadence) (§30 ex.4 fn).

**Examples** (F major, 3/4, 8 bars = 4+4, binary periods)
- Ex.A: period 1 suspensive, half cadence on C chord at bar 4.
- Types 1-4 shown with the same pitch material re-rhythmed; ternary periods (3 subperiods) equally possible.

**Agent applicability**
- Default 8-bar theme: 4 bars ending V (HC) + 4 bars ending I (PAC); choose A-A (parallel period) or A-B (contrasting) by whether bars 5-6 restate bars 1-2's rhythm.

**Tags:** #form/period #form/phrase #harmony/half-cadence

## I. The 3-period phrase §31 (scan p020-p021, printed 28-29)

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] Suspensive/conclusive types: 1 s-s-c (ex. Sonata 6 op.10/2 I bars 1-12 [?]); 2 c-s-c (Sonata 5 op.10/1 I bars 1-14 [?]); 3 s-c-c — the 3rd period then acts as a Coda; 4 c-c-c — almost unused (same objection as A-A c-c).
- R2 [ALLOWED] Rhythmic types: A-A-A, A-A-B, A-B-A, A-B-B, A-B-C (§31).
- R3 [MUST] A-A-A or A-B-A whose 3rd period is a reexposition = ternary "Lied" phrase (frase Lied ternaria) (§31, §46).

**Examples**
- Inserting a suspensive A period between the two periods of §30 ex.1/2 yields A-A-A; changing the 3rd to B gives A-A-B; re-rhythming the 2nd gives A-B-B; inserting a suspensive B between gives A-B-A.

## I. The 4-period phrase §32 (scan p021-p023, printed 29-33)

**Concepts**
- Normally binary: [group 1 = periods 1-2] | [group 2 = periods 3-4]; periods here play the role subperiods play in a 2-period phrase. If period 3 is conclusive, it is a ternary conclusive phrase and period 4 acts as Coda.

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] Group types: suspensive group | conclusive group; conclusive group | conclusive group.
- R2 [ALLOWED] Period combinations: one conclusive s-s-s-c; two: s-s-c-c, s-c-s-c, c-s-s-c; three: s-c-c-c, c-s-c-c, c-c-s-c; four: c-c-c-c (§32).
- R3 [MUST] If period 3 is conclusive, period 4 functions as Coda; if period 2 is conclusive it is debatable whether this is one 4-period phrase or two 2-period phrases, especially if period 3 starts with a new rhythmic formula (§32).
- R4 [ALLOWED] Rhythmic types: A-A-A-B, A-A-B-A, A-B-A-A, A-A-B-B, A-B-A-B, A-B-B-A, A-A-B-C, A-B-A-C, A-B-B-C, A-B-C-A, A-B-C-B, A-B-C-C, A-B-C-D.
- R5 [PREFER] Most common = binary Lied (Lied binario): P1 suspensive | P2 conclusive | P3 suspensive | P4 conclusive AND reexpositive (P4 restates P1/P2 material) (§32). Examples: Sonata 1 II bars 1-16; Sonata 2 II bars 1-19; Sonata 4 IV bars 1-16 (all with 2nd & 4th periods conclusive).
- R6 [ALLOWED] Period 3 may be episodic (new material C, suspensive) giving A-B-C-B / A-B-C-A, or a partial reexposition of A in another key, suspensive (A expo susp — A episodic — A partial reexp, other key, susp — A concl & complementary) (§32 ex.3, 5).

**Examples**
- One conclusive period: Sonata 11 op.22 IV bars 1-18; Sonata 18 op.31/3 III bars 1-16; Sonata 30 op.109 III bars 1-16. Three: Sonata 8 III bars 1-17 (P2 repeats P1, P4 repeats ornamented P3; a bass pedal keeps none fully conclusive). Four: Sonata 16 op.31/1 IV bars 1-16.

**Agent applicability**
- 16-bar song/AABA-like theme: bars 1-4 (A, end V), 5-8 (A', end I), 9-12 (B contrast, end V), 13-16 (A return, end I) = Lied binario.

## I. Symmetry, square phrase, asymmetry §34-41 (scan p023-p027, printed 34-42)

**Concepts**
- Symmetric/regular (simétrica/regular) phrase (§34): every period has the same number of bars; a period is symmetric when its subperiods are; a subperiod when its members are. Otherwise asymmetric/irregular. Ex.: symmetric phrase with asymmetric periods: periods of 2+2+3 bars.
- Square phrase (frase cuadrada) (§34bis): binary periods or groups of periods AND binary subperiods. Almost all dances are square. (Some call only the square phrase "regular".)

**Rules**
- R1 [NEVER] A "limping" (coja) phrase: a bar missing or extra without reason; especially serious in dance music where steps/turns must match (§35).
- R2 [PREFER] Deliberate asymmetry is normal and useful to break the monotony of constant bar counts (§35). Means: (a) elision, (b) rhythmic contraction, (c) rhythmic dilatation, (d) echo, (e) appendices, (f) amplifications. Count real bars, not written (§35 fn).
- R3 [ALLOWED] Elision / double function (doble función por elipsis) (§36): the initial ictus of a unit coincides with the final ictus of the previous → one bar fewer. Very frequent. Ex. Sonata 2 op.2/2 II bar 57: phrase end = start of the minor-mode reexposition of the principal phrase.
- R4 [ALLOWED] Rhythmic contraction (contracción) (§37): note values reduced so two bars fuse into one (Sonata 3 op.2/3 II bar 10, combined with elision in the bass; also bar 53). May change metric accentuation.
- R5 [ALLOWED] Rhythmic dilatation (dilatación) (§38): values lengthened so a unit gains bars. Sonata 1 op.2/1 I bar 47: dilatation at the end of the exposition gives rotundity.
- R6 [ALLOWED] Echo (eco) (§39): immediate repetition of a note group (same or other octave). Causes asymmetry only when it adds bars AND is not done in every period; very short echoes need not add bars. Ex. Sonata 18 op.31/3 bar 33.
- R7 [PREFER] Appendices (apéndices) (§40): supplementary fragments rounding a period's tonal function — dominant (making the move to tonic more desired) or tonic (affirming it). A Coda is an appendix affirming the tonic. Appendices that form whole periods of equal length don't cause asymmetry. Ex. Sonata 2 op.2/2 II: V reached bar 64, principal reexposed bar 68 — bars 64-67 intensify V to prepare the return; last 5 bars = conclusive Coda.
- R8 [PREFER] Prepare reexpositions by prolonging the dominant (classical practice) unless a sudden reexposition (reexposición súbita) is intended (§40 fn).
- R9 [ALLOWED] Amplification (amplificación) (§41): enlarge a period/subperiod by modulations, repetitions or sequences (progresiones) for expression; very frequent, especially when reexposing phrases.

**Examples (§41)**
- Sonata 2 op.2/2 II principal phrase: A suspensive (4 bars) — B suspensive (4) — A reexpositive & conclusive (6 bars), amplified by touching keys of II and IV (e→G→D) for stronger closure than a literal return.
- Sonata 14 op.27/2 II bars 25-36: double period reexposed 4 bars longer by repeating a subperiod twice.
- Sonata 4 op.7 III: bars 1-12 = 3 periods × 4 bars; the 4th period is amplified by insisting on a figure, like a parenthesis.
- Sonata 12 op.26 I bar 23: a deceptive (rota) modulating cadence extends the period by 2 bars.

**Agent applicability**
- Keep dance/groove music square (4+4, 8+8). In lyrical/classical pieces, extend a returning phrase by 2 bars via a deceptive cadence or a detour through ii/IV before the final cadence.
- Before a recapitulation, prolong V for 2-4 bars (dominant appendix).

## I. The phrase as part of a composition §42 (scan p027-p030, printed 42-48)

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] Everything said for periods within a phrase applies to phrases within a composition (§42).
- R2 [AVOID] Conclusive phrases late in a piece ("closed door, motor stopped") risk sounding final and, if the end is delayed, cause fatigue; after one, keep momentum (§42.1). Ex. Sonata 8 op.13 II: principal 8-bar conclusive phrase repeated an octave higher; reexposed bars 29-36 without repetition and continued with an anacrusis + change of mode + faster accompaniment to avoid a void.
- R3 [PREFER] Suspensive phrases ("open door, motor running"): ending on a tonic chord of a non-main key makes the listener expect return; ending on a non-tonic chord makes the listener want to move on (§42.2). Secondary phrases between returns of the principal should be suspensive and end on V7 of the main key (classical norm: reach the dominant key, affirm its tonic, then turn it into V7 of the main key, which resolves into the reexposition) (op.13 II, bars 23 and 48; dominant appendix bars 23-28).
- R4 [PREFER] Near the end of a suspensive phrase, change the rhythmic/melodic sign to introduce the germ of the next phrase (§42.3). Ex. Sonata 1 op.2/1 I: bars 1-10 on the opening 2-bar rhythmic formula, half cadence bar 8 marks the centre; bar 11 the triplet-16th ending becomes protagonist; bar 15 radical change and new element; next phrase begins on the last note of bar 20.
- R5 [PREFER] Secondary phrases serve principals by **cohesion** (derive from principal's melodic/rhythmic elements in other keys; Sonata 3 III bars 17-19) or **contrast** (sudden key change "shadow-light", mode change — Sonata 3 II bar 11, Sonata 8 II bar 37; new rhythmic formulas — Sonata 20 op.49/2 II bars 21-36; vague improvisatory contour — Sonata 8 II bars 17-23) (§42.4).
- R6 [PREFER] Alternate rest and motion: principal phrases tonally stable (only brief intratonal touches), secondary phrases modulating (§42.5). Ex. Sonata 1 II: principal bars 1-16 stays in F; secondary starts in d (relative) → C. Sonata 2 II: principal in D touches e, G; secondary starts in b → F#, G, reaches V7 of D → reexposition. Sonata 8 II: principal Ab (touching Eb); 1st secondary (bar 17) f → Eb; 2nd (bar 37) ab → E (=Fb, subdominant function) → V7 of Ab.
- R7 [ALLOWED] A phrase may contain one or more themes; its elements can become thematic material and be developed (theme engenders phrase: Beethoven 5th Symphony opening 4 notes; Sonata 4 op.7 opening 4-bar rhythmic formula; Sonata 17 op.31/2: 2-bar dominant arpeggio before each period becomes the basis of phrase 2) (§42.6).
- R8 [ALLOWED] Phrases may be reduced, truncated or merely hinted; kept fragments join a new phrase or stand alone as "no-man's-land" connecting passages (= puente/bridge) (§42.7). Exs: Sonata 6 op.10/2 I bar 138 principal reexposed minus its first 4 bars; Sonata 8 III bar 121 reduced to first 2 periods; Sonata 6 II bars 119-124: 6-bar fragment links Db to the f reexposition; Sonata 5 op.10/1 II bar 45: a single V7 chord links the Eb ending to the Ab reexposition.
- R9 [PREFER] Codas are far more common than introductions; long codas are often chains of short fragments each of which could end the piece (Sonata 3 III: 23-bar coda). Slow introductions often precede an Allegro and vice versa (Sonata 8 op.13: 10-bar Grave with an important thematic idea; Sonata 14: first 5 bars intro; Sonata 24 op.78: 4-bar slow intro). Modern tendency: short, sudden endings (§42.8).
- R10 [PREFER] Delimit suspensive phrases that "bite each other's tail" by changes of rhythmic/melodic sign (§42.9). Sonata 1 op.2/1 I sonata landmarks: bar 9 bridge (puente), 21 theme B, 49 development, 101 recapitulation.
- R11 [ALLOWED] In polyphonic writing, themes develop without forming phrases (overlapping entries prevent coincident cadences); then "parts/sections" mark the stages (§42.10).

**Agent applicability**
- In a multi-section arrangement: principal sections stay in the home key; contrasting sections modulate and end on V7 of home key; re-enter with the principal.

## I. Small formal types §43-46bis (scan p030-p032, printed 48-52)

**Concepts**
- Small formal types (pequeños tipos formales) (§43): short pieces of ≤3 parts without a traditional title; built on the phrase, not on the theme.
- Small primary type (pequeño tipo primario) (§44): a single phrase; brevity compensated by repetitions (strophic songs with changing verses, dance figures). Intro/Coda optional.
- Small binary type (§45): any structure of the 2-period / 2-group phrase applied to phrases/sections.
- Small ternary type (§46): combinations of §§31/33 applied to sections: A-B-A, A-A-B, A-B-B, A-B-C.
- Large formal types (§46bis): extensive works; theme as important as phrase; thematic development. Basic types: (a) binary from A-B; (b) binary or ternary with one reexposition (Lied §§31-32, 155, 157; Sonata §§144-153); (c) several reexpositions alternating with free/episodic sections (Rondo §§127, 161). Riemann's four: I binary Lied; II ternary Lied (= minor rondo); III major rondo forms; IV sonata.

**Rules**
- R1 [NEVER] In binary A-A, the second A must not be an exact repeat (that is a repeated primary type); modify rhythm, line, harmony or tonal process (§45).
- R2 [MUST] Binary A-B: part 1 = A ending suspensive or conclusive; part 2 = B in the same or opposite mode, ending conclusive (§45). Song form copla (verse = secondary) + estribillo (refrain = principal) fits here (§223).
- R3 [MUST] Binary reexpositive (Lied binario): reexposition must be PARTIAL (one period of A). Part 1: exposition of A, suspensive or conclusive at will. Part 2: B + reexposition of one period of A, ending conclusive (same or different from part 1's end; if changed, choose a more conclusive turn in melody, harmony or both) (§45 + fn).
- R4 [MUST] Ternary reexpositive (Lied ternario, preferred A-B-A): Part 1 exposition of A (principal idea), suspensive or conclusive; Part 2 B = episode with own or A-derived elements; Part 3 reexposition of A, conclusive. Total reexposition allowed only here (§46).
- R5 [AVOID] Making B too short or too long: the reexposition arriving too early or too late unbalances the form (§46 fn). B may legitimately be half A's length when it is a mere episode leading to the reexposition.
- R6 [ALLOWED] B may be A's phrase transposed to another key (A-B-A by tonality) (§46). Try keys whose tonic chord shares a note with the home tonic, for brightening/darkening effects (§46 fn).
- R7 [ALLOWED] Strengthen the final cadence of a reexposed period by a touch of the subdominant key (e.g. a minor in C) (§45 ex. Schumann 16; cf. §41).

**Examples** (Schumann *Album for the Young*)
- No.5: tiny Lied binario — A ends beat 2 of bar 8; B to mid-bar 12; textual reexposition of A's 2nd period to beat 2 of bar 16.
- No.16: last 4 bars of reexposed period varied, modulating to a minor (subdominant key) for stronger close. No.21: the period's ending is lengthened so only its opening remains.
- No.6 [?]: A-B-A, 8 bars per section; B suspensive, new idea built on A's opening notes A-D-E; reexposition differs only in melody moved to LH. No.12: same, 24 bars per section, each itself A-B-A. Nos.3, 8: B = same phrase in another key.
- No.28: A = binary suspensive irregular phrase (bar 8 cadence deliberately modulating, letting the subperiod repeat, expected cadence at bar 10 in the dominant key); in the reexposition (bar 15) the phrase becomes regular with a new final subperiod to end in the home key.
- No.36: 2-bar intro + 4-bar coda; coda uses intro and A elements.

**Agent applicability**
- Short piece template (Lied ternario): A 8 bars (I→V or I→I) | B 4-8 bars (contrast key/mode, end on V7) | A 8 bars ending PAC; optional 2-bar intro and 2-4 bar coda reusing intro material.
- When A returns after B, rewrite its last subperiod so it closes in the home key.

## II. Imitation and Canon §47-52bis (scan p032-p034, printed 53-57)

**Concepts**
- Imitation (imitación) (§47): one part reproduces a melody another part stated earlier; a writing style, not a formal type (peak: J. S. Bach; revalued in dodecaphony).
- Antecedent (antecedente, propuesta, dux) and consequent (consecuente, respuesta, comes); several consequents numbered 1, 2… (§48).
- Classes (§50): (a) direct motion; (b) contrary motion (inversion); (c) retrograde direct (cancrizante, "crab"; also "mirror"); (d) retrograde contrary (retrograde inversion); (e) augmentation; (f) diminution. Aug/dim combine with contrary and retrograde.
- Canon (§52): imitation sustained at length, especially throughout a piece (formerly called "fuga"). Infinite canon (repeats identically) vs finite (ends freely). Open canon (all parts written), closed canon (only the antecedent + signs), enigmatic canon (puzzle) (§52bis).

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] Imitation length and time distance are free; the closer the entry, the "tighter" (más cerrada/estrecha) and more interesting (§49.1-2).
- R2 [ALLOWED] Any interval; unspecified = same notes (unison, P8, P15); otherwise state it ("at the 5th above", "at the 2nd below") (§49.3).
- R3 [MUST] Textual (strict) imitation preserves interval quality (M2→M2, m6→m6); answering M2 with m2 is free/inexact imitation (§49.4).
- R4 [ALLOWED] Imitate pitch line only (imitation with different values) or rhythm only (rhythmic imitation) (§49.5); imitating parts may be alone or with free parts.
- R5 [PREFER] Direct-motion imitation is most used; retrograde least (hard to hear, interests the reader more than the listener). Rhythmic imitation remains one of the most fertile bases of thematic development (§51).

**Examples**
- Schumann Album No.27: canon at the octave, parts swap roles at bar 13 and back by bar 22 (two bars suppressed). "Volga Boatmen" shown as closed canon at the octave below.

**Agent applicability**
- Cheap cohesion device: answer a 1-2 bar riff in another instrument 1-2 beats later at the octave or 5th (direct motion); use rhythmic imitation of the motif in drums/bass.

**Tags:** #counterpoint/imitation #counterpoint/canon

## II. The Fugue and its particulars §53-72 (scan p034-p044, printed 57-76)

**Concepts**
- Fugue (fuga) (§53): one-movement polyphonic piece for a fixed number of real parts, built on insistent repetition and imitation of a theme with free passages between. Origins (§54): 16th-c. tiento, canzona, ricercare, capriccio, fantasia, motet; heyday mid-17th c. to Bach (§55). School fugue (fuga de escuela/escolástica: strict plan) vs free fugue (fuga libre) (§56).
- Subject (sujeto; dux, tema, guía) (§57); answer (respuesta; comes) — real (exact transposition at P5 above) or tonal (some notes at 5th, some at 4th above; the differences are mutations, mutaciones) (§59).
- Countersubject (contrasujeto) (§61): counterpoint accompanying S/R above or below; with the answer it can be called contrarrespuesta. With a real answer it is transposed up a P5; with a tonal answer it can take mutations.
- Entries (entradas) (§64): every statement of S or R.
- Episodes (episodios, divertimentos) vs codettas (codas, conducción, enlace) (§66): free passages between entries — episode if of some length, coda if a short appendix to S/R.
- Stretto (estrecho) (§69): an entry starts while another of S/R is still sounding.

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] Every fugue begins with the subject (§57).
- R2 [MUST] Subject ends on tonic, dominant or their thirds; [PREFER] begins on tonic or dominant (WTC: all except II/13, II/21) (§57).
- R3 [PREFER] First subject entry alone (more relief; all WTC subjects) (§58).
- R4 [PREFER] Answer enters (a) on the subject's last note or (b) after it ends; (c) before it ends = a stretto, now avoided in the exposition (reserve stretti for later) (§60). WTC 4(I) real answer type a; 11(I) tonal type b; 19(I) tonal type c.
- R5 [ALLOWED] 0 to (real parts − 1) countersubjects (§62).
- R6 [MUST] If the first subject entry has no CS, the CS first appears with the answer, in the part that just stated the subject (§63).
- R7 [PREFER] A CS starts together with or after S/R; starting before is very exceptional (§63). Several CS may enter all at once or one per entry; a later CS can replace an earlier one (WTC 23(II) bar 28).
- R8 [ALLOWED] Entries may differ from the first statement in: starting beat (beat 3 vs 1 in 4/4), first-note value, last note, small details; Bach alters e.g. 3rd or 6th note for harmonic reasons (§64).
- R9 [MUST] Exposition keys: first S in the main key, first R in the dominant key; later entries may be in other keys (§65). The key of an entry does not fix its harmonisation (an answer "in G" may be harmonised in c) (§65).
- R10 [PREFER] Episode material derives from S, R, CS or codettas (may be new), developed mainly by sequences (progresiones), combining elements, inversion, retrograde, aug/dim (§67-68).
- R11 [ALLOWED] Stretto variants (§69): normal/direct (S then R at original interval), inverted (R then S), free (others); S-S or R-R at the octave; weak-against-strong beat placement accepted; 3+ entries equidistant or not (WTC 22(I): 5 equidistant entries); slight alteration of S/R to make it fit; interrupt S/R and continue freely; head-only stretti; with inversion/aug/dim; CS can also be in stretto.
- R12 [ALLOWED] Stretti are optional (absent in WTC I nos. 2, 3, 7, 9, 10, 12, 13, 14, 17, 18, 21, 22, 24) (§69i).
- R13 [PREFER] Cadences: a fugue should feel continuous; Bach uses cadences sparingly — occasionally a full cadence in all parts (WTC 1(I) bar 13), more often cadencing only in the outer voices while inner voices continue (bar 19) (§70).
- R14 [ALLOWED] Temporarily reduce or increase the number of parts; add free filler parts (more parts at the final bars of WTC I 2, 3, 6, 10; fewer in 3 bars 35-43, 7 bars 17-20, 12 bars 16-27) (§71).
- R15 [MUST] Formal plan: sections are real but not marked by cadences or new themes; only the 1st section has a fixed structure; many fugues have 3 sections, others only a free 2nd section (§72).

**Agent applicability**
- For fugato textures: subject in tonic, answer at the 5th (dominant), countersubject continuing in the first voice; keep internal cadences off at least one voice so motion never stops.

## II. Fugue 1st section — Exposition §73-77 (scan p044-p046, printed 76-81)

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] Exposition (exposición) presents S, R and (if any) CS; establishes the main key (via S) and dominant key (via R) (§73).
- R2 [MUST] Each part states S or R at least once: minimum entries = number of real parts; maximum free (school fugues demand 4 entries even in 2-3 part fugues, §97) (§74a).
- R3 [ALLOWED] If extra entries follow the obligatory ones after a substantial episode, they form a counter-exposition (contraexposición) (§74).
- R4 [MUST] First two entries are S then R; normal order with 4 entries S-R-S-R; exceptional S-R-S-S, S-R-R-R, S-R-R-S (WTC 1(I) S-R-R-S; 12, 14 S-R-S-S) (§74b).
- R5 [MUST] No part enters a second time before all parts have entered (§74c).
- R6 [MUST] Key and mode of S and R stay invariable within the exposition (§74d).
- R7 [ALLOWED] Entries successive, equidistant or not; separable by codettas or episodes (§74e,g).
- R8 [NEVER] Present a CS alone — it always accompanies S or R (§74f).
- R9 [MUST] When S enters alone: after its entry each part (except the last) continues with CS1, CS2… then free; the last part to enter takes no CS, the penultimate can take 1, the antepenultimate 2, etc. (§75). Scheme 4 parts / 3 CS: P1 S C1 C2 C3; P2 R C1 C2; P3 S C1; P4 R. Ex. WTC 12(I): tenor S (ends bar 4) + codetta → CS1; alto R bar 4; bass S bar 7; soprano 4th entry bar 13; CS lines cross.
- R10 [PREFER] When S is accompanied by CS from the start, a part that entered with a CS does not usually move straight to S/R (§76).
- R11 [AVOID] Entries by inversion/aug/dim in the exposition — legal but rare (none aug/dim in WTC expositions; WTC 3(II) has an inverted entry; 15(I) supplementary inverted entries in the same keys) (§77).

**Agent applicability**
- 4-voice exposition plan: S (I) in voice 1 → R (V) in voice 2 while voice 1 plays CS → S (I) in voice 3 → R (V) in voice 4; insert a 1-2 bar codetta if the answer needs to return to the tonic.

## II. Fugue 2nd and 3rd sections §78-83 (scan p046-p049, printed 81-87)

**Concepts**
- 2nd section (also called development, episode, divertimento) types (§78): (1) tonal expansion — S/R in a cycle of keys, nearly always neighbours of the main key, different from the exposition's (most common); (2) no expansion — entries in the original keys (old type); (3) mixed (common in WTC). Variants: (a) S/R in original form, no stretto; (b) aug/dim/inversion/stretto.
- 3rd section (conclusión) (§81): return to and reaffirmation of the main key; clearly exists only when the 2nd section is type 1.

**Rules**
- R1 [PREFER] Stay within neighbouring keys in the development (Beethoven quoting an "old rule"; Bach occasionally goes farther) (§78 fn).
- R2 [ALLOWED] An episode often, not always, separates the exposition's last entry from the 2nd section's first; between later entries there is complete freedom (§79). The episode becomes indispensable for marking where the 2nd section starts when the new entry shows no key/mode difference from the corresponding exposition entry (fn cites "type 1.a) §78" [as printed, ?]); without it the entry would count as one more exposition entry. Some theorists assign this episode to the exposition; the author assigns it to the 2nd section.
- R3 [ALLOWED] CS in the 2nd section: keep all, some or none; usually dropped in stretti; new CS allowed; any CS may be in stretto (§80).
- R4 [PREFER] 3rd section: S/R entries back in original keys (other keys may be interleaved), or merely an episode reaffirming the key; stretto common but optional in free fugue (obligatory in school fugue, §96) (§81).
- R5 [ALLOWED] Episodes in the 3rd section optional; in type-2 fugues a 3rd section exists only if an episode separates it from the 2nd (§82).
- R6 [PREFER] End over a (usually bass) tonic pedal; a dominant pedal may come shortly before (§83). WTC I tonic pedal endings: 1, 2, 4, 6, 7, 15, 18 (bar 37), 20; dominant pedals: 1 (bar 21), 4 (bar 105), 14 (bar 37).

**Examples**
- WTC 10(I) 2nd section key cycle: relative (G), answer in VII natural (D), subdominant (a)… WTC 3(I) (C#): R in relative a# (bar 14), S in mediant e# (bar 19), dominant g# (bar 24), tonic c# (bar 26).
- WTC 3(I): extended episode before 3rd section (bars 28-42); WTC 17(II): brief episode bars 38-41, then S-R stretto in original keys.

**Agent applicability**
- Fugato key plan: Exposition I/V → middle entries in vi, IV, ii (or III, v in minor) separated by sequential episodes → final entries in I, ideally in stretto, over V pedal then I pedal.

**Tags:** #counterpoint/fugue #form/fugue/development #harmony/pedal

## II. School fugue: subject and answer §84-87 (scan p049-p050, printed 87-89; continues)

**Rules — subject (§84)**
- R1 [AVOID] Subjects too short (few resources) or too long (hard to remember).
- R2 [MUST] Give the subject rhythmic or melodic personality, especially its head, without becoming a sampler of rhythms and intervals.
- R3 [MUST] Clear key and mode, suitable for clear harmonisation.
- R4 [MUST] Begin on tonic or dominant; end on them or on degree III or VII (not the leading tone in minor). (Some schools allow starting on III.)
- R5 [NEVER] Sound finished before its end.
- R6 [MUST] Vocal subjects: range ≤ an octave (12 semitones), preferably less, in a comfortable register.
- R7 [ALLOWED] If it modulates to a key other than the dominant, only to a neighbour and only in passing; modulating to the dominant is very common and the only one many schools accept.
- R8 [MUST] It should allow stretto with the answer.

**Rules — answer (§85-87)**
- R9 [MUST] The difficulty of the answer is placing the mutation(s) in tonal answers; rules divide into basic (which subject notes are answered by which) and auxiliary (where to mutate) (§85).
- R10 [PREFER] School fugue: the subject's features decide real vs tonal answer; tonal answer arose to affirm the main key; free/modern fugues often prefer real answers (faithful copy) (§85bis).
- R11 [MUST] A mutation changes one answer note to the note above or below what strict imitation gives; allowed only if no ascending interval becomes descending or vice versa (§86). Ex. (C major): subject C–D… answered G–G (unison against a 2nd) = correct; answering an ascending semitone with a descending semitone = incorrect.
- R12 [MUST] Tonic/dominant exchange (§87a): if the subject begins or ends on the tonic, the answer does so on the dominant, and vice versa (subject in C starting C → answer starts G; subject starting G → answer starts C). (Original rigid rule: every tonic in S ↔ dominant in R, softened because it deformed melodies.)
- (§87b continues in next range: subject not beginning on the tonic but with the tonic before the end of its head…)

**Agent applicability**
- Generating a fugue subject: 1-3 bars, ≤ octave range, start on 1 or 5, end on 1, 3, 5 or 7 (major), distinctive head rhythm; check it can be overlapped with its own answer.
- Tonal answer: swap scale degree 1↔5 at the subject's start/end; keep every interval's direction.
