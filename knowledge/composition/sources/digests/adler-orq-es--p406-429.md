## Brass as an independent contrapuntal voice — end (scan p.406–411, printed p.407–412)

**Examples**
- Ex. 11-22 (tail, printed p.407), Bartók, *Concerto for Orchestra* I, mm. 379–387 (brass alone in mm. 316–339
  and 380–387 per the caption on printed p.403): a brass fugato in close imitation — 3 C trumpets, 3 trombones +
  tuba, horns 1+3 a2 — each line marked with accents/tenuto, all `cresc.` together. Under it the strings sneak in
  `p cresc.` with non-divisi tremolo double stops (vln 2, then vc, then vla `mf cresc.`, then db `f`→`ff`), so a
  string tremolo bed grows beneath the brass counterpoint; at m. 386 woodwinds a2/a3 + strings burst in `ff`
  with a fast unison upward run and horns 2+4 add a sustained `f cresc.` dyad: the brass fugato is handed over to
  the tutti.
- Ex. 11-23, Schoenberg, *Five Pieces for Orchestra* op. 16, No. 1 "Vorgefühle", mm. 57–77 (Molto allegro,
  printed p.409–412). Huge orchestra (2 picc, 2 fl, 3 ob, EH, 2 A cl, D cl, bass cl, A contrabass cl, 3 bsn,
  cbsn, 4 hn, 3 tpt, 4 trb, bass tuba, xylophone, timpani, strings with divided vla/vc).

**Concepts**
- Muted but loud: in this passage every brass instrument plays with straight mute (sordina recta,
  *mit Dämpfer*) yet is expected to play loudly; the only soft spot is the `ffpp` at m. 73.
- Flutter-tongue (frullato, *Flatterzunge*): rolled-tongue tremolo on a wind/brass note.

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] Straight-muted brass at `f`–`fff`: a mute changes colour, not only volume; Schoenberg asks the
  muted bass tuba to play "as loud as possible" (*so stark wie möglich*, `fff`). *Why:* gives a pinched,
  nasal, aggressive brass colour that still cuts.
- R2 [PREFER] In dense counterpoint give trombones, trumpets and woodwinds genuinely independent lines that
  interact, with horns adding separate exuberant calls (rising accented gestures) rather than doubling.
- R3 [ALLOWED] End a long contrapuntal build with a sudden new colour: Schoenberg closes with flutter-tongue
  (bassoons, trombones 3–4, tuba), which surprises after so much counterpoint.
- R4 [PREFER] Give significant material to normally neglected instruments (tuba, double bass, contrabassoon,
  even mandolin), as Schoenberg does. *Why:* fresh colours and a stronger bass register.

**Examples** (what the score shows)
- m. 57: 4 horns "alle 4 mit Dämpfer"; trumpets 1–2 and trombones 3–4 sustain long notes; bass tuba muted `fff`;
  violins/violas `ff` angular lines; cellos in 4 parts with the 2nd desk pizzicato (*der II. Sp. pizz.*) in an
  eighth-note ostinato; double basses later divided in 3 (*3 fach get.*) `ff`.
- m. 69: trombones 1–2 muted `ff` in rapid 32nd-note figures; trumpets 1–2 muted zu 2 accented stabs; xylophone `ff`.
- m. 73: trombones 3–4 and bass tuba muted `ffpp` then "always pp" (*immer pp*), timpani `fpp` with hairpins
  under a woodwind/strings swirl; then "swelling quickly" (*rasch anschwellend*) to `fff` and flutter-tongue on
  the final chord, with a ritardando.
- Further study (one line): Schoenberg, *Gurrelieder*, *Erwartung*, complete *Five Pieces*.

**Agent applicability**
- Muted brass ≠ quiet brass: when a library has straight-mute patches, they may be driven to high CC1 for a
  biting colour in modernist/tense cues.
- Flutter-tongue is a "closing colour" — use a flutter patch (or fast 32nd repeated notes as a fallback,
  inferred) on the last sustained chord of a dense section, not throughout.
- A low brass `ffpp` sustain (sharp attack, instant drop to pp) under busy upper parts = velocity high, CC1
  dropping to ~20–30 within ~100–200 ms (inferred).

## Building climaxes with the brass (El clímax sonoro en la sección de metal) (scan p.412–422, printed p.413–423)

**Concepts**
- Brass climax (clímax sonoro): using the brass to drive a musical climax is universal; Adler gives three
  techniques: (1) reserving means, (2) alternating orchestral sections, (3) repetition.
- Reservation of means (reserva de medios): withholding certain instruments/timbres so that they sound new
  when finally introduced at a chosen moment.
- Alternation of sections (alternancia de secciones orquestales): brass and another section take turns
  stating/answering material; the climax itself may be carried by others.
- Climax by repetition (mediante la repetición): reiterating a figure/theme with growing intensity and
  added layers.

### 1. Reserving means (scan p.412–415, printed p.413–416)

**Rules**
- R5 [PREFER] Hold back an instrument group (e.g. trombones) for most of a movement — using it only to double
  lines or hold chords — and then give it a melodic statement of its own at a structurally important point.
  *Why:* the new timbre gives the theme a new tint.
- R6 [ALLOWED] The reserved entry can be `pp`, not loud: Schubert's trombones enter `pianissimo` in a very
  quiet moment, adding an aura of mystery.
- R7 [PREFER] Re-use the reserved colour at the corresponding formal point (Schubert repeats the trombone
  statement at the end of the recapitulation). *Why:* it clarifies the form (sonata form here).

**Examples**
- Ex. 11-24, Schubert, *Symphony No. 9* ("Great" C major) I, mm. 198–232 (Allegro, printed p.413–416):
  alto+tenor trombone a2 with bass trombone an octave below, `pp` with diminuendo hairpins, state the closing
  theme of the exposition in half/whole notes; strings `pp` answer with a slurred eighth-note triplet figure
  (vln 2, vla) and detached quarters (vln 1, vc, db), woodwinds on off-beat chords. The theme repeats on
  rising degrees; from m. 210–216 everything is marked *cresc. poco a poco*; horns join with a held dyad
  (m. 220); m. 222+ horns repeat quarter-note chords while trombones continue the theme; m. 228 `ff` tutti:
  2 horns and 2 trumpets a2 on a pedal of repeated quarters, trombones on the theme, timpani roll, violins in
  octaves, woodwinds in sustained chords. Shows: reserved timbre → long crescendo → full-brass arrival.
  [era: early-19th-c. orchestra: alto/tenor/bass trombones, 2 natural horns and 2 natural trumpets in C]

### 2. Alternating sections (scan p.415–417, printed p.416–418)

**Rules**
- R8 [ALLOWED] Let the brass alternate phrases with other sections; the climax can then be carried mainly by
  strings and woodwinds.
- R9 [ALLOWED] Treat horns as members of the woodwind section in such alternations (Franck does).
- R10 [era: 19th-c. French practice] Writing for both trumpets and cornets (cornets à pistons) in the same
  score is typically French (Franck). Today trumpets normally play all these parts. For a period French sound,
  split the brass phrase between trumpet (top line) and cornets (inner harmony).

**Examples**
- Ex. 11-25, Franck, *Symphony in D minor* III, mm. 74–87 (Allegro, printed p.417–418): F trumpet carries a
  slurred melody in half/quarter notes, two A cornets harmonise it in 3rds/6ths beneath, trombone 3 + tuba
  sustain a low bass line; then woodwinds (EH, cl, bass cl, bsn) + strings answer `p molto espress.` with the
  violins doubled by clarinet; the brass phrase returns `mf`; at m. 81 woodwinds a2, horns and all strings take
  the material `f molto sostenuto` with swelling hairpins while the brass phrase recurs between. Shows:
  call-and-response between a brass choir and a woodwind+horn+string choir, intensifying each turn.

### 3. Repetition (scan p.417–422, printed p.418–423)

**Rules**
- R11 [ALLOWED] Build a climax by repeating a memorable figure (Tchaikovsky reintroduces the opening trumpet
  fanfare a third time at `fff`). Adler notes Tchaikovsky is often criticised for over-using this, yet calls
  him a master of climax building. *Why:* recognition plus added intensity.
- R12 [PREFER] On each repetition add or renew something: Tchaikovsky gives the strings a new, agitated,
  ornamented version of the woodwinds' top line under the repeated fanfare.
- R13 [PREFER] After a `fff` plateau, break the texture and thin it out (strings fade until only cellos and
  basses are working), then re-enter suddenly with a large tutti. *Why:* the drop makes the next arrival hit
  harder.
- R14 [PREFER] At that tutti arrival add a new, powerful thematic element in the trombones: a slower melody
  that sounds like a cantus firmus against the faster fragments already heard (here in woodwinds and high
  strings), with the rest of the brass supporting and a timpani roll underneath.

**Examples**
- Ex. 11-26, Tchaikovsky, *Symphony No. 4* I, mm. 278–290 (Moderato con anima, 9/8, printed p.419–423):
  - m. 278–281: woodwinds, horns a2 and trombones/tuba on `fff` dotted-quarter chords (one per beat); trumpets
    notated in 3/4 against the orchestra's 9/8, playing the fanfare rhythm (tied note + triplet-16th figure);
    timpani roll; all strings in continuous slurred-pair 16ths `fff` (violins in octaves); basses on
    dotted-quarter beats.
  - m. 282–283: texture breaks to a single eighth-note stab; cellos and basses keep a 16th-note line alone
    (`fff`).
  - m. 283b–290: sudden tutti `fff`: horns + trumpets in dotted rhythmic repeated chords, woodwinds on the
    theme in chords, tenor trombones a2 + bass trombone in octaves on a slow dotted-half-note line (the
    cantus firmus), tuba on a low pedal note each bar, timpani roll, cellos/basses tremolo, violins/violas in
    fast 16th figuration; diminuendo hairpins from m. 290.
- Pointer: Adler says nearly every orchestral work has examples; ch. 14 returns to climax by repetition.

**Agent applicability**
- Plan the brass budget per piece: keep one brass group (trombones, or full brass melody) out of melodic
  roles until the climax or a key formal moment; meanwhile use it only for doublings/pads.
- For a "mystery" moment, a reserved trombone choir `pp` in octaves (e.g. trombone + bass trombone 12 semitones
  apart) is more effective than a loud entry.
- For an alternation build: brass phrase → woodwind/string answer → brass → tutti, each turn louder (CC1 steps
  up), ending with the woodwinds+horns+strings on the climax.
- For a repetition build: repeat the motto 3 times, add a new string figuration on the last, thin to low
  strings alone for 1–2 bars, then tutti with a slow trombone cantus firmus (half/dotted-half notes, octaves)
  against fast figuration, timpani roll and repeated-chord horns/trumpets.
- Cross-metre notation (trumpets in 3/4 vs 9/8) only affects the score: in MIDI write the triplet rhythm at
  the shared tempo.

## Timbral effects of the brass section (Efectos tímbricos de la sección de metal) (scan p.423–429, printed p.424–430)

**Concepts**
- Many brass special effects are recent and often belong to one composer's repertoire. Adler shows three from
  jazz and several from other sources.
- Harmon / wa-wa mute (sordina wa-wa, harmon): jazz mute with a stem; originally jazz only, used very often in
  North American orchestral music in the late 20th c. Works on trumpet and trombone.
- Derby mute (sordina Derby) / hat: a felt-crowned hat (or derby) held over the bell to dampen the sound.
- Rip: a jazz effect, now common in recent compositions: the player tongues the note hard while making a
  short upward lip glissando into it; it also gives a strong attack to the note that follows.
- Niente (`n`): a note that grows out of silence or dies into it.
- Druckman's mute symbols (Ex. 11-30): a triangle = straight mute; a mute-shaped symbol = harmon mute; `o` =
  harmon open, `+` = harmon closed with the stem (apéndice) covered.

**Rules**
- R15 [ALLOWED] Use harmon (wa-wa) mute on trumpet and trombone for a jazzy, buzzy, nasal colour; it is
  established in American orchestral writing [era: late 20th c., American].
- R16 [ALLOWED] For a soft, veiled trumpet solo use a derby mute or hat ("in hat with felt crown").
- R17 [PREFER] Start such a muted/hat solo `sf` then drop at once to `p` (`sf>p`); Adler says this entry is
  very common today.
- R18 [ALLOWED] Use a rip on brass (horns, trumpets, trombones) to underline the `sffz` that ends a phrase.
- R19 [ALLOWED] Mix open and closed harmon within a section (e.g. trumpet 1 closed while trumpet 2 open), and
  move notes in/out of silence (`n < mp > n`).
- R20 [MUST] When asking for techniques that are not yet standard, notate them meticulously (mute type and
  open/closed state per player, `n` dynamics, *senza sord.* returns). *Why:* players cannot guess.

**Examples**
- Ex. 11-27, Gershwin, *Rhapsody in Blue* (original Whiteman band version), mm. 131–137 (Allegro, printed
  p.424): soprano sax states the accented syncopated figure; Bb trumpet answers "with jazz mute (harmon)",
  then the trombone "with jazz mute" takes the next answer — short accented notes, a triplet turn. Shows
  harmon mute on trumpet and trombone in call-and-response.
- Ex. 11-28, Gershwin, *Concerto in F* II, mm. 31–44 (Andante con moto, printed p.425–427): oboes + EH carry the
  opening line; horns 1 and 3–4 muted `sfz-p` long notes; three clarinets (2 Bb cl + bass cl) `pp` in close
  harmony; cellos on a low pedal (whole notes); trumpet 1 solo "in hat with felt crown" (cued in flutes and
  violins) enters on a long tied note with swell, then a bluesy line; later trumpets 1–2 accented `mp`, horn 4
  "closed" `sfz-p`; violas/cellos divisi `p` chords. Shows: hat-muted trumpet solo over a soft clarinet choir.
- Ex. 11-29, Morton Gould, *Interplay* IV, 5 bars before rehearsal 21 (Allegro, 2/2, printed p.428): piano
  soloist builds `sffz` chords with string chords; horns 1–4, trumpets 1–3 and trombones 1–2 all "Rip" into
  an accented `sffz` chord together with the piano/strings stab; then horns/trombones/tuba hold `f` chords
  with accents, a solo snare drum (`mf`, rim shot) bridges, and at 21 woodwinds+strings+piano enter `ff`.
  Note "one Tuba on Trb. 3" (tuba doubling 3rd trombone).
- Ex. 11-30, Jacob Druckman, *Windows*, mm. 4–10 (printed p.429–430): a catalogue of new brass techniques —
  horns `sfz ppp` attacks, harmon-muted trumpets 1–3 `mp`/`mf < ff` swells, harmon-muted trombones 1–2,
  open trombone 3 and tuba, `n < mp > n` niente swells, *senza sord.* releases, all inside a texture of
  woodwind trills/flourishes, harp gliss, tam-tams, and strings split into 2 solo players vs the rest.
  Adler stresses that Druckman's brass demands are as extensive as those on the other sections.
- Additional passages for study (pointer, list on printed p.429): Dukas *La Péri* fanfare; Janáček
  *Sinfonietta* opening; Sibelius *Symphony No. 2* III after the trio repeat; Stravinsky *L'Histoire du
  soldat* "Marche" (cornet + trombone); Wagner *Rienzi* overture (allegro energico).

**Agent applicability**
- Mutes in MIDI: choose the library's harmon / straight / cup articulation per track or keyswitch; if a mute
  is unavailable, do not substitute a different mute silently — report it (inferred, consistent with the
  project's plugin rule).
- Rip (inferred MIDI realisation): start the target note with a pitch-bend sweep from about −3 to −5 semitones
  up to 0 over ~60–120 ms, or a fast chromatic grace run of 3–5 short notes leading into the accented target,
  with high velocity on the target; place all brass rips so they land together on the `sffz` beat.
- `sf>p` hat/derby solo: high-velocity attack, CC1/CC11 drop to ~30–40 within ~150 ms, then shape the phrase.
- Niente swells (`n < mp > n`): CC1/CC11 from 0 → ~70 → 0 across the note; needs a long-note patch with no
  hard attack.
- Jazz-flavoured orchestral cues (Gershwin/Gould idiom): harmon trumpets and trombones in call-and-response
  with saxes or clarinets; brass rips into ensemble stabs; snare drum as connective tissue.
