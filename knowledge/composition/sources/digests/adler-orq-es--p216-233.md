## 7 — Clarinet in Bb/A, end (scan p.216–218, printed p.207–209)

**Concepts**
- clarinetta ("little trumpet"): 18th-c. name, because early orchestral clarinet parts imitated the clarino
  trumpet. By the 19th c. the clarinet was "the nightingale of the orchestra" (ruiseñor de la orquesta).
- chalumeau (registro chalumeau): the low register of the clarinet family.
- dal niente attack (ataque dal niente): tone begins from near-total silence with no audible articulation,
  swells (e.g. to p) and fades back to nothing. Notated with "N" at the hairpin ends.
- echo tone (sonido de eco): extreme pianissimo, often marked *sotto voce*.

**Rules**
- R1 [PREFER] Use the Bb clarinet for flat keys and the A clarinet for sharp keys. *Why:* easier fingering
  and key signature for the player. [era: tonal repertoire, 18th–19th c.]
- R2 [ALLOWED] In 20th-c. and later scores the Bb clarinet predominates whatever the key, because tonality is
  weaker. (Concert-pitch MIDI is the same either way. The choice only matters for parts.)
- R3 [ALLOWED] The clarinet is agile and versatile, equally effective in virtuoso and lyrical passages in all
  registers: fast runs, wide arpeggios and cadenzas are idiomatic.
- R4 [PREFER] Clarinet staccato is very dry and cutting. It is less penetrating than oboe staccato and slightly
  more articulated than flute staccato. Use it for crisp, light detached lines.
- R5 [PREFER] Write single tonguing. Double and triple tonguing exist but only some clarinettists use them.
  Keep fast repeated-note passages moderate (inferred).
- R6 [PREFER] The clarinet does dal niente entries and fades to nothing better than any other woodwind. In MIDI,
  start CC11/CC1 near 0 and ramp up without an accented attack (low velocity) (inferred).
- R7 [ALLOWED] Echo tones (ppp, sotto voce): the book says only the clarinet can play this soft. They are
  most ethereal in the chalumeau register, and contemporary composers like them there.
- R8 [ALLOWED] Every trill and tremolo is possible. Wide-interval tremolos are harder above the staff
  (written ≳ G5) but still possible.
- R9 [ALLOWED] Glissandi between notes are easy (see ch.6).
- R10 [AVOID] Glissandi across the register break (cambio de registro). They are easier above the break than
  below it.
- R11 [MUST] Real glissandi go **upward only**. Downward, the player can only "bend" (modular u oscilar) with
  the embouchure, over a small range. Use that only when you want microtones.
- R12 [AVOID] Relying on key clicks, air through the tube, mouthpiece-only sounds or multiphonics in orchestral
  writing without a verbal description. Their notation is not standardised, and not every orchestral
  clarinettist can do them. [era: contemporary techniques, late 20th c.]

**Examples** (what each shows)
- Tchaikovsky, Sym. 5/I mm.1–10 (2 A cl. a2): dark motto theme in the low chalumeau, p→più f→mf.
- Rimsky-Korsakov, Golden Cockerel suite (A cl. solo cadenza, f, a piacere): chromatic runs over the
  whole range.
- Wagner, Tannhäuser overture mm.295–298 (A cl., p): leaping arpeggios from the low register to the top, with
  accents.
- Stravinsky, Soldier's Tale "Marcha del soldado" mm.47–52 (A cl., ff): accented high-register notes over
  changing 3/8–2/4 meters.
- Additional study passages: one-line pointer (Beethoven 6, Copland, Kodály, Mendelssohn 3, Mussorgsky,
  Prokofiev 5, Tchaikovsky 6, Thomas, Weber).
- Ex. 7-61/7-62: echo tones, ppp lines descending into the chalumeau with quintuplets. Ex. 7-62 marks a small
  pitch oscillation (bend).
- Kodály, Psalmus Hungaricus (2 A cl.): repeated-chord tremolos sff>p, then trills in the low/middle register.
- Ex. 7-64: bends (sound oscillation) and an upward gliss.

**Agent applicability**
- For a clarinet entry "out of nothing", use velocity ≤ 20 and a CC11 ramp from ~0. Never use a hard attack.
- Never write a descending clarinet glide. For "bends", keep them small (≤ 1 semitone, inferred) and downward.
- Ppp chalumeau pads/lines work well on clarinet, where other woodwinds would be too loud.

## Other clarinets — Eb/D clarinet examples (scan p.219, printed p.212)

(The descriptive text is on missing pp.210–211.)

**Examples**
- R. Strauss, Till Eulenspiegel, 43 bars before the end (D clarinet, "Freely, but quite fast", mf→sfz,
  dim.→pp): shrill high-register squeal, then a descent. This is the small clarinet as a grotesque,
  piercing voice.
- Mahler, Sym. 3/I at [12] (3 Bb cl. + 2 Eb cl., ff, "Heavy"): the 3 Bb clarinets play loud triads
  **bells up** (aufgehobener Schalltrichter, pabellón en alto). The Eb clarinets play grace-note figures.
  Footnote: Mahler often asks for bells-up in all his clarinets.
- Additional passages (p.214) for the Eb clarinet: pointer only (Barber Medea, Berg Wozzeck with 2 Eb cl. in unison,
  Carter, Chávez, Corigliano, Glass, Harbison, Ravel Daphnis, Strauss Heldenleben).

**Rules**
- R13 [ALLOWED] Use bells-up for a brassier, wilder, louder clarinet color at ff. [era: late-Romantic, Mahler]

## Bass clarinet (clarinete bajo; Bassklarinette) (scan p.219–221, printed p.212–214)

**Concepts**
- Usually pitched in Bb. A few past composers wrote for bass clarinet in A. [era: 19th c.]
- Notation:
  - German method: bass clef, sounding a M2 (2) below written. [era: late 19th–early 20th c., mainly Germany]
  - French method: treble clef, sounding a M9 (14) below written. The French method spread during the 19th–20th c.
    and is now widespread. Adler recommends it for current scores.

**Range** (Ex. 7-72; French/treble notation, sounding = written − 14)
- Lowest note, historically: written E3 → sounding D2 (MIDI 38). The text says "MI3", but its octave numbering
  is ambiguous [?].
- Lowest note, standard today: written Eb3 → sounding Db2 (37). It is reasonable to expect this on every bass
  clarinet.
- Lowest note, extended: written C3 → sounding Bb1 (34). Russian composers asked for it in the 1930s–40s. Not
  every orchestra has it, so it is risky. On the chart the accidental of the lowest sounding note is unclear
  [?].
- Top: written C6 → sounding Bb4 (70). Extreme top: written E6 → sounding D5 (74).

**Registers** (Ex. 7-73; same register names as the Bb clarinet)
- Chalumeau: the lowest octave plus a third. It runs from written Eb3/C3 to about G4, sounding ~Db2–F3
  (37–53).
- Clarion (clarín): written A4/B4–G5 [the text says A4, the chart bracket starts on B4 ?]. Sounding ~G3/A3–F4 (55/57–65).
- Weak top: written G5–C6, even E6. Sounding F4–Bb4/D5 (65–70/74).

**Rules**
- R14 [PREFER] Feature the bass clarinet in the chalumeau. This is its most distinctive and warm timbre:
  mysterious, dark, even sinister. The quality fades as the line rises.
- R15 [ALLOWED] The top notes (sounding ≈ F4–D5) are weak and hard to produce. Modern scores still ask for them
  often. Treat them as thin solo colour, never as a powerful lead (inferred).
- R16 [ALLOWED] The instrument plays lyrical legato and all kinds of staccato. Because of its size, its attack is a little less
  incisive than the smaller clarinets'.
- R17 [AVOID] Writing below sounding Db2 (37) unless you know the players have low-C instruments.

**Examples**
- Wagner, Götterdämmerung Act I Sc.3 mm.1–13 (Bb cl. + bass cl., pp): the clarinet melody over the bass
  clarinet's sustained low tones and rising chalumeau line. It shows the family blending as one voice
  across registers.
- Wagner, Tristan Prelude to Act II mm.13–20 (bass cl. solo, più p → cresc. → f): a long rising
  low-register melody.
- Stravinsky, Rite of Spring "Adoration of the Earth" mm.28–31: f staccato low figures, then a solo of rapid
  32nd-note runs. It shows low-register agility.
- R. Strauss, Salome at [320] (pp→sfz→molto dim. ppp): virtuosic chromatic runs over the full range, up into
  treble-clef territory.
- Additional passages: pointer only. Mostly works for Eb cl. + bass cl., and Strauss Don Quixote Var. I.

**Agent applicability**
- Bass clarinet MIDI range: 37–70 safe, 34–74 extreme. Its core colour is 37–53.
- Use it as the bass of a clarinet choir, or under clarinet melodies at pp.

## Alto clarinet in Eb (clarinete contralto en MIb; Altklarinette) (scan p.222, printed p.215)

**Range** (Ex. 7-78; sounds a M6 (9) below written)
- Written E3–E6 → sounding G2–G5 (MIDI 43–79). Read from the chart [?].

**Rules**
- R18 [ALLOWED] Rarely used in the orchestra. It is a standard member of the concert/wind band. [era: band use, 20th c.]
- R19 [PREFER] Use only the lowest two octaves, sounding ≈ G2–G4 (43–67). Only these are effective.
- R20 [PREFER] Its sound is rather weak. Use it to fill in harmony and for soft solo passages.
- R21 [ALLOWED] It has the same fingering as the Bb/A clarinets. Legato, staccato, long lyrical lines and fast scales are all
  easy.

**Examples**
- I. Dahl, Sinfonietta (band)/II mm.1–5 (Eb alto cl., pp dolce espressivo, 6/8 Andante con moto): a soft lyrical
  solo.

## Basset horn (corno di bassetto; Bassetthorn) (scan p.222–223, printed p.215–216)

**Concepts**
- Sometimes called the tenor clarinet of the orchestra. Always in F, sounding a P5 (7) below written, like the
  English horn. It is not a horn. The name probably comes from its sickle shape ("basset" = little bass).
- Invented c.1770 by the Mayrhofers. It appears rarely in 20th-c. scores after Strauss, but is still made, to
  play the older repertoire.
- Its bore is narrower than the alto or bass clarinet's. One musician called its timbre "affected gravity".

**Range** (Ex. 7-80)
- Written C3 (bass clef)–G6 → sounding F2–C6 (MIDI 41–84). Read from the chart [?].

**Rules**
- R22 [PREFER] For Mozart-era or Strauss-like scoring, use basset horns for veiled, solemn mid-low clarinet
  colour. [era: Classical (Mozart) and late Romantic (Strauss)]

**Examples**
- R. Strauss, Capriccio Sc.2 mm.22–26 (p → dim. pp): flowing triplet and sixteenth-note lines.
- Additional passages: pointer only. Mozart Clarinet Concerto K.622 (written originally for basset
  instrument), Magic Flute "O Isis und Osiris", Requiem; Strauss Rosenkavalier; Stravinsky Threni.

**Tags:** #orchestration/woodwinds/basset-horn #era/classical

## Contrabass clarinet (clarinete contrabajo) (scan p.223–224, printed p.216–217)

**Concepts**
- Pitched in Bb or Eb, an octave below the bass clarinet.
- Bb model: made of metal and folded back on itself, like a tiny contrabassoon. Other clarinets are wood, apart
  from the bells of the bass and alto.
- The Eb model is often called "contra-alto clarinet" and is usually made of rosewood (footnote).

**Range** (Ex. 7-82; read from a small chart [?])
- Bb contrabass: sounds 2 octaves + M2 (26) below written. Written ≈ D3 [?]–B5/C6 → sounding ≈ C1 (24) [?]
  up to ≈ A3–C4 (57–60) [?].
- Eb contra-alto: sounds an octave + M6 (21) below written. Written Eb3–B5 → sounding Gb1–D4 (30–62) [?].

**Rules**
- R23 [PREFER] Its sound is very rich, especially in the lowest register. Use it for low sustained pedals
  and the deep bass.
- R24 [AVOID] Agile writing. It lacks the bass clarinet's agility.
- R25 [ALLOWED] It is a recent instrument: rare in orchestral scores, common in band and wind ensemble works.

**Examples**
- G. Schuller, Concerto for Orchestra No.2/II mm.113–120 (Bb contrabass cl.): very low sustained tones, p→mp→ppp,
  with a short flourish at the end. A soft, deep pedal.

## Saxophone (saxofón; Saxophon) (scan p.224–228, printed p.217–221)

**Concepts**
- Invented by Adolphe Sax, Paris, c.1840. It has a metal body and a conical bore, but it is treated as a
  woodwind for four reasons:
  1. Its sound is closest to the clarinet family.
  2. It uses a single-reed mouthpiece like the clarinet's.
  3. Most clarinettists also play sax.
  4. It is used as a member of the woodwind section, not the brass.
- Family: sopranino Eb, soprano Bb, alto Eb, tenor Bb, baritone Eb, bass Bb. A sopranino in F also exists but
  is very rare (Ravel's Boléro, chamber music).
- Jazz sound vs symphonic sound:
  - Jazz sound: either sweet and sentimental with much vibrato, or stentorian.
  - Symphonic/classical sound: less vibrato and more dynamic control.

**Transpositions and ranges** (Ex. 7-84)
- All saxophones read the same written range in treble clef:
  - Bottom: written Bb3 B3 C4 C#4 D4.
  - Top: written D6 D#6 E6 F6 F#6 G6. The chart lists the top as a zone and does not mark which top
    notes are standard. Inferred from general practice: F6 (89) is the traditional key top, and F#6/G6 are
    extensions on modern instruments.
- Sounding ranges (MIDI). "Top" = sounding of written F6; "max" = sounding of written G6:

| Saxophone | Transposition | Low (written Bb3) | Top (written F6) | Max (written G6) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sopranino Eb | m3 (3) up | Db4 61 | Ab6 92 | Bb6 94 |
| Soprano Bb | M2 (2) down | Ab3 56 | Eb6 87 | F6 89 |
| Alto Eb | M6 (9) down | Db3 49 | Ab5 80 | Bb5 82 |
| Tenor Bb | M9 (14) down | Ab2 44 | Eb5 75 | F5 77 |
| Baritone Eb | M13 (21) down | Db2 37 | Ab4 68 | Bb4 70 |
| Bass Bb | 2 oct + M2 (26) down | Ab1 32 | Eb4 63 | F4 65 |

- F sopranino: sounds a P4 (5) above written (inferred, not charted).

**Rules**
- R26 [AVOID] Very soft playing at both extremes of the range, especially the lowest notes. Most players struggle there.
- R27 [PREFER] Keep important material in the best-controlled zone: from a P5 (7) above the lowest note, for
  almost two octaves (written ≈ F4–D6), on every saxophone. Sounding (subtracting the transposition, inferred):
  sopranino ≈ 68–89, soprano ≈ Eb4–C6 (63–84), alto ≈ Ab3–F5 (56–77), tenor ≈ Eb3–C5 (51–72), baritone ≈
  Ab2–F4 (44–65), bass ≈ Eb2–C4 (39–60).
- R28 [AVOID] Using a sax as a blending inner voice in the symphony orchestra without thought. Its sound is
  characteristic and tends to dominate other instruments. That is one reason it never fully entered the symphony
  orchestra. The other reason: early playing and tone were considered primitive. [era: pre-1920s; much improved
  since the 1920s]
- R29 [PREFER] In the orchestra, use the sax mainly as a soloist. The alto is the usual choice; soprano, tenor
  and baritone appear in some works.
- R30 [PREFER] For a jazz or popular evocation, use the jazz sound: lots of vibrato, sweet or stentorian. For
  "symphonic sax", use less vibrato and controlled dynamics. Early-20th-c. composers used the sax to suggest
  jazz. Contemporary composers use extended techniques developed by virtuosi. [era: early 20th c. vs late 20th c.]
- R31 [PREFER] Standard band/wind-ensemble sax sections:
  - (1) 2 altos, tenor, baritone, (bass).
  - (2) soprano, 1–2 altos, tenor, baritone, (bass).

**Examples**
- Ravel, Boléro 2–18 after [7]: the F sopranino solo (mp espressivo, vibrato) passes the melody to the Bb soprano
  (mp). Two sax colours share a single line.
- Bizet, L'Arlésienne Suite No.2/II mm.17–20 (alto sax solo, p, Moderato): a lyrical, singing alto line.
  [era: 19th-c. French orchestra]
- R. Strauss, Sinfonia Domestica mm.950–951, 964–984: bass, baritone, alto and soprano saxes at f–ff, with
  imitative entries of a short accented motif. This is a full sax quartet inside the orchestra.
- Band/wind-ensemble examples are in chs.17 and 19.
- Additional passages: pointer only. Berg (Der Wein, Violin Concerto, Lulu), Carpenter, Copland Piano Concerto,
  Gershwin An American in Paris, Harbison, Harris 5, Khachaturian "Sabre Dance", Mussorgsky-Ravel "Il vecchio
  castello", Penderecki St Luke Passion, Prokofiev Kijé, Rachmaninoff Symphonic Dances (alto), Vaughan Williams 6
  (tenor), Walton Belshazzar, Waxman.

**Agent applicability**
- In an orchestral mix, an alto sax solo stands out by default. Keep CC11 lower than you would for a clarinet in
  the same role (inferred).
- Avoid pp at the bottom fifth of each sax's range.

## Bassoon (fagot; Fagott) (scan p.228–230, printed p.221, 224–225)

**Concepts**
- A double-reed, conical-bore instrument: the bass of the woodwind section.
- The reed sits on a curved metal tube, the bocal (bocal, gancho). Pulling the bocal out lengthens the instrument
  (flatter). Pushing it in shortens it (sharper).
- It is related to the oboe but less nasal.
- Notation: bass clef. Tenor clef (C on 4th line) for passages with ledger lines above the bass staff, roughly
  from G4 (67) up.

**Range** (Ex. "7-8" [sic, probably 7-88])
- Bb1 (MIDI 34) to the top ≈ Eb5 (75). The top note is flat-marked, above the tenor staff; the ledger count is
  unclear [?]. Non-transposing.

**Rules**
- R32 [ALLOWED] The bassoon is excellent for lyrical melodies, and equally effective in staccato attacks and passages.
- R33 [PREFER] The basic orchestral bassoon section is 2. Larger orchestras use 3, or up to 4, and the last player usually doubles
  contrabassoon.
- R34 [ALLOWED] Multiple bassoons work in unison, in parallel intervals and in complex contrapuntal phrases.

**Examples**
- Stravinsky, Rite of Spring Part I opening mm.1–15 (bassoon solo, Lento ad lib., tenor clef, p … poco più f):
  the solo sits in the extreme high register, with grace notes, triplets/quintuplets and changing meter. The
  strained top register is used as an exotic, archaic colour.
- Dukas, Sorcerer's Apprentice mm.72–99 (3 bassoons a3 soli, mf, staccato, 3/8 Vif, poco cresc.): the grotesque
  march theme in unison. It shows the comic, dry staccato of massed bassoons.
- Bartók, Concerto for Orchestra/II mm.164–171 (3 bassoons, p):
  - Bsn 1 and 2 play slurred-staccato lines with triplets and trills.
  - Bsn 3 plays p staccato running sixteenths underneath.
  - Independent counterpoint for 3 bassoons.
- Additional passages: pointers only.
  - Solo: Beethoven 4/IV, Haydn 103 and 104, Mahler 9/II, Mozart 38/I, Prokofiev Peter and the Wolf [15],
    Ravel Boléro mm.41–48, Rimsky Scheherazade/II, Tchaikovsky 5/III.
  - Multiple: Berlioz Fantastique/V, Bizet Arlésienne 2/IV, Debussy "Fêtes", Mozart 40/II, Strauss Don Juan,
    Tchaikovsky 6/IV, Wagner "Mild und leise".

**Agent applicability**
- Write high-register bassoon solos (≥ C5, 72) only for a deliberately strained, haunting colour.
- For a "comic/grotesque" scherzo, use 2–3 bassoons in unison staccato in the low-mid register.

## Contrabassoon (contrafagot; Kontrafagott) (scan p.230–233, printed p.225–228)

**Concepts**
- Neither oboe nor bassoon has a higher auxiliary instrument. Both have lower ones.
- The contrabassoon is the lowest woodwind. It extends the bassoon an octave down and **sounds an octave below
  written**.

**Range** (Ex. 7-97)
- Written Bb1–Bb4 [top ?] → sounding Bb0 (MIDI 22)–Bb3 (58).
- The lowest 12th (19 semitones) is its most effective register: sounding Bb0–F2 (22–41).

**Rules**
- R35 [ALLOWED] It uses essentially the same techniques as the bassoon.
- R36 [AVOID] Fast articulation in the lowest register. Articulation there is thicker, tougher and slower. Its
  clumsy effect is often exploited on purpose (comic or grotesque).
- R37 [PREFER] Sustained low notes act like a long organ pipe. They have an audible buzz (zumbido) from the slow
  vibrations, and they give a solid foundation. This works best when it doubles cellos, basses and bassoons at
  the octave below.
- R38 [MUST] Give periodic rests in long lowest-register passages. The low notes use a lot of air.
- R39 [AVOID] Writing it in its upper/top register. There it is just a weaker, thinner bassoon and loses its
  character.
- R40 [ALLOWED] Legato and staccato are both effective.
- R41 [AVOID] Fast repeated staccato, especially in the lowest register. The air column is large and responds too
  slowly.
- R42 [PREFER] Contrabassoon solos are rare; Schuller wrote a concerto. Normally blend it with other instruments.

**Examples**
- R. Strauss, Salome Sc.3, 6–27 after [151] (contrabassoon, p → ff): sustained low notes with chromatic runs in
  septuplets and accents, then crescendo to ff. An eerie, exposed solo.
- Brahms, Haydn Variations mm.1–10: the "St Antoni" chorale for 2 oboes, 2 bassoons, contrabassoon and 4 horns.
  The contrabassoon doubles the pizzicato cello/bass line under the wind chorale. It is a blended foundation,
  not a solo.
- Ravel, Mother Goose "Beauty and the Beast" mm.114–123 (contrabassoon, pp, legato, chromatic): the Beast as a
  slow lyrical solo.
- Ravel, La Valse [37]–[38] (contrabassoon, f/ff, accents): a heavy waltz bass with arpeggio runs.
- Additional passages: pointers only. Beethoven Fidelio Act II duet (with muted cellos and basses in unison) and
  9/IV Alla Marcia; Bloch Schelomo; Brahms 1/IV; Górecki Beatus Vir (2 contrabassoons); Mahler 9/II; Schuller
  concerto; Strauss Elektra and Till.

**Agent applicability**
- Contrabassoon MIDI 22–58. Keep it mostly in 22–46.
- Double the cello/bass line an octave below for weight in tutti and soft chorales.
- Insert a rest of at least a beat every few bars on low sustained notes (inferred from R38).
