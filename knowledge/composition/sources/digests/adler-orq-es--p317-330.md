# Adler, El estudio de la orquestación — scans 317–330 (horn)

Pitch convention: C4 = middle C = MIDI 60. The book's own note names (e.g. "FA#2", "SOL2 y DO2") match this
convention (checked against the staff positions in Ex. 10-3 and 10-4). Horn examples are in **written** pitch.
Where I give concert pitch, I converted it myself: horn in F sounds a perfect fifth (7 semitones) lower than written.
Natural-horn examples are crook-dependent and are left as written.

## 10 La trompa — introduction (scan p.317, printed p.312)

**Concepts**
- Horn (trompa; corno It., cor Fr., Horn Ger.): the English "French horn" name is historically odd, since most
  development happened in Germany. Early orchestral parts (c. 1710) were often labelled *cor de chasse* (hunting
  horn); Bach used *corno da caccia*. The book just says "trompa".
- Dual role: in chamber music the horn counts as both woodwind and brass. It sits in both the woodwind quintet and
  the brass quintet.
- Symbolism: in 18th-c. Mozart opera the horn stood for cuckoldry (cornudez); in 19th-c. symphonic music, for
  heroism. `[era: 18th–19th c. topical symbolism]`
- Two kinds: the natural or hand horn (trompa natural / de mano; corno naturale, cor simple, Waldhorn, hand horn)
  and the valve horn (trompa de válvulas; corno ventile, cor à pistons, Ventilhorn), which most orchestras use today.

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] The horn is as agile as any other brass instrument. It works well both as a soloist and for doubling
  other voices.
- R2 [ALLOWED] Its sound is sweeter than the trumpet's, but in loud passages it has enough brightness and push to be
  heard over almost any combination of instruments.
- R3 [PREFER] Use the horn section, especially its middle register, as a velvety accompaniment under any solo
  instrument.

## La trompa natural — construction, register, hand technique (scan p.318, printed p.313)

**Concepts**
- Construction: a coiled metal tube about 6 mm in diameter at the mouthpiece, widening to about 75 mm at the bell
  throat (cuello del pabellón). The bell opens to about 28 cm across. `[era: natural horn]`
- The basic natural horn has 8 ft of tubing with C as its fundamental.
- Harmonic series, Ex. 10-1 ("Tr. DO", written): 1 C2 (fundamental, in parentheses: not playable), 2 C3, 3 G3,
  4 C4, 5 E4, 6 G4, 7 Bb4×, 8 C5, 9 D5, 10 E5, 11 F#5×, 12 G5, 13 A5×, 14 Bb5×, 15 B5, 16 C6.
  Written MIDI: 36, 48, 55, 60, 64, 67, 70, 72, 74, 76, 78, 79, 81, 82, 83, 84.
  "×" = badly out of tune. The 11 good notes are shown as whole notes: harmonics 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12, 15 and 16.
- Hand horn (trompa de mano): the player's right hand in the bell corrects out-of-tune harmonics and can even produce
  some notes outside the series. The source says the hand corrects notes that are flat (it "raises" flat low
  harmonics) and a relaxed embouchure corrects notes that are sharp. `[era: natural/hand horn, c.1750–1850]`
- Notation of hand technique: "+" = hand-stopped note, "o" = open note.

**Rules**
- R4 [AVOID] `[era: Classical natural horn]` Classical composers seldom asked for notes above the 12th harmonic.
- R5 [ALLOWED] `[era: natural horn, from Beethoven on]` From Beethoven onwards, harmonics up to the 16th were used often.
- R6 [ALLOWED] `[era: Romantic]` Brahms and Wagner had valve horns available but kept writing for the natural horn,
  often using the high harmonics.
- R7 [NEVER] `[era: natural horn]` Write the fundamental (harmonic 1). It generally could not be played.
- R8 [ALLOWED] `[era: hand horn]` Stopped (hand) notes can be mixed with open notes in a melody. The timbre change is
  clearly audible, because the hand also damps the sound.

**Examples**
- Ex. 10-2 Schubert, Symphony 9 (C major), I, bars 1–8. Horns in C, a2, Andante, p → pp. This is the opening unison
  theme. Most notes are open; written A4, B4 and F5 are marked "+" (hand-stopped). It shows that a hand-horn player
  could perform a stepwise melody that mixes open and stopped notes.

**Agent applicability**
- To evoke a period natural horn, restrict each horn line to the open harmonics of its crook (harmonics 2–6, 8–10, 12,
  15, 16, transposed by the crook). Avoid 7, 11, 13 and 14 except as deliberate, coloured "stopped" notes.

## Transposition and crooks (scan p.319–320, printed p.314–315) `[era: natural horn, 18th–early 19th c.]`

**Concepts**
- Crooks or tuning slides (varas de entonación / bombas / cilindros): interchangeable tubes that change the
  fundamental, and with it the whole harmonic series. Each crook gives a different transposition. They were first fitted
  at the end of the tube near the mouthpiece; later, they were inserted where the tuning slide usually sits.
- Most popular crooks in the 18th and early 19th c. Written-to-sounding offset in semitones:
  C alto 0 (sounds as written); Bb alto −2 (M2); A −3 (m3); Ab −4 (M3); G −5 (P4); F −7 (P5); E −8 (m6);
  Eb −9 (M6); D −10 (m7); C basso −12 (octave); Bb basso −14 (M9); A basso −15 (octave + m3).
- *alto* means high and *basso* means low. *Basso* adds an octave to the normal (*alto*) transposition. If the score
  does not say *basso*, the player uses the *alto* transposition.
- Less common crooks: G basso and even B basso (Brahms, Symphony 1). Rare *alto* crooks: F# (Haydn, "Farewell"
  Symphony), Db (Massenet, *Phèdre*; Bizet, *Carmen*). The book gives no offsets for these.
  Inferred offsets: G basso −17, B basso −13, F# −6 (inferred). Db: offset not given [?].

**Rules**
- R9 [MUST] `[era: natural horn]` Every natural horn except the one in C alto transposes downward.
- R10 [PREFER] `[era: late 18th–early 19th c.]` The leading players of the time preferred the brighter F crook to the
  darker, more sombre Eb and D crooks. The source thinks this probably led to the valve horn being built in F.

**Agent applicability**
- To imitate a Classical score, give each horn pair one crook per movement (e.g. horns in D for a D-major movement).
  Then write only that crook's open harmonics.

## Divided horns: cor alto / cor basso (scan p.319–320, printed p.314–315) `[era: natural horn, late 18th c.]`

**Concepts**
- From the horn's first use in the orchestra, natural horns were divided into firsts and seconds. The 1st horn
  (*cor alto*) took the high horn part; the 2nd (*cor basso*) took the low part. Their registers were fixed toward the
  end of the 18th c.
- Ex. 10-3 (written, natural-horn notation):
  - Cor basso (Tr. 2): normal range C3 up to about G5 [?]. Pedal tones G2 and C2 are shown in parentheses.
  - Cor alto (Tr. 1): C4 to C6.

**Rules**
- R11 [ALLOWED] `[era: natural horn]` Low-horn players often used a larger mouthpiece. By relaxing the embouchure they
  even played the pedal tones G2 and C2 (written).
- R12 `[era: period-instrument practice, as stated in the source]` Natural horns are still used in some European
  orchestras; the source names the Vienna and Berlin Philharmonics. Those players use the larger mouthpiece and
  practise lowering pitch with the embouchure for works that need it, such as Haydn's early Symphony in G.

**Agent applicability**
- In a Classical-style pair, keep horn 1 in written C4–C6 and horn 2 in written C3–G5 at most [?]. Horn 2's typical
  role is the low harmonics (C3, G3, C4, E4, G4).

## La trompa de válvulas — history and construction (scan p.320, printed p.315)

**Concepts**
- `[era: c.1840s–1900]` Natural and valve horns coexisted for about 50 years. The reasons were distrust of the valve
  mechanism and a taste for the natural horn's rustic "hunting" quality. By the early 20th c., probably because of
  post-Wagner chromaticism, the valve horn had become the standard symphony instrument.
- Wagner and Bruckner kept writing natural/hand-horn passages. Many of them actually sound better and are more in
  tune on the valve horn.
- Construction: three rotary valves, played by the left hand's index, middle and ring fingers. Each valve switches
  spring-loaded tube lengths into the main circuit. Together with the two main tube sets, they make the horn fully
  chromatic.
- Double horn (trompa doble): essentially a valve horn in F with a second set of coiled tubing. The F side is about
  12 ft long. The Bb alto side (división de Sib) is about 9 ft long; a left-thumb valve switches to it, shortening
  the F tube by 3 ft.
- On the Bb side, the 12th harmonic equals the 16th harmonic of the F side. That makes high notes much easier on Bb.

**Rules**
- R13 [MUST] For a double horn, write only for horn in F. The player decides whether to use the F or the Bb side.

## Valve horn in F: range and register (scan p.320–321, printed p.315–316)

**Concepts**
- Range, Ex. 10-4:
  - Normal range, written F#2 (42) to C6 (84). Sounding B1 (35) to F5 (77).
  - Bb-side pedal tones (in parentheses, stemless black notes): written C2, Db2, D2, F2 (36–41). Sounding F1, Gb1, G1,
    Bb1 (29–34). Conversion is mine: written − 7.
  - Footnote: F-side pedal tones are not used.
- Register colours, Ex. 10-5 (written pitch, bracket ends read from the chart; concert pitch = −7, conversion mine):

  | colour (Spanish) | written | concert | concert MIDI |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | dark, somewhat indistinct (oscuro y un poco indefinido) | C3–G3 | F2–C3 | 41–48 |
  | deep and solid (profundo y sólido) | B3 [?, maybe C4]–D4 | E3–G3 | 52–55 |
  | bright and heroic (brillante y heroico) | D4–G5 | G3–C5 | 55–72 |
  | bright and loud (brillante y fuerte) | G5–B5 [?, maybe C6] | C5–E5/F5 | 72–76/77 |

**Rules**
- R14 [AVOID] Low notes are hard to control, because the embouchure has to be very relaxed. This is especially true
  of the rarely used Bb-side pedals from written F#2 down (concert B1 down, MIDI ≤35).
- R15 [PREFER] Use pedal and very low notes as long notes, or in slow-moving passages.
- R16 [NEVER] Put pedal tones in fast passages. *Why:* they speak slowly.
- R17 [MUST] If a part stays in the top register for a long time, give it frequent rests. *Why:* playing up there
  continuously is tiring.
- R18 [AVOID] Large leaps and passages that are excessively fast or winding (sinuosos). *Why:* the player must hear
  each note mentally, then find it with the embouchure. The rest of the range is otherwise agile.

**Agent applicability**
- Default concert range for horn MIDI is 35–77. Use 29–34 only as sustained pedal notes, never in runs.
- Heroic/solo writing goes in concert 55–72. Above 72, use short spans and follow them with rests.
- For pianissimo low pads, concert 41–48 sounds dark and indistinct. Concert 52–55 is deep and solid.

## Four-horn section layout (scan p.321, printed p.316)

**Concepts**
- Today horns 1 and 3 play the high parts and horns 2 and 4 the low ones. *Why (historical):* when four horns first
  entered the orchestra, pair 1–2 (high/low) was crooked in a different key from pair 3–4 (also high/low).
  `[era: natural horn origin; convention still current]`
- Ex. 10-6a: 4 horns in F on two treble staves, horns 1+2 on the top staff and 3+4 below. The parts of horns 2 and 3
  cross: horn 3 is higher than horn 2.
- Ex. 10-6b: with 3 horns, horn 3 (traditionally a high horn) sits on the lower staff but is voiced above horn 2.

**Rules**
- R19 [PREFER] Voice four horns from the top as 1 > 3 > 2 > 4. Horn 3 sits above horn 2 in pitch even though it is
  notated on the 3–4 staff.
- R20 [ALLOWED] Some modern scores put the two high horns (1, 3) on one staff and the two low horns (2, 4) on the
  staff directly below.
- R21 [PREFER] With three horns, voice horn 3 above horn 2.

**Agent applicability**
- When assigning a 4-note chord to four horn tracks: top note → HN1, 2nd → HN3, 3rd → HN2, bottom → HN4.

## Old and new notation (scan p.322, printed p.317)

**Concepts**
- New notation: in all recently published scores and new editions of older ones, horn-in-F parts always sound a
  perfect fifth below written. This holds in treble clef and in the less common bass clef.
- Old notation (antigua): many 19th-c. scores have lower-horn parts in bass clef that sound a perfect fourth *above*
  written, i.e. they are written an octave lower than new notation. In those scores, treble-clef parts still sound a
  fifth lower. `[era: 19th-c. scores]`
- Ex. 10-7: sounding F2 (41) is written C3 (48) in new notation and C2 (36) in old notation.

**Rules**
- R22 [MUST] Use new notation for any bass-clef horn part you produce (P5 down, same as treble clef).
- R23 [MUST] When reading 19th-c. horn bass clef, check which convention it uses. Under old notation, sounding =
  written + 5 semitones, not − 7. Players used to old notation may ask which convention a part follows.

## Representative horn passages (scan p.322–325, printed p.317–320)

What each example demonstrates:
- **Solo:**
  - Ex. 10-8, Brahms, Symphony 1, IV, bars 30–38 (horn in C, Più Andante, *f sempre e passionato*, <> swells): a broad,
    lyrical solo theme in the middle/upper register over the orchestra.
  - Ex. 10-9, Beethoven, Symphony 6, III, bars 132–153 (horn 1 in F, Allegro 3/4, *p dolce*, cresc.): a rustic
    stepwise solo melody in the middle register.
  - Ex. 10-10, R. Strauss, *Till Eulenspiegel*, bars 6–12 (horn in F, 6/8, p → cresc.): the famous agile solo. It
    climbs high in the treble, then plunges into the bass clef to accented low notes, showing wide range and agility.
  - Ex. 10-11, Ravel, *Pavane pour une infante défunte*, bars 1–11 (2 horns in G; horn 1 solo pp, horn 2 holding low
    notes underneath; *p > pp expressif*): a soft, intimate lyrical solo with a second horn as a quiet bass.
- **Horns in unison:**
  - Ex. 10-12, R. Strauss, *Don Juan*, bars 530–540 (horns in E, *ff molto espr.*): the big heroic theme in horn
    unison. Unison horns give power and warmth.
  - Ex. 10-13, Mahler, Symphony 1, I, bars 345–356 (horns in F): horns 1,3 above 2,4, then "+5,6" and "+7" enter
    while the dynamics grow f → ff → fff. It ends in a unison triplet fanfare (ff → fff). The climax is built by adding
    horns, then massing them in unison.
  - Additional study passages: one pointer list (Brahms 4, Dvořák 5, Mahler 3 opening with eight horns in unison).
- **Horns in harmony:**
  - Ex. 10-14, Handel, *Judas Maccabaeus*, bars 9–16 (horns in G): two horns in harmony, mostly in thirds and sixths
    on natural-horn notes. `[era: Baroque natural horn]`
  - Ex. 10-15, Weber, *Der Freischütz*, overture, bars 10–25 (2 horns in F + 2 horns in C, Andante, soli, mf): the
    famous four-horn chorale. The two pairs in different crooks trade and share the melody and harmony.
    `[era: natural horn, early 19th c.]`
  - Ex. 10-16, Humperdinck, *Hänsel und Gretel*, overture, bars 1–8 (4 horns in F, ♩ = 69, *sehr weich*): a soft
    four-part horn chorale in close harmony on two staves, a hymn-like opening.
  - Additional study passages: one pointer list (Franck, Liszt, Mahler 1, Mendelssohn "Nocturne", Wagner *Tannhäuser*).

**Agent applicability**
- For heroic themes, put 4–8 horns in unison in the middle register (concert ~G3–C5), ff.
- For soft chorales, use four-part close harmony, p/pp, in the middle register. Horns blend as a self-contained choir.

## Articulation and tonguing (scan p.325–327, printed p.320–322)

**Concepts**
- As on any wind instrument, notes under a slur (ligadura de expresión) are played in one breath, without
  re-tonguing.
- Two kinds of single tonguing (picado sencillo):
  1. Normal, staccato or hard tonguing, syllable "tu".
  2. Soft or legato tonguing, syllable "du".
- Double and triple tonguing (picado doble / triple) are also possible on the horn.

**Rules**
- R24 [ALLOWED] Fast repeated notes and chords in a horn section are playable with double or triple tonguing, both
  loud and soft.

**Examples**
- Ex. 10-17, Wagner, *Siegfried*, Act I, scene 2, bars 1–14 (horn in F, Lebhaft 6/8, accents, fermatas, *sehr stark
  ausgehalten*, p): hard staccato "tu" tonguing in a horn-call figure.
- Ex. 10-18, Tchaikovsky, Symphony 5, II, bars 8–16 (horn 1 solo, Andante, *dolce con molto espr.*): legato "du"
  tonguing in a cantabile solo.
- Ex. 10-19, Rimsky-Korsakov, *Capriccio espagnol*, V, bars 119–131 (4 horns in F, Allegro, f/sf): fast repeated
  16th-note chords, probably double-tongued because of the speed.
- Ex. 10-20, R. Strauss, *Don Juan*, bars 501–505 (4 horns in E, Allegro, mf → f): triple-tongued repeated notes
  (triplets and sextuplets).
- Ex. 10-21, Scriabin, *Poem of Ecstasy*, bars 182–183 (4 horns, pp): a very simple case of triple tonguing. The two
  horn pairs alternate soft repeated triplet notes.

**Agent applicability**
- For sustained horn lines, use slurred, overlapping MIDI notes.
- Repeated-note ostinati at Allegro speed are idiomatic for horns: in triplets at pp, or in 16ths at f.

## Muted and stopped horn (scan p.327–328, printed p.322–323)

**Concepts**
- Muted horn (*con sordino*; muted horn): the player normally puts a non-transposing mute in the bell. It changes the
  colour but not the pitch. Write *senza sordino* where the mute comes off.
- Stopped horn (trompa tapada; *chiuso* It., *bouché* Fr., *gestopft* Ger., stopped horn): the right hand is pushed
  as far into the bell as possible, up to the throat, blocking much of the sound. The result is soft, light and
  somewhat nasal. It can be used on single notes or whole passages. Notation: "+" = stopped, "o" = open; *al aire*
  (open) marks where stopping ends.

**Rules**
- R25 [MUST] Mark *con sordino* where muting begins and *senza sordino* where it ends.
- R26 [MUST] Mark *al aire* (open) where stopping ends.
- R27 [ALLOWED] Use stopped horn for an echo: play a phrase open, then repeat it stopped and softer.
- R28 [PREFER] Stopped horn works well on sf > p attacks.
- R29 [ALLOWED] A player may use a mute instead of the hand for stopped passages, although the sound will differ.
- R30 [MUST] Allow only hand-stopping when the part switches quickly between open and stopped (no time to fit a mute).

**Examples**
- Ex. 10-22, Debussy, *Prélude à l'après-midi d'un faune*, bars 106–109 (4 horns muted, "sourdines", ppp/pp, 12/8):
  muted horns 1 and 3 carry lines with soft held notes from 2 and 4. This is the famous muted-horn colour.
- Ex. 10-23, Rimsky-Korsakov, *Capriccio espagnol*, II, bars 45–48 (horn solo, 3/8): a figure played *mf ouvert*,
  then answered *p bouché*. This is the open/stopped echo.
- Ex. 10-24, Mahler, Symphony 4, IV, bars 76–79 (4 horns in F, Wieder lebhaft): *gest.* chords at mf/ff with
  diminuendo, alternating p f p f, then ff → p. Fast open/stopped changes like these need the hand.

**Agent applicability**
- With sample libraries, render "stopped" by using a stopped/muted articulation patch, a lower dynamic and a thinner
  EQ, and do not use the open patch for those notes (inferred). The source does not discuss the pitch effect of
  stopping.

## Special effects: trills, tremolos, glissandi, cuivré, bells up (scan p.328–330, printed p.323–325)

**Concepts**
- Trills (trinos): played with a valve or by changing the lip position. They sound somewhat heavy and clumsy, and can
  have a comic or sardonic effect.
- Tremolos at intervals wider than a second are possible but very difficult, so risky.
- Glissandi are rarely given to horns.
- Cuivré (Fr., "brassy"): a metallic, strident sound made by raising lip tension, blowing harder and attacking harder,
  so that the instrument's metal itself vibrates.
- Bells up (pabellones en alto / al aire; *pavillons en l'air* Fr., *Schalltrichter auf* Ger., bells up Eng.): the
  player takes the right hand out of the bell and turns the bell upward, so the opening points at the audience.

**Rules**
- R31 [PREFER] Write horn trills to a major or minor second above the main note.
- R32 [AVOID] Tremolos wider than a second. *Why:* very difficult and risky.
- R33 [PREFER] Write horn glissandi ascending, loud, and in the upper register, where the harmonics lie close together.
- R34 [AVOID] Descending glissandi. *Why:* they are almost impossible and must be faked. This is acceptable only when
  the whole orchestra plays the same glissando and covers it.
- R35 [ALLOWED] A very fast written-out arpeggio may be played as a glissando.
- R36 [PREFER] Use bells up in very loud, boisterous passages.

**Examples**
- Ex. 10-25, R. Strauss, *Till Eulenspiegel*, bars 641–643 (4 horns in F, 1/3 and 2/4, Sehr lebhaft, f cresc.): a
  trilled chord held across bars, ending in a short note.
- Ex. 10-26, R. Strauss, *Salome*, at rehearsal [360] (2 horns in F, Fast, ff): trills with triplet turns, a grotesque
  and sardonic effect.
- Additional trill/tremolo study passages: one pointer list (Chabrier *España*, Falla *El amor brujo*, Mahler 9).
- Ex. 10-27, R. Strauss, *Der Rosenkavalier*, introduction, bars 30–31 (horn, ff): rapid upward triplet arpeggios
  ("whoops") to a high note, written as arpeggios but played as glissandi because of the speed.
- Ex. 10-28, Barber, Symphony 1, bars 135–136 (horns in F, ff): a held high note under a fermata, then a descending
  gliss marked "gliss." to an accented note and a diminuendo. In practice it is played unorthodoxly, masked by the
  whole orchestra's identical glissando.
- Additional glissando study passage: one pointer (Stravinsky, *Rite of Spring*, "Danse sacrale").
- Ex. 10-29, Bizet, *L'Arlésienne* Suite 1, "Carillon", bars 1–4 (4 horns in E, Allegretto 3/4, *Cuivré*, ff, every
  note accented ^): a repeated three-note bell figure, brassy and strident.
- Ex. 10-30, Mahler, Symphony 4, III, bars 319–326 (horns 1,2 and 3,4 in F, Pesante, ff, *Schalltrichter auf*): loud
  chordal climax with bells up.

**Agent applicability**
- For horn rips and whoops in MIDI, use a fast upward run of harmonic-series notes. Prefer a rip/gliss articulation
  if the library has one.
- Never write long descending horn glissandi as exposed solos.
- For cuivré or bells up: use top dynamic layers (CC1 high), hard accents and high velocity, in loud passages only
  (inferred).
