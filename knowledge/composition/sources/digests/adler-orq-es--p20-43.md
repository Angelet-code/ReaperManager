## 1 La orquesta: ayer y hoy (scan p.20–23, printed p.3–6)

**Concepts**
- Orchestration is personal: timbre and texture give clarity to form and content; specific colours and the
  particular spacing of chords in the orchestral fabric give each composer a recognisable sound (Wagner vs Brahms,
  contemporaries with very different orchestral sounds).
- "Hearing mentally" (oír mentalmente): the orchestrator must hear what is written on the page (Piston's dictum).
- Consort types, 17th-c. England: *musica fracta* = broken / heterogeneous consort (mixed families);
  *musica sociata* = whole / homogeneous consort (one family). `[era: 17th-c. England]`
- Two broad periods of orchestral history: (a) beginnings (~1600) to the deaths of Bach and Handel (~1750);
  (b) Mannheim school, Haydn, Mozart to the present.

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] In Medieval/Renaissance style, instruments need not be specified: parts are labelled only by voice
  (soprano, alto, tenor, bass) and any suitable instrument plays them. `[era: Medieval–Renaissance]`
- R2 [PREFER] For early-Baroque style, keep one uniform orchestration through a whole piece (Monteverdi,
  *Combattimento*, 1624: a uniform basic mood calls for uniform instrumentation). `[era: early Baroque]`
- R3 [ALLOWED] Substitution between families was accepted up to the mid-18th c. (L. Mozart, 1740: a viola may play
  an alto trombone part if the trombonist is inadequate); by mid-18th c. this was the exception. `[era: ~1740]`
- R4 [PREFER] Late-17th c. / early Baroque: strings are the core; Lully (1686) used strings + flutes (or recorders),
  oboes, bassoons, horns, trumpets and timpani, but this set was not universal. Bach varied combinations freely,
  and player availability dictated the orchestra. `[era: Baroque, ~1680–1750]`
- R5 [MUST] Classical orchestra (Haydn/Mozart) template: strings (Vn1, Vn2, Va, Vc, Cb); woodwinds 2 fl, 2 ob,
  2 cl, 2 bsn; brass 2 hn, 2 tpt + timpani. No independent percussion section in the symphony; snare drum, bass drum,
  triangle and cymbals appear in opera scores. `[era: Classical, ~1770–1800]`
- R6 [PREFER] Classical timpani play with the trumpets: they were classed with the brass and were rarely used
  independently of the trumpets. `[era: Classical]`
- R7 [MUST] Score order: horns sit above trumpets even though trumpets usually play higher (historical: horns joined
  the orchestra first; trumpets were placed next to the timpani they played with).
- R8 [PREFER] Post-Classical expansion adds auxiliary winds to extend range: piccolo (flautín), English horn
  (corno inglés), bass clarinet (clarinete bajo), contrabassoon (contrafagot); from the opera orchestra come
  trombones, harps and a larger percussion battery. `[era: early–mid 19th c.]`
- R9 [ALLOWED] Berlioz-scale orchestras more than double winds, brass and percussion and greatly enlarge strings.
  `[era: Berlioz, mid-19th c.]`
- R10 [PREFER] Late-Romantic / early-20th-c. "large orchestra" (Mahler, Stravinsky) as the norm: strings
  18-16-14-12-10 (Vn1-Vn2-Va-Vc-Cb) instead of the older 6-6-4-4-2; winds can reach 6 fl, 5 ob, 6 cl, 4 bsn; brass
  8 hn, 4 tpt, 4 tbn, 2 tubas; 2 harps, piano, and percussion needing 4–5 players. `[era: ~1890–1915]`
- R11 [ALLOWED] Re-orchestrating older works for larger forces is a historical practice: Mozart added clarinet and
  trombones to Handel's *Messiah*; Wagner, Mahler, Weingartner, Mengelberg, Toscanini and Beecham "improved"
  Beethoven and Schumann symphonies for larger orchestras. Tastes in orchestration change (discussed in ch. 15–17).
- R12 [PREFER] Study method: reduce orchestral scores to piano and orchestrate piano pieces; Ravel, Debussy and
  Stravinsky often composed advanced scores at the piano and then orchestrated them (inferred workflow value: a
  piano sketch is a legitimate first stage).

**Agent applicability**
- When asked for a "classical" (Haydn/Mozart) sound, restrict to R5 forces, keep timpani tied to trumpet entries,
  and add no trombones/percussion section; for "Mahler/Strauss" size, use R10 forces.
- Track order in REAPER templates should follow score order: woods, horns, then trumpets, trombones, tuba, timpani,
  percussion, harp/piano, strings (R7).
- Source references: Berlioz (rev. R. Strauss) and Rimsky-Korsakov treatises are the two classic 19th-c. texts.

## 2 Instrumentos de cuerda y arco — overview (scan p.24–25, printed p.7–8)

**Concepts**
- The modern orchestra has four sections or choirs (secciones o coros): strings, woodwinds, brass, percussion.
- Bowed strings (instrumentos de cuerda y arco; cordófonos = chordophones): violins, violas, cellos, double basses;
  the "violin family". It was the first section fully developed (construction perfected around 1700).

**Rules**
- R13 [PREFER] Use the string family as the orchestra's backbone, for these properties: (1) range of about seven
  octaves from basses to violins; (2) homogeneous tone colour across the range, with only slight register changes;
  (3) widest dynamic range, from barely audible pp to the loudest ff; (4) warm, rich tone suited to expressive
  passages; (5) versatility: arco, pizzicato, col legno, fast passages, long slow melodies, leaps, trills, double
  stops, chords, special effects; (6) continuous sound with no breathing limits (unlike winds).
- R14 [PREFER] Full symphony-orchestra string section, two players per stand (atril):
  Vn1 16–18 players (8–9 stands); Vn2 14–16 (7–8); Va 10–12 (5–6); Vc 10–12 (5–6); Cb 8–10 (4–5).

**Agent applicability**
- Use R14 counts to estimate how many divisi parts a section can carry (each stand = 2 players) and for balance:
  a full section is ~16 Vn1 against single wind players.
- Strings can sustain indefinitely: no need to insert breath gaps in string MIDI (contrast winds).

## Construcción (scan p.25–26, printed p.8–9)

**Concepts** (part names for lookup; all four instruments share the violin's design, only proportions differ)
- Body (caja) + neck (mástil). Top/belly (tapa, tabla de resonancia), back (fondo), ribs (aros); inside, the bass bar
  (barra armónica) transmits string vibration. Fingerboard (diapasón), pegbox (clavijero), pegs (clavijas), scroll
  (voluta), nut (cejilla), bridge (puente), tailpiece (cordal), f-holes (aberturas de resonancia en forma de f).
- Four strings; the double bass sometimes five. The bow excites the string between the end of the fingerboard and
  the bridge; the bridge passes vibration to the top and, less, to the back.

**Tags:** #orchestration/strings/construction

## Afinación — tuning (scan p.26, printed p.9)

**Rules**
- R15 [MUST] Violin, viola and cello are tuned in fifths; the double bass in fourths. Open strings (cuerdas al aire)
  = strings sounding without left-hand stopping.
- R16 [MUST] Open strings, concert pitch (MIDI):
  - Violin: G3 55, D4 62, A4 69, E5 76.
  - Viola: C3 48, G3 55, D4 62, A4 69.
  - Cello: C2 36, G2 43, D3 50, A3 57.
  - Double bass (written E2 A2 D3 G3): sounding E1 28, A1 33, D2 38, G2 43 (converted from written).
  - Five-string bass / low extension (written C below E2): adds low C, sounding C1 24, tuning C–E–A–D–G
    (converted). Adler describes the extra low C both as a fifth string and as reached "by a mechanical extension"
    (the text blends the two options).
- R17 [MUST] The double bass is the only transposing member of the family: it sounds an octave lower than written.
  MIDI must be entered at sounding pitch (one octave below the printed part).

**Agent applicability**
- Lowest concert notes: Vn 55, Va 48, Vc 36, Cb 28 (24 with C extension/5-string). Reject lower notes.
- Open-string pitches (Vn 55/62/69/76, etc.) are the resonant notes for chords and drones.

## Digitación — fingering and positions (scan p.27, printed p.10)

**Concepts**
- Stopping (pisar) a string with left-hand fingers shortens it and raises the pitch. The hand moves from first
  position (primera posición), near the nut, up the fingerboard towards the bridge, changing position (shift).
- Finger numbers written above the staff: 0 = open string, 1 = index, 2 = middle, 3 = ring, 4 = little finger.
- Violin/viola basic positions on the A string (each position starts one diatonic step higher):
  1st pos: fingers 1–4 = B, C, D, E; 2nd: C, D, E, F; 3rd: D, E, F, G; 4th: E, F, G, A; 5th: F, G, A, B.
  (On violin A string = A4 69: 1st position spans B4–E5, 5th position F5–B5.)

**Rules**
- R18 [PREFER] Fingering principles are the same for all bowed strings, but cello and bass details differ (covered
  per instrument in ch. 3). First-position charts: violin p.52, viola p.66, cello p.77, bass p.85 (printed).

**Agent applicability**
- A fast passage that stays inside one position (≈ a fourth to a fifth per string) is easier than one that shifts
  every few notes (inferred).

**Tags:** #orchestration/strings/fingering

## Cuerdas dobles, triples y cuádruples — multiple stops (scan p.28, printed p.11)

**Concepts**
- Multiple stops (cuerdas múltiples): two or more notes on adjacent strings played together. Double stop (doble
  cuerda) = 2 notes; triple / quadruple stops (triples / cuádruples cuerdas) = 3 / 4 notes.
- Two kinds of double stops: (1) one or both notes on an open string; (2) both notes stopped.

**Rules**
- R19 [MUST] Each note of a multiple stop must lie on a different, adjacent string. A double stop whose two notes
  would both have to be played on the same string is impossible (Ex. 2-7 to 2-10 show impossible cases, e.g. two
  notes both below the second-lowest open string).
- R20 [MUST] Triple stops can be attacked simultaneously only at a fairly strong dynamic (f or mf), because the bow
  must press the middle string to catch all three. *Why:* bow pressure.
- R21 [PREFER] Triple stops at p/pp are played slightly arpeggiated (rolled).
- R22 [MUST] Quadruple stops are always arpeggiated: the modern bow can sustain only two strings at once.
- R23 [PREFER] The best-sounding triple and quadruple stops include one or two open strings (more resonance).
- R24 [MUST] Double bass: only double stops that include an open string are practical (Ex. 2-10).
- R25 [ALLOWED] In 17th–18th-c. style, four-note chords could be held more easily: the Baroque bow's stick curved
  outward (away from the hair), the modern one curves slightly inward. `[era: Baroque bow, 17th–18th c.]`

**Examples**
- Ex. 2-7 to 2-10: tables of possible vs impossible double/triple/quadruple stops for Vn, Va, Vc, Cb; possible ones
  cluster around open strings (5ths, 6ths, octaves, 3rds over open strings). Fuller charts in ch. 3.

**Agent applicability**
- For one string line playing chords (non divisi), check: notes count ≤ 4, each pitch assignable to a distinct
  adjacent string with lowest note ≥ the lowest open string; triple stops only at velocity ≥ ~mf; for 4-note chords
  stagger onsets (lower pair first, upper pair a few tens of ms later) (stagger values inferred).
- In a sample library, write double stops in pp as two divisi lines instead (inferred; consistent with R21).

**Tags:** #orchestration/strings/double-stops #orchestration/idiomatic-limits

## Divisi (scan p.29–31, printed p.12–14)

**Concepts**
- Terms: divisi (It.), divisés (Fr.), geteilt (Ger.), divisi (Eng.); abbreviation *div.* Cancel with *unisoni* /
  *unis.* *non div.* = every player plays the double stop.
- Stand split: outside player (exterior, right side of the stand) takes the upper note; inside player (interior,
  left side) the lower note.
- *div. a 3* / *div. a 4*: three / four different players per chord.
- Division by stands (dividir por atriles): first stand top note, second stand next lower, etc. Terms: *da leggii*
  (It.), *par pupitres* (Fr.), *Pultweise / Pult.* (Ger.).
- Half section: *la mitad*; It. *la metà*, Fr. *la moitié*, Ger. *die Hälfte*, Eng. *half*. The inside players rest.
  All return with *todos*: *tutti* (It.), *tous* (Fr.), *alle* (Ger.), *all* (Eng.).

**Rules**
- R26 [MUST] Without the word *divisi*, players treat two notes in one part as a double stop; write *div.* to split.
- R27 [PREFER] When triple/quadruple stops must be divided, specify the split (Ex. 2-12: brackets show which notes go
  together, e.g. upper two vs lowest note; *non div.* where all play the chord).
- R28 [PREFER] For division by stands, write 3–4 separate lines in the part and add "divide by stands"; in the full
  score the composer may also mark divisi within each stand (Ex. 2-13).

**Examples**
- Ex. 2-11 Debussy, *Nocturnes* "Nuages", bars 7–15 (strings only), Modéré 6/4: Vn1 *div. a 6*, pp sustained
  high chords (8va) then *a 3* streams of parallel chords; Vn2 *a 3* parallel chords; violas *a 2* lines; Vc pp;
  Cb pizz pp. Demonstrates multi-part divisi building a soft, rich chordal string pad from parallel chord streams.
- Ex. 2-13 R. Strauss, *Also sprach Zarathustra*, at 27 ("Like a dance"): Vn1 1st stand div. vs stands 2–5
  *una corda* with glissando; Vn2 and violas split per stand (1st, 2nd, 4th stand), viola solo f. Demonstrates
  stand-by-stand divisi notated in the full score for an intricate, transparent texture.

**Agent applicability**
- Model each divisi part as its own track or MIDI channel; with a full section (R14) div. a 2 halves the players
  per line, div. a 6 leaves ~3 per part (inferred: thinner and softer per line, good for pp pads).
- Use *half section* (inside players rest) when a lighter section sound is wanted without changing register.

**Tags:** #orchestration/strings/divisi #orchestration/texture/pad

## Vibrato (scan p.31, printed p.14)

**Rules**
- R29 [PREFER] Use vibrato (rocking the stopped finger rapidly around the pitch) on sustained notes: it adds beauty
  and intensity without distorting the fundamental pitch. Most string players use it by default.
- R30 [ALLOWED] *senza vibrato* (sin vibrato) for a white, pale sound.
- R31 [MUST] An open string cannot be vibrated. Two workarounds: (a) finger and vibrate the note an octave higher on
  the next higher string so it vibrates sympathetically (only on the lower three strings); (b) play the same pitch
  stopped on the next lower string and vibrate it (only on the upper three strings).

**Agent applicability**
- For pale/ethereal string colour choose non-vibrato patches or set the vibrato CC (e.g. Albion CC21) low; keep
  vibrato up on long expressive notes.

**Tags:** #orchestration/strings/vibrato #performance/expression

## Glissando y portamento (scan p.32–33, printed p.15–16)

**Concepts**
- Glissando (*gliss.*): sliding a finger along one string from one pitch to another; notated as a line between two
  notes, with or without "gliss." Up and down both possible.
- Portamento (*port.*): a more natural, expressive slide used to connect melody notes far apart; rarely notated.
- Fingered / written glissando (glissando digitado / escrito): every note written and played (fast chromatic run);
  more common in solo literature.

**Rules**
- R32 [PREFER] A proper glissando is played in one long legato bow so the intermediate pitches (or at least the
  first and last) sound.
- R33 [MUST] *port.* = minimal, discreet slide; *gliss.* = deliberate slide at full volume.
- R34 [MUST] A glissando across more than one string is not a "true" glissando: the slide breaks when it reaches the
  open string and continues on the next string.
- R35 [ALLOWED] A fingered glissando played by a whole section sounds like an indefinite glissando.

**Examples**
- Ex. 2-14 Ravel, *La Valse*, at 30, Viennese waltz tempo: Vn1 *sur Sol* (on the G string), mf espressif melody with
  expressive slides on wide intervals; shows the G-string glissando as a stylistic Viennese gesture.
- Ex. 2-15 Bartók, *Music for Strings, Percussion and Celesta*, II, 1 bar before 170, ♩ = 138: Vn1 alternates
  pizz. and arco with glissandi, one played on the D string (III); shows gliss. in a rhythmic, percussive context.
- Ex. 2-16 Mahler, Symphony 10, I, bars 151–152, Adagio: Vn1 f, wide melodic leaps ending in a gliss. that spans
  strings.
- Ex. 2-17 Mahler, Symphony 7, II, 2 bars before 92, Allegro moderato: Vn1 ff>pp then ff, triplet chromatic runs
  marked "A-D-G with one finger": fingered glissando across three strings.
- Additional study passages (pointer): Bartók *Miraculous Mandarin*; Debussy *Iberia*; Mahler Sym. 4 III; Ravel
  *La Valse*; Schwantner *Aftertones of Infinity*; R. Strauss *Till Eulenspiegel* (fingered gliss.).

**Agent applicability**
- Render a gliss. with pitch bend or a library's portamento/slide legato on one track; keep the slide inside one
  bow (no re-attack). Portamento = short slide time and lower level; gliss. = audible, full-volume slide.
- Romantic/Viennese styles accept expressive portamento on wide melodic leaps (Ravel, Mahler) `[era: late-Romantic]`.

**Tags:** #orchestration/strings/glissando #orchestration/strings/portamento

## El arco — the bow (scan p.33–34, printed p.16–17)

**Concepts**
- History: early bows were curved like archery bows (still used in Arabic and Far-Eastern fiddles); European bows
  were curved until the 16th c.; Corelli, Vivaldi and Tartini used bows curved slightly outward; François Tourte
  (1747–1835) fixed the modern inward-curved shape. `[era: Baroque vs post-1780 bow]`
- Parts: stick (pernambuco wood), tip plate (punta), horsehair (cerdas), metal ferrule at the frog (talón), screw
  (tornillo) to tension the hair. Hair tension gives elasticity; the bow balances at its centre.
- Bow stroke (golpe de arco, arcada): normally midway between fingerboard end and bridge; other contact points
  change the sound. Down-bow (arco abajo, ⊓) = frog to tip; up-bow (arco arriba, V) = tip to frog. Players do
  not always use the whole bow.

**Rules**
- R36 [PREFER] Bowing choices depend on style, character, tempo and dynamic; even experts disagree. Mark bowings
  only where needed (see R43).

**Tags:** #orchestration/strings/bowing

## Uso del arco — non legato and legato (scan p.34–37, printed p.17–20)

**Concepts**
- Non legato: no slurs; each note takes its own bow change, slow or fast.
- Legato: a slur means all notes under it are played in one bow (one direction).

**Rules**
- R37 [ALLOWED] Separate bows on every note can still sound connected: skilled players change bow inaudibly
  (Ex. 2-18).
- R38 [PREFER] An anacrusis starts up-bow unless the composer marks down-bow.
- R39 [PREFER] Two consecutive up-bows (with a short break shown by a tenuto line) are common, so the next accented
  downbeat falls on a down-bow (Ex. 2-19, bar 1: E♭ quarter, brief stop, E♭ eighth in the same up-bow).
- R40 [PREFER] Two consecutive vigorous attacks need two successive down-bows; to prepare, change bow early on the
  long note before (Ex. 2-20).
- R41 [PREFER] More weight and volume near the frog (more hand pressure). A crescendo is most effective on an up-bow;
  a diminuendo is often on a down-bow.
- R42 [NEVER] Write long phrase slurs in string parts. Slurs mean "one bow" only.
- R43 [PREFER] Mark bow directions only to counteract the players' natural habits; do not over-mark parts.
- R44 [MUST] The number of notes per bow is limited, mainly by tempo and dynamic: fast and soft passages allow many
  notes per bow; at forte, fewer (Mendelssohn, Ex. 2-21 vs 2-22: about six notes per bow at f).
- R45 [AVOID] Overloading the bow in slow passages even at soft dynamics; especially for cellos and basses, whose
  bows are slightly shorter than violin/viola bows.
- R46 [PREFER] To get a very long legato line, split the section and stagger bow changes between players
  (half the section changes at different points) (Ex. 2-25).

**Examples**
- Ex. 2-18 Elgar, *Pomp and Circumstance* No. 1, trio, Molto maestoso: Vn1 broad melody with a bow change on every
  note; demonstrates R37.
- Ex. 2-19 Schubert, Symphony 5, II, bars 1–8, Andante con moto 6/8, all strings p: slurred groups and a retaken
  up-bow on beat 2 of bar 1 (R39).
- Ex. 2-20 Beethoven, *Coriolan* overture, bars 276–286, Allegro: Vn1/Vn2 tied whole notes with ⊓V on the long note,
  then ff down-bow triple-stop chords; demonstrates R40.
- Ex. 2-21 Mendelssohn, Symphony 4, I, bars 378–388, Allegro 6/8: Vn1 p long slurs over many eighths (fast + soft).
- Ex. 2-22 Same work, bars 461–464: violas f cresc., only six notes per bow (R44).
- Ex. 2-23 Liszt, *Les Préludes*, bars 30–34, Adagio: unison strings with a long slur under a crescendo; impossible as
  written unless rebowed from the end of bar 30. Ex. 2-24 shows a rebowing into several bows; Ex. 2-25 shows half the
  section bowing differently (R45, R46).

**Agent applicability**
- MIDI legato patches: slurs map to overlapping notes; long phrases are fine in MIDI but, for realism, insert
  occasional re-articulations (bow changes) every 1–2 bars at f, longer at p in fast tempi (inferred from R44).
- Crescendos placed on up-bows: no MIDI effect; just shape CC1/CC11 smoothly (inferred).

## Arcadas especiales con el arco sobre las cuerdas — on-string bowings (scan p.38–42, printed p.21–25)

Adler divides special bowings into (1) bow stays on the string; (2) bow bounces off the string.

**Concepts**
- Détaché (Fr.; "separate bows"): one bow per note, clear articulation, no accent unless marked.
- *Con la punta* (at the tip): *a punta d'arco* (It.), *à la pointe* (Fr.), *an der Spitze* (Ger.).
- *Con el talón* (at the frog): *al tallone* (It.), *au talon* (Fr.), *am Frosch* (Ger.).
- Louré (Fr.) / portato (It.): legato with slight separations inside one bow; notated with tenuto lines under/over the
  notes plus slurs for the bow changes.
- Staccato (from *staccare*, to detach): on strings, reserve the word for the on-string effect. Dot above/below.
  - Separate-bow staccato (arcada separada staccato): short separate strokes.
  - Slurred staccato (staccato ligado): a series of short notes separated within one bow.
- Martelé (Fr.) / martellato or marcato (It.): "hammered": fast, well articulated, heavy, separate stroke like a
  sforzando; any part of the bow; the bow stays on the string; a gap between notes; each stroke starts with a strong
  accent. Signs: dot, or ^ (marcato hat), or > with a dot, above the note.

**Rules**
- R47 [PREFER] Détaché in fast tempi at f/mf uses the middle to upper third of the bow; for louder sound, at or near the
  frog.
- R48 [PREFER] Use *punta d'arco* for a much lighter, delicate sound (fast pp figures).
- R49 [PREFER] Use *al tallone* for a strong, heavy stroke.
- R50 [PREFER] A series of down-bows gives a strong, vigorous effect; it can be played fairly fast by lifting the bow
  between strokes, usually at the frog.
- R51 [PREFER] Louré is very expressive and common in accompaniments; easy both up and down bow.
- R52 [PREFER] Staccato works best in moderate to slow tempi; can be loud or soft.
- R53 [MUST] A staccato note sounds roughly half its written value: a quarter plays as an eighth plus an eighth rest
  (Ex. 2-32); slurred staccato quarters likewise sound as eighths with rests (Ex. 2-33).
- R54 [PREFER] Slurred staccato is played like louré (one bow) but shorter and more spaced.
- R55 [MUST] Dotted figure in one bow (e.g. dotted quarter + eighth): the long note is shortened, leaving a small gap
  before the short note; put the staccato dot under the short note only. A dot on both would audibly shorten the long
  note too.
- R56 [PREFER] For a lively, light dotted-eighth + sixteenth figure, omit slurs and ask for separate bows, usually
  near the tip, with the long note on an up-bow; dots may be added over the sixteenths (Ex. 2-36).
- R57 [MUST] In fast tempi "staccato" inevitably becomes off-string (bouncing): call it spiccato or saltando, not
  staccato.

**Examples**
- Ex. 2-26 Tchaikovsky, *Romeo and Juliet*, bars 141–143, Allegro giusto: all strings f cresc., fast sixteenth-note
  scale runs, détaché (R47).
- Ex. 2-27 Bartók, *Concerto for Orchestra*, V, bars 8–13, Allegro con fuoco: Vn2 div. pp *punta d'arco* rapid
  sixteenths, a light murmuring background (R48).

## Usos especiales del arco sin tocar las cuerdas — off-string bowings (scan p.43, printed p.26; continues)

**Concepts**
- Spiccato (It.): Adler names three ways of playing it, depending on speed and dynamic.
  - Deliberate spiccato (spiccato deliberado): slow/moderate tempo; the player deliberately bounces the bow, reducing
    right-hand pressure and letting the wrist drop the middle of the bow in a semicircular motion. Notated with dots
    like staccato.
  - Spontaneous spiccato (spiccato espontáneo, saltando): fast tempi; a short, quick wrist-only up-down motion makes the
    bow bounce by itself on each stroke.
  - Slurred spiccato (spiccato ligado): a short series of spiccato notes grouped in one bow.

**Rules**
- R58 [MUST] Required lightness and speed decide between deliberate and spontaneous spiccato: slow/moderate →
  deliberate; fast → spontaneous (saltando).

**Examples**
- Ex. 2-38 Stravinsky, *Dumbarton Oaks* concerto, I, at 22, Tempo giusto ♪ = 152: Vn1 f deliberate spiccato.
- Ex. 2-39 Rachmaninoff, *Symphonic Dances*, I, at 18, Allegro: Vn1 p→pp continuous sixteenths, spontaneous
  spiccato.
- Ex. 2-40 Mahler, Symphony 4, I, bars 21–23: violas and cellos pp *leggiero*, short slurred-spiccato groups of
  sixteenths on up-bows.

**Agent applicability**
- Moderate-tempo bouncing notes: short spiccato patch, moderate velocity; fast continuous sixteenths p/pp: spiccato
  patch with low velocity and slight velocity variation (inferred).
