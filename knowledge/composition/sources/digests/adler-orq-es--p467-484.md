## 13 Keyboard instruments — introduction (scan p.467, printed p.468)

**Concepts**
- Keyboard instruments (instrumentos de teclado): present in the orchestra throughout its history, but with a changing role.
- Continuo (continuo / bajo cifrado): harpsichord or sometimes organ realizing the harmony of a figured bass.

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] [era: Baroque to c.1770] Give a harpsichord (or organ; later fortepiano) a continuo part realizing the figured-bass harmony. *Why:* standard practice in Baroque music, early Haydn and Mozart symphonies, the Bach sons and the Mannheim school.
- R2 [PREFER] [era: late 19th c. onward] Write piano and celesta as full orchestral parts woven into the texture, like the string, wind or percussion parts. Use them for their own timbre, their ability to play several lines at once, or as soloists, not as a chord-filling continuo.
- R3 [ALLOWED] Standard orchestras employ at least one full-time pianist, who also plays harpsichord and celesta. Large orchestras add a part-time organist. *Agent use:* a single "keyboard" player can change instrument between movements, but cannot play two keyboards at once (inferred).

**Agent applicability**
- To evoke a period, add a harpsichord continuo track (Baroque/early Classical). In a post-1880 orchestral texture, write a real piano or celesta line instead.

## Piano (piano / pianoforte) (scan p.467–474, printed p.468–475)

Names: Pianoforte (It.), Piano (Fr.), Klavier (Ger.), Piano (Eng.).

**Concepts**
- History: the piano existed from the 18th c., but it became an orchestral (not solo) instrument only in the late 19th c., first in France (Saint-Saëns, d'Indy, Debussy, Stravinsky). Its use spread widely in the 20th c.
- [era: 19th c. to early 20th c.] Footnote: while the harpsichord was forgotten, the piano often played continuo in Baroque performances. Conductors dropped this when they began giving the figured bass to woodwinds; Siloti's Vivaldi arrangements are the typical example.
- Five orchestral roles (formas de uso):
  1. Concerto soloist.
  2. Obbligato part. Examples: Bloch *Concerto grosso No 1*; Stravinsky *Petrushka*. Saint-Saëns *Carnival of the Animals* and *Symphony No 3* use two pianos.
  3. Purely orchestral member. Examples: Debussy *Printemps*; F. Martin *Sym 4*; Shostakovich *Sym 1, 5*; Copland *Sym 3*; Prokofiev *Sym 5*; Sessions *Sym 2*; Colgrass *As Quiet As*.
  4. Percussion instrument: replaces or contrasts with xylophone, marimba or vibraphone. Its extreme low register can reinforce timpani or bass drum.
  5. Filler (relleno): in amateur and school orchestras it substitutes for missing instruments (oboe, bassoons, viola). See ch.16.

**Range**
- Ex. 13-1: A0 (MIDI 21) to C8 (MIDI 108), written A1 under 8vb and C6 under 15ma. The widest range of any orchestral instrument except the organ.

**Pedals (pedales)**
- Damper or sustain pedal (pedal de resonancia), right: lifts all dampers so strings keep ringing after the keys are released. Most used. Found on grands and uprights.
- Soft pedal, *una corda* (pedal de sordina), left: on a grand it shifts the hammers so they strike one (sometimes two) of the three strings. On an upright it moves the hammers closer to the strings. Both soften the sound. Second most used.
- Sostenuto pedal (pedal tonal / *sostenuto* / 3rd pedal), middle: sustains only the notes held down when the pedal is pressed. Most useful for bass pedal points while other notes are played. It has no effect above middle C. Either of the other pedals can then be used without affecting the held notes.

**Rules**
- R4 [PREFER] In the orchestra, use the piano to double a passage or to emphasize a *secco* note or chord. Prefer its extreme registers (very high, very low) to the middle.
- R5 [ALLOWED] Doubling the piano with strings, woodwinds or brass in any combination works well.
- R6 [ALLOWED] In *fortissimo* passages, give arpeggios to the piano instead of the harp. *Why:* the harp would be covered.
- R7 [AVOID] Expecting an orchestral piano solo to sound like a concerto soloist. *Why:* the piano usually sits at the back of the orchestra, so short contrasting solos do not give a soloist impression.
- R8 [MUST] Name the pedals as *una corda*, *sostenuto* and pedal de resonancia (damper). [AVOID] calling the right pedal "sostenuto". *Why:* that usage confuses players.
- R9 [AVOID] Relying on the sostenuto pedal for older pianos, European pianos and most uprights. *Why:* many lack it. [ALLOWED] on the orchestra's concert grand (about 3 m), which almost certainly has it.
- R10 [ALLOWED] [era: 20th-c. extended techniques] Recent effects:
  - prepared piano (piano preparado): objects such as nails or screws placed on or between the strings;
  - strings struck with sticks or brushes, sounding like a dulcimer or mallet percussion;
  - strings plucked, *secco* or left to ring;
  - thunderous bass tremolo, imitating a full battery of bass drums: hold the damper pedal and alternate both hands on the lowest strings inside the piano;
  - upright piano for bar or vaudeville "local color" (Bernstein *The Age of Anxiety*; Copland *Billy the Kid*);
  - toy piano and player piano (pianola) (Nancarrow; Colgrass *As Quiet As*).
- R11 [ALLOWED] Whole "orchestras" of several pianos plus percussion: Stravinsky *Les Noces* (4 pianos + percussion); Orff *Catulli Carmina*.

**Examples**
- Ex. 13-2, Stravinsky *Petrushka*, Tableau I "Russian Dance", mm.1–9, Allegro giusto, quarter = 116. Piano (f) doubles the full woodwind section a2 (piccolos, flutes, oboes, English horn, clarinets, bassoons) in a repeated chordal figure, with the left hand in steady eighths. This adds "ping" and very fresh articulation. Strings are pizzicato and horns/trumpets play off-beat chords. At the end, the piano glissando joins the xylophone glissando and the harps. Stravinsky later arranged the dance for solo piano.
- Ex. 13-3, Shostakovich *Symphony No 1*, mvt 2, mm.113–118, Allegro, quarter = 192. Piano in octaves in both hands, upper register (8va), ff. It doubles piccolo, flutes, oboes, clarinets and violins/violas on a fast chromatic sixteenth line, adding an incisive, cutting edge. Bassoons, low brass, cellos and basses hold pedal notes, and horns/trumpets punctuate.
- Ex. 13-4, Bernstein *On the Town*, "Times Square", mm.1–5, Allegro, half = 88. Piano as accompanist: RH plays mf staccato chords on every quarter, LH plays bass notes on beats 1 and 3, all under an E-flat clarinet solo. Only the piano's bass notes are doubled, very lightly, by double-bass pizzicato (p). Snare drum (brushes) and bass drum, p, reinforce the piano's rhythm. The upper strings rest.
- Further study (pointer): Berg, Bernstein, Copland, Corigliano, Shostakovich, Stravinsky (*Les Noces*, *Oedipus Rex*, *Petrushka* Tableau II, *Symphony of Psalms* with two pianos) (printed p.475).

**Agent applicability**
- To sharpen a woodwind or string unison line, double it on a piano track in octaves, high register, with short notes and high velocity. Avoid the middle register (C4–C5) for doublings.
- For a light-music or show accompaniment (oom-pah), write RH quarter-note chords and LH roots on beats 1 and 3. Double the bass notes softly with DB pizz and the rhythm with brushes.
- For ff arpeggios that a harp could not carry, give them to the piano. Sample piano: low register (A0–C2) under timpani or bass-drum hits as reinforcement.
- Sustain: write CC64 for the damper pedal. Most sample libraries do not model the sostenuto pedal (CC66) or una corda (CC67), so check before using them (inferred).

## Celesta (celesta / célesta) (scan p.474–476, printed p.475–477)

Name: Céleste (Fr.).

**Concepts**
- A "steel-bar piano": it looks like a miniature piano and sounds much like a glockenspiel. The tone is soft and delicate, penetrating because of its high frequencies, but far less incisive than glockenspiel.
- Mechanism: felt hammers strike steel bars mounted over wooden resonator boxes.

**Range**
- Text: four octaves. Ex. 13-5: written C3 to C7, sounding one octave higher, i.e. C4 (MIDI 60) to C8 (MIDI 108). Converted from written.
- The example also shows a second, filled-in note just above the written C7 [?: probably D7–F7 written, an optional extension on larger instruments (inferred)].

**Rules**
- R12 [NEVER] Expect a truly short *staccato*. *Why:* the resonators sustain the notes.
- R13 [ALLOWED] Damper pedal for extra sustain. Its effect is much subtler than on the piano.
- R14 [ALLOWED] Melodic lines, chords and arpeggios are all effective. The orchestra pianist usually plays the celesta.
- R15 [PREFER] Celesta doubling any combination of strings, harp, piano and soft woodwinds. *Why:* it adds a silvery glitter (brillo argentino) to the whole sound.

**Examples**
- Ex. 13-6, Tchaikovsky *The Nutcracker*, "Dance of the Sugar Plum Fairy", mm.5–12, Andante, 2/4. The classic solo use: a staccato-marked melody in the RH with a light staccato accompaniment in the LH, both in treble clef, mf rising to f.
- Ex. 13-7, R. Strauss *Der Rosenkavalier*, Act II, Octavian's "Mir ist die Ehre", 4 mm. before reh. 25. The celesta marks every reference to the silver rose with a unique color. It plays measured tremolo chords (ff then dim.) doubling flutes, glockenspiel, harps and high divisi violins (3 solo violins). After reh. 25 (quarter = 69) it plays ppp chromatic arpeggiated figures with the flutes, harps and muted solo violins.
- Further study (pointer, printed p.477): Bartók *Music for Strings, Percussion and Celesta* mvt 3; Holst *The Planets* ("Venus", "Mercury"); Respighi *Fountains of Rome*; Grofé *Grand Canyon* "On the Trail" (cadenza). Hovhaness and Toch write the celesta in a different key from the strings.

**Agent applicability**
- MIDI is concert pitch: write celesta notes C4–C8 (60–108). The sweet spot for glitter is about C5–C7 (inferred).
- Use the celesta as a doubling "sparkle" on the top line of soft strings, harp or flute. Avoid relying on it for crisp staccato (sample release will ring).

## Harpsichord (clave / clavecín) (scan p.477–478, printed p.478–479)

Names: Cembalo (It.), Clavecin (Fr.), Cembalo (Ger.), Harpsichord (Eng.).

**Concepts**
- A plucked-string keyboard: quills or leather plectra pluck the strings; no hammers.
- Registers (stops) are slides worked by 5–8 pedals or hand stops. Large two-manual instruments have a coupler that combines both manuals. Stops and coupler vary the tone, and coupled manuals add power.
- Pedal harpsichord (clavecín de pedales): has a full organ pedalboard. Rare; must be requested specifically.

**Range**
- Ex. 13-8: F1 (MIDI 29) to F6 (MIDI 89) [? top read as F6; could be E6].

**Rules**
- R16 [ALLOWED] [era: Baroque, early Haydn] Harpsichord always present in the orchestra, playing continuo.
- R17 [AVOID] [era: post-Baroque] Using the harpsichord as a structural ensemble member in a larger, later-style orchestra. *Why:* its sound is relatively small, which is why the piano replaced it.
- R18 [ALLOWED] [era: last ~50 years] Renewed interest in building harpsichords and using them in the orchestra for color.
- R19 [MUST] Keep the harpsichord from being swamped. It sounds best alone or with instruments that can play very softly.
- R20 [ALLOWED] Amplified harpsichord (Penderecki *Partita*) removes the balance problem.

**Examples**
- Ex. 13-9, M. Powell *Miniatures for Baroque Ensemble*, mvt 4, mm.1–5, Brioso, quarter = 192. A thin chamber texture (flute, oboe, violin, viola, cello) around a ff harpsichord. The harpsichord plays angular chromatic eighth-note lines and staccato chords. The strings use short sul ponticello ff chords, then pizzicato and ord. eighths. Winds play short accented figures (fz > dim.). The other instruments mostly play in the harpsichord's rests or very short notes, so the harpsichord stays audible.
- Recent works that place the harpsichord inside an orchestral texture: Falla *El retablo de Maese Pedro*; F. Martin *Petite symphonie concertante*; R. Strauss *Dance Suite after Couperin*; Carter *Double Concerto*.
- Further study (pointer, printed p.479): Colgrass *As Quiet As*; Falla *Harpsichord Concerto*; Sheriff; Trimble.

**Agent applicability**
- For harpsichord in a mix, thin the ensemble around it: few instruments, short notes, entries in its rests. Never put it under tutti forte.
- For Baroque pastiche, a continuo track with figured-bass realization in the range F1–F6 (29–89).

## Organ (órgano) (scan p.479–481, printed p.480–482)

Names: Organo (It.), Orgue (Fr.), Orgel (Ger.).

**Concepts**
- [era: Baroque] Indispensable as continuo and a frequent soloist with orchestra.
- When the continuo disappeared, the organ withdrew to churches. It kept a place in the opera house for religious scenes: Meyerbeer *Le Prophète*, Gounod *Faust*, Halévy *La Juive*, Puccini *Tosca*, Wagner *Lohengrin* [era: 19th-c. opera].
- Mendelssohn, Schumann, Brahms and Reubke wrote solo organ music, but no major concert work with organ part came until Saint-Saëns *Symphony No 3*. Mahler (*Sym 8*) and Strauss (*Also sprach Zarathustra*) led an expansive orchestral use. In the last 70 years there have been many organ concertos.
- Foot registers: 8' = written pitch; 16' = octave lower, 32' = two octaves lower, 4' = octave higher, 2' = two octaves higher.

**Range** (written at 8' pitch, without 16'/32'/4'/2' extensions; Ex. 13-10)
- Manuals (manuales): C2 (36) to C7 (96), top written C6 under 8va.
- Pedal (pedal / pedalero): C2 (36) to G4 (67).
- The organ has the largest range of any orchestral instrument.

**Rules**
- R21 [MUST] To extend the range downward one or two octaves, write "+16'" or "+32'" at the start of the passage. To extend upward one or two octaves, write "+4'" or "+2'".
- R22 [AVOID] Counting on 32' (most organs lack it) or always on 2' (some lack it).
- R23 [PREFER] Use the organ for what it does best: notes sustained at constant volume indefinitely, and a large number of timbre combinations to complement or contrast orchestral colors.
- R24 [MUST] Plan for substitution. Many concert halls have no pipe organ, so the intended large organ may be replaced by an electronic organ or a small positive organ with insufficient power and color.

**Examples**
- Ex. 13-11, Saint-Saëns *Symphony No 3*, mvt 1, mm.350–365, Poco adagio, quarter = 60, D-flat major. Strings (arco pp) play the melody. The organ (pp) sustains whole-note and tied chords in the manuals over pedal notes (Ped.), later adding a slow quarter-note inner line. The organ lends the strings its sustain and makes the sound more sensual; it is well integrated throughout the symphony.
- Further study (pointer, printed p.482): Holst *The Planets*, "Mars" and "Neptune" (extensive organ throughout); Respighi *Pines of Rome* mvt 4 from reh. 20; R. Strauss *Zarathustra* mm.19–21; Corigliano *Three Hallucinations* (electric organ); Grainger; Laderman; Shostakovich. Opera organ passages: Gounod, Mascagni, Puccini, Verdi, Wagner.

**Agent applicability**
- An organ pad under strings: manuals hold long chords pp, with pedal on the root at C2–G4 (or an octave lower with a 16' stop). Hold notes; change at harmonic rhythm.
- For "church/religious scene" color in opera-style scoring, add an organ track [era: 19th-c. opera].
- Pedal at 16' sounds an octave below written. In MIDI, write the sounding pitch; a 16' pedal on C2 sounds C1 (24) (inferred).

## Harmonium (armonio) (scan p.482–484, printed p.483–485)

Names: Organetto (It.), Harmonium (Fr., Ger., Eng.).

**Concepts**
- A small bellows organ, also called a reed organ (órgano de lengüetas). The player pumps two pedals under the keyboard to work the bellows; the air makes reeds vibrate when keys are pressed.
- One or two manuals, five-octave compass. Some have up to 15 stops that change tone and extend the range an octave up and down. Rare today [era: late 19th to early 20th c.].

**Range**
- Ex. 13-12: F1 (29) to F6 (89) [? top read as F6], written F2 under 8vb at the bottom. An extra filled-in note under 8va shows the upward extension available on some instruments [?].

**Rules**
- R25 [ALLOWED] [era: early 20th c.; reviving in Europe] Few orchestral works use the harmonium. It had a revival in the early 20th c. (R. Strauss, Bartók, Hindemith) and seems to be returning to fashion in Europe.
- R26 [PREFER] Write harmonium parts like organ parts without pedalboard (two staves, manuals only).
- R27 [PREFER] Use the harmonium to sustain a harmonic accompaniment under recitative. *Why:* no instrument except the organ does this as well.

**Examples**
- Ex. 13-13, R. Strauss *Ariadne auf Naxos*, mm.80–89. Recitative between the Music Master and Major-domo. A small string group of single stands punctuates: ff chords, later p pizzicato. Between the punctuations the harmonium holds soft (p) sustained chords, often with fermatas, under the voice.
- Further study (pointer, printed p.485): Maxwell Davies *Missa super L'Homme armé* (harpsichord, celesta, honky-tonk piano); Lederman *Magic Prison*; Webern *Five Pieces for Orchestra* Op. 10 No 5. Earlier list also cites Schreker *Kammersymphonie* (piano, celesta, harmonium).

**Agent applicability**
- For intimate or chamber-opera recitative, write harmonium sustained chords (manuals F1–F6) under the voice. Leave rhythmic punctuation to a few strings.
