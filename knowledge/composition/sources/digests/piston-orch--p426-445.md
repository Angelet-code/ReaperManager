# Piston, *Orchestration* — scans s213L–s222R (printed p.426–445)

This part of the book is the "Problems" section: Piston sets a melody or a two-element texture, then gives a
model solution with commentary. The exercises themselves are skipped; what is kept is the scoring logic of
each solution. Element letters follow the book's method (A = melody, B = accompaniment / harmonic background).

## Orchestration of melody — the groups combined (scan s213L–s215L, printed p.426–430)

**Concepts**
- Pure colors (colores puros): scoring a line without much unison doubling, so each instrument's timbre stays audible. Piston calls this clearer and more transparent, "greatly to be desired".
- Tone-weight (peso sonoro): how much a part counts in the balance; added by unison doublings.
- Joining colors (empalme de timbres): handing one long melodic line from one instrument to another across its range.

**Rules — strings + woodwind**
- R1 [ALLOWED] Double a string section with one woodwind an octave above or below (e.g. flute above violin I, bassoon below). *Why:* sounds well in soft dynamics.
- R2 [MUST] In loud passages, double the woodwind part itself (2+ players) when it doubles strings at the octave, or it will not balance the strings.
- R3 [ALLOWED] Unison doubling of a string part by a woodwind is common; every combination works wherever the ranges coincide. Especially congenial unisons to try across the wind's range: violin + flute, viola + English horn, cello + bass clarinet, double bass + contrabassoon.
- R4 [PREFER] Judge each string+wind unison by the music being played and by register: the same pair gives different colors at different dynamics and parts of the range.
- R5 [ALLOWED] One woodwind added to a string part mainly strengthens the string tone; at a fairly loud dynamic its own color is largely absorbed.
- R6 [ALLOWED] Two woodwinds on a string part: the wind color starts to be clearly heard. Use this to tint a string line audibly.

**Rules — brass + woodwind**
- R7 [PREFER] Use horns as the link between woodwind and brass: their tone blends with woodwinds far better than trumpets/trombones.
- R8 [ALLOWED] In *piano*, octave doublings between trumpet and flute, and between horn and flute, oboe or clarinet, sound good.
- R9 [MUST] In *forte*, treat high woodwinds as reinforcement of the brass's upper partials, and give them as much doubling as available if you want any approach to balance with the brass.
- R10 [PREFER] In brass *forte*, put bassoons and bass clarinet on the bass line with tuba, trombone III and horn IV. *Why:* they are too low to reinforce brass upper partials.
- R11 [ALLOWED] Clarinets/oboes in unison with trumpets add tone-weight. [AVOID] when you want the trumpet's brilliance: the doubling dulls it.
- R12 [PREFER] Horns in unison with clarinets or bassoons: effective blends.
- R13 [AVOID] Woodwind unisons with trombones: seldom useful.
- R14 [ALLOWED] Tuba in unison with bass clarinet or contrabassoon combines well.

**Rules — strings, woodwind and brass together**
- R15 [PREFER] When a full tutti is not required, score a melody for only part of the three groups, even thinly, using pure colors. *Why:* clearer, more transparent sonority.
- R16 [ALLOWED] Thick tutti fortissimo concentrated in one octave (Fig. 136a): nearly all instruments in unison on two adjacent octaves. To keep it concentrated, omit the lowest octave (double basses, contrabassoon), the tuba and the piccolo. Omit trumpets II and III so the whole does not sound purely brass (especially where the melody rises).
- R17 [ALLOWED] Brilliant tutti spread over four octaves (Fig. 136b): every instrument used, each octave doubled across all three families (see Examples).
- R18 [ALLOWED] Add unison or octave doublings to only part of a melody, for intensity, volume, change of color or a sudden accent.
- R19 [PREFER] In a gradual crescendo, let doublings accumulate as it grows (add instruments progressively).
- R20 [MUST] When a line spans a range wider than one instrument, hand it from one color to another.
- R21 [PREFER] Make the hand-off where the two instruments' registers do not contrast sharply, and overlap the two parts by at least one note (Fig. 137).

**Examples**
- Fig. 135 (Problem 4, Moderato sostenuto, ff, 4/4, C major): melody C5–C5 | F5 E5 D5 | C5 B4 G5 | C6 B5 A5 … ending B4; to be scored for strings, woodwind and brass.
- Fig. 136a (concentrated, one octave, all at actual pitch): upper line (around C4–F4) 2 oboes + English horn + 2 clarinets, trumpet I + horns I/III + trombone I, violins I/II + violas; lower octave (around C3) 2 bassoons + bass clarinet, 2 trombones + horns II/IV, cellos. No basses, contrabassoon, tuba, piccolo, trumpets II/III.
- Fig. 136b (four octaves, C2–C6 on the first note): top octave piccolo + 2 flutes and violin I; next octave oboe I + 2 clarinets, 2 trumpets, violin II; next English horn + oboe II + bassoon I, trumpet III + horns I/III, violas; next bass clarinet + bassoon II, horns II/IV + 3 trombones, cellos; bottom contrabassoon, tuba, double basses. Each family alone spans all octaves of its share; the melody is harmonized in octave-doubled chords from bar 2 [notes partly uncertain].
- Fig. 137 (6/8 chromatic run): bass clarinet → bassoon → clarinet II → clarinet I → oboe → flute, each entry overlapping the previous instrument's last note(s): a model of joining colors across about four octaves.
- Problems 5–8 (Fig. 138–141): further melodies to score for mixed groups (Allegretto leggiero mf; Moderato 6/8 p; Largo in alto clef, f; Allegro scherzando 3/8 mf). Exercises only.

**Agent applicability**
- For a melody doubled by winds over strings, count the dynamic: p → one wind per octave is enough; f → double the wind line (a2) or move it to unison.
- For a brass-led forte, put flutes/oboes/clarinets on top as upper-partial reinforcement and send bassoons/bass clarinet to the bass with tuba/trb III/hn IV.
- When a run or melody crosses instrument ranges, split it across tracks with a 1-note overlap at each hand-off, at a point where the two registers are close in color.
- Choose tutti layout by intent: "thick/massive" → two adjacent octaves, no extreme low or high instruments; "brilliant" → 4–5 octaves with every family covering every octave.

## Ch. 27 Background and Accompaniment (scan s215R–s221R, printed p.431–443)

**Concepts**
- Background / accompaniment (fondo / acompañamiento): supplies tonal orientation, harmonic color (choice of chord structures), harmonic rhythm (pattern of root changes), and the rhythmic pulse that sets the music's underlying rate of motion; it also sets mood and atmosphere.
- Transparency (transparencia): an elaborate accompaniment through which a melody played softly by one or two instruments is still perfectly heard; the sonority is heard as the sum of many details (Piston points to Debussy, *La Mer*, Fig. 385 in Ch. 20).
- Subelement (subelemento): a separable layer of the accompaniment with its own rhythm/rests/register.

**Rules**
- R22 [MUST] Treat the accompaniment's rhythmic style and texture as part of the composition, not as an orchestration add-on: it can change what the melody means.
- R23 [AVOID] Orchestral "effects" irrelevant to the musical meaning; orchestration should confirm the accompaniment's harmonic and rhythmic qualities.
- R24 [ALLOWED] Contrast melody and accompaniment in tone color to detach the melody from the background; this is one means, not a requirement.
- R25 [ALLOWED] Melody with accompaniment is most often all strings; less often all woodwind or all brass.
- R26 [NEVER] Let the accompaniment overwhelm the melody in sound.
- R27 [ALLOWED] An elaborate accompaniment under a soft solo melody, if transparent (thin layers, rests, light articulation).
- R28 [PREFER] Derive accompaniment figuration from the techniques of the instruments, not from piano writing. *Why:* orchestral figuration and idiom originate in the instruments' techniques.
- R29 [ALLOWED] For strings alone, the straightforward layout of a 4-voice harmonic skeleton works very well: soprano → vn I, alto → vn II, tenor → va, bass → vc, with or without basses.
- R30 [PREFER] For a lighter texture with a sense of motion from the start, break the skeleton into an off-beat figure: vn II and va play detached notes on the weak beats (rest–note–rest–note) while cellos sustain the bass and basses add sparse pizzicato (Fig. 143).
- R31 [PREFER] Split a light accompaniment into two subelements that rest at different points (e.g. violins I+II vs. violas+cellos) for transparency (Fig. 144).
- R32 [ALLOWED] Omit double basses in a light, transparent accompaniment; often done.
- R33 [ALLOWED] Double a woodwind melody partially (for a few bars) with another wind, e.g. flute an octave above, for variety (Fig. 144).
- R34 [ALLOWED] A melody on divided first violins in octaves plus a solo cello an octave below gives a thinner but more intense and personal tone than full violins I+II with the full cello section (Fig. 145).
- R35 [ALLOWED] Give the bass line to bass clarinet, partly doubled by English horn and bassoon, under a string melody; clarinets and flutes supply motion and the remaining harmony (Fig. 145).
- R36 [ALLOWED] Let the cadence bars become four-voice part writing, after melody-and-accompaniment (Fig. 145).
- R37 [ALLOWED] For a warmer, ampler sound, double the melody in three octaves (flute I, violin I, cello + horn), strongest in the horn/cello register (Fig. 146).
- R38 [ALLOWED] Vary a melody's scoring within the phrase: pass a motive to the high flute with oboe an octave below; swap horn for English horn on the cello line; finally gather the melody into one octave (vn I + ob II + vc + Eng hn) (Fig. 146).
- R39 [ALLOWED] Use flutes briefly to draw attention to a secondary voice (Fig. 146).
- R40 [PREFER] Build a syncopated accompaniment motive from chord tones but give it its own melodic curve (Fig. 146: clarinets I/II + violins II div.). Add horns to it at the cadence; add a second motive (violas) marking beat 2.
- R41 [AVOID] Basses far below the rest of the texture in a light piece: low double basses can make the bass too heavy. Doubling them with bassoons clarifies the tone and bridges the gap; putting them an octave higher instead raises the bass's melodic intensity (Fig. 146).
- R42 [PREFER] When rescoring, if a melody sits high for its original instrument (e.g. high horn), move it to an instrument where the same pitch is idiomatic and expressive: Fig. 148 gives the Brahms horn tune (about A4 concert, converted from horn in D) to cellos high on the A string, in unison with violas who are not on their A string, which tempers the intensity.
- R43 [ALLOWED] Replace held string harmony with a full horn quartet when the strings take the melody (Fig. 148).
- R44 [AVOID] Doubling a melody's first note with a solo horn when the melody is in the strings: the horn would stand out too much against the violins (Fig. 148).
- R45 [PREFER] Pair a double-bass pedal with the octave of horn IV and contrabassoon (Fig. 148: pedal A).
- R46 [PREFER] Give an arpeggio figure to a passed-around ensemble of woodwinds (bassoons, clarinets, flute) rather than one solo instrument when the melody is on a section (vas + vcs): a more impersonal, style-appropriate effect (Fig. 148).
- R47 [ALLOWED] Rescoring practice: keep the musical style but redistribute the two elements radically (melody to woodwind instead of strings, etc.). Changing dynamic (p → ff) or transposing up a fifth forces real rescoring adjustments.

**Examples**
- Fig. 142 (Problem 9, Allegro grazioso, G minor, cut time): melody (A) from G4 rising to D5, over a 4-part harmonic skeleton (B) on a G2 bass, p. Solutions (a)–(d) below.
- Fig. 143, strings alone: vn I melody p; vn II and va off-beat staccato quarter notes (beats 2 and 4) under slurs; vc held bass from G2; db pizz on beat 1 only in some bars (R30).
- Fig. 144, melody in woodwind: clarinet I and bassoon I in octaves, p dolce; flute I joins an octave higher for a few bars; strings p with staccato chordal quarters in two subelements (vn I+II; va+vc with rests in different places); db only near the cadence (R31–R33).
- Fig. 145, melody in strings: vn I div. in octaves + solo cello an octave below; bass clarinet bass, partly doubled by English horn and bassoon; clarinets give detached off-beat chords, flutes harmonic fill; four-voice cadence (R34–R36).
- Fig. 146, groups mixed with horns: melody fl I / vn I / vc + hn I; syncopated accompaniment cl I–II + vn II div.; va motive on beat 2; bassoons a2 + basses on the bass; horns II join at the cadence (R37–R41).
- Fig. 147 Brahms, Symphony no. 2 (Allegro non troppo, D major): horn I in D carries the melody p; strings hold p sempre harmony with a cello (later violin) rising 8th-note arpeggio. Fig. 148 "typical solution": melody in vas + vcs, held harmony in 4 horns, db + c-bn + hn IV pedal A, arpeggio shared by bassoons, bass clarinet, clarinets and flute (R42–R46).
- Fig. 149 Mendelssohn, Symphony no. 3 "Scottish" (Adagio cantabile, A major, 2/4): melody p over a pp accompaniment of repeated 16th-note chords plus held harmony; to be rescored and compared with the original.
- Fig. 150 Mozart, Symphony in D, K.504 "Prague" (Allegro): a string phrase (vn I melody, repeated 8ths/quarters in vn II, va, vc-db) to be rescored for woodwinds alone, one version adding two horns.
- Fig. 151 Tchaikovsky, Symphony no. 4 (Moderato con anima, 9/8, f espr.): melody in fl/cl/bn a2 in octaves over mf repeated-16th string chords. Rescoring tasks include woodwinds only in close position within a two-octave total spread at p; woodwind + horns at f; melody in strings + woodwind with accompaniment in brass at ff.
- Fig. 152 (Problem 14, Lento, 12/8 pp): ornate melody over a soft, rhythmic chordal background; to be scored once with flute and once with solo violin.
- Exercise types suggested (one-line pointer): rescore from a reduction and compare; orchestrate your own melody + harmonic skeleton; same with a prescribed figuration; rescore a literature passage by moving the elements to other families.

**Agent applicability**
- Before writing accompaniment MIDI, decide its harmonic rhythm and pulse rate: they define the piece's motion as much as the melody does.
- For a light string accompaniment, write vn II/va as off-beat staccato notes (velocity low, short duration), vc held bass, db pizz on downbeats only or omitted.
- Give each accompaniment layer different rest positions so the texture breathes and the melody stays audible.
- For an intimate string melody, use "vn I div. octaves + solo cello" (a solo-cello track) instead of full sections.
- When the double-bass line sits far below everything else, double it with bassoon(s) at pitch, or write it an octave higher.
- When a melody moves to strings, a horn quartet makes a good held-chord pad; do not let one horn double the melody's first note.

## Ch. 28 Scoring of Chords, start (scan s222L–s222R, printed p.444–445)

**Concepts**
- Balanced chord (acorde equilibrado): ideally a flat vertical plane of sound, no voice sticking out. Piston doubts this can be fully achieved with real instruments and the chord tones a style gives.
- Numerical balance (equilibrio numérico): giving the same number of instruments to each chord note. This alone does not ensure balance.

**Rules**
- R48 [PREFER] Score chords from a remembered sound of each instrument at that pitch and dynamic, not from formulas; a voicing that works in one musical situation may fail in another.
- R49 [AVOID] Assuming one instrument per chord note means balance (Fig. 153a is "quite badly balanced").
- R50 [AVOID] At *f*, brass on the high chord notes above low flutes: the brass overpowers, the low flutes are weak, and an oboe note stands out against the second flute and a soft bassoon (Fig. 153a).
- R51 [PREFER] Rearrange the same instruments so the flutes are on top and the brass lower; put oboe and bassoon in octaves on the same pitch class (C). The brass is then less strident and the flutes are heard (Fig. 153b). Note: this gives up the rough, strident quality of (a), which may be wanted.
- R52 [MUST] At *forte*, expect woodwind to remain weaker than brass even after rearranging; add woodwind unison doublings to approach the brass's tone-weight (Fig. 153c: 2 flutes, 2 oboes, 2 clarinets, 2 bassoons against single trumpet, horn, trombone).
- R53 [ALLOWED] One horn cannot equal the *forte* of a trumpet or trombone. Two horns are better, but that only makes the brass overpower the woodwind further.
- R54 [ALLOWED] Count each string section (vn I, vn II, va, vc) as one instrument when computing balance (Fig. 153d), the composers' usual practice, with a wide margin of error: section sizes vary, a divided section counts as a half/third of a section (one "instrument" except when compared with an undivided section), and a solo violin also counts as one instrument (cf. Ch. 2, p.63).

**Examples**
- Fig. 153 (f, the same chord four ways): (a) trumpet/horn/trombone high, 2 flutes low, oboe, bassoon; (b) 2 flutes on top, oboe, trumpet, horn, trombone, bassoon below; (c) 2 fl, 2 ob, 2 cl added in unisons over trumpet, horn, trombone, 2 bassoons; (d) strings: vn I top, vn II div., va, vc. Exact pitches not transcribed [?].

**Agent applicability**
- In a mixed-wind forte chord, never put single woodwinds against brass note-for-note; put woodwinds on top and double them (a2) or let brass carry the chord with winds reinforcing above.
- For balance math, treat each string section track as weight 1, a div. half-section as about 0.5 against an undivided section, and a solo string as 1 [weights inferred].
