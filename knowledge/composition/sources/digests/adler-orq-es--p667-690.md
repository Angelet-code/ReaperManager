# Adler, El estudio de la orquestación — scans 667–690 (printed p.666–689)

## 17 Transcription for orchestra — introduction (scan p.667–669, printed p.666–668)

**Concepts**
- Transcription (transcripción): a literal transfer of an already-composed work from one medium to another.
- Arrangement (arreglo): a larger compositional process; the given material may be as little as a melody (or
  part of one), to which the arranger supplies harmony, counterpoint and sometimes a distinctive rhythmic
  setting *before* orchestrating.
- Three areas covered by the chapter: (1) keyboard or small chamber combinations → orchestra; (2) band /
  wind ensemble → orchestra; (3) transcription for whatever combination happens to be available (school
  ensembles, incomplete instrumentation, players of limited ability). Transcription *for* band is ch.19.

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] For an arrangement, first harmonize the melody and write the whole piece out in piano score or
  condensed score (partitura general abreviada); only then orchestrate it with the same procedure as a
  transcription.
- R2 [MUST] Before transcribing, have: (a) thorough knowledge of every instrument involved, in the source
  and the target medium, including the character of each register; (b) intimate knowledge of the piece's
  structure and formal details; (c) understanding of the composer's orchestral style, or — if the composer
  never wrote for orchestra — familiarity with the orchestral practice of the composer's period; (d) love for
  the work; (e) a valid reason to transcribe it.
- R3 [PREFER] Valid reasons: the work calls for orchestral colour; the composer intended but never managed to
  orchestrate it; a conductor wants it for a given orchestral medium; instruments of the original are not
  available.
- R4 [MUST] Taste governs: respect the work, the composer and the period, but decide with common sense about
  (a) the size and makeup of the target orchestra, (b) whether a given instrument suits a given phrase,
  (c) whether the orchestration clarifies the form.
- R5 [ALLOWED] [era: Baroque] Re-scoring a piece for whatever instruments were available was normal practice;
  Baroque composers often did not specify the instrument, only a general indication such as "any instrument
  in C" (cualquier instrumento en DO). Transcribing Baroque music to other forces is therefore idiomatic to the
  period, not a violation of it.
- R6 [ALLOWED] [era: 19th c.] Transcription to piano 2/4 hands, two pianos, violin + piano etc. was the main
  way amateurs got to know masterworks at home; conversely many standard orchestral works began as piano
  pieces.

**Examples** (what each demonstrates)
- Bach: transcriptions of Vivaldi violin concertos; own violin concertos turned keyboard concertos (E major
  violin concerto → D major keyboard concerto: transposed for the new instrument); own instrumental movements
  reused as cantata sinfonias.
- Handel: 2nd movt of his D major violin sonata reused as chorus + orchestra in *Solomon*.
- Beethoven: his Violin Concerto transcribed for piano and orchestra.
- Liszt: all Beethoven symphonies for piano; Brahms, Dvořák, Grieg: their own Hungarian / Slavonic /
  Norwegian dances orchestrated from piano 4-hands originals.
- Liszt *Hungarian Rhapsodies*, Bizet *Jeux d'Enfants*, almost all of Ravel: orchestral repertoire born at the
  piano.
- Mussorgsky/Ravel *Pictures at an Exhibition* (arguably more effective than the piano original); Husa
  *Music for Prague* (band original, arguably better for orchestra); Schoenberg *Variations* Op.43 (arguably
  better in the band original than the orchestral version): the direction of transcription is not a value
  judgement.

**Agent applicability**
- When asked to "orquestar" a piano piece or a lead sheet: if only a melody exists, it is an arrangement —
  generate full harmony/counterpoint as a piano or short score first, validate it, then orchestrate.
- Before orchestrating, decide the target ensemble size (chamber vs full orchestra) and check every line's
  register against `data/instruments.json`.
- For Baroque material, free re-assignment of lines to available instruments is period-correct.

## 17 Keyboard to orchestra — principles 1–3 (scan p.669–672, printed p.668–671)

Context: keyboard → orchestra is the most frequent change of medium. The orchestra cannot imitate the piano,
but piano originals can yield very idiomatic orchestral music. Ravel wrote all but three of his brilliant
orchestral works first for piano; Stravinsky wrote his first ballets first for piano (possibly only because a
rehearsal version was needed).

**Rules**
- R7 [NEVER] Do not try to imitate the piano. Translate the pianistic idiom into the various orchestral idioms
  while keeping the spirit of the music (principle 1).
- R8 [ALLOWED] The orchestral version may thicken the texture of the piano original (more chord tones, wider
  doublings) to make a more exuberant tutti (Ex.17-1).
- R9 [ALLOWED] A secondary melody in the piano left hand need not be carried over literally; contrast can come
  from instrumental colour instead (Ex.17-2).
- R10 [MUST] Remember the piano is one player and the orchestra many: problems that never trouble a pianist
  can appear in the orchestral version. Simplify rhythm, change notation, or re-bar the music when needed for
  ensemble clarity (principle 2).
- R11 [PREFER] Re-bar long irregular measures into shorter ones so the conductor beats a downbeat more often
  (Ex.17-3: 5/4 + 4/4 bars regrouped as 3/4, 2/4, 4/4).
- R12 [MUST] Write crescendo, diminuendo, rubato and even fermatas into the orchestral texture itself; they
  become more evident that way (principle 3).
- R13 [PREFER] Crescendo = add instruments; diminuendo = remove instruments.
- R14 [ALLOWED] Diminuendo/slowing can also be written by lengthening note values (16ths → triplet 8ths →
  8ths), i.e. a written-out rhythmic deceleration (Ex.17-4).
- R15 [ALLOWED] Crescendo can be built by staggered entries plus rhythmic activation: repeated-note triplets in
  the horns under a growing string texture, winds entering one by one, full tutti at the *ff* goal (Ex.17-5).

**Examples**
- Ravel, *Ma Mère l'Oye*, "Le Jardin féerique", bars 20–23 (Lent et grave, q = 56), Ex.17-1: piano block
  chords become full orchestral chords; on the held chord woodwinds and strings sustain while a harp line
  moves through the bass; the climax chord is filled out across the whole range — texture thickened for a
  richer tutti.
- Ravel, *Menuet antique*, bars 64–66 (Lent), Ex.17-2: the piano's pedalled bass and left-hand line become
  tied sustained notes in the low strings/winds plus a separate moving inner line; the left-hand "melody" is
  not literally reproduced; colour supplies the contrast.
- Bartók, *Mikrokosmos*, "Syncopation" (Sincopación), bars 1–3 (Allegro, q = 152), Ex.17-3: piano original in
  5/4–4/4 alternation re-barred for orchestra as 3/4 – 2/4 – 4/4 so the accents fall on downbeats.
- Mahler, *Lieder eines fahrenden Gesellen* No.1 "Wenn mein Schatz", bars 89–96 (Schneller), Ex.17-4:
  bass clarinet/viola/cello figure slows from 16ths to triplet 8ths to 8ths while dynamics fall (dim. →
  *pp*); flute ends *pppp*; harp harmonics (Flag.), violin I harmonic on the IV string *pp*, double basses
  pizz. *pp* triplets — a written-out diminuendo and ritardando.
- Beethoven, Symphony No.6, i, bars 305–312 (Allegro moderato), Ex.17-5: strings start *pp*; horns enter
  *p cresc.* with repeated triplet chords; violin II, then 1st clarinet and 1st bassoon, then oboe join;
  the goal bar is *ff* tutti including flute — crescendo by accumulation.
- Bizet, *Jeux d'Enfants*, "La Toupie", bars 1–5 (Allegro vivo), Ex.17-6: an *ff* string attack spread over
  octaves; violin I drops out after bar 2, violin II after bar 3, cello after bar 4 (then pizz. *p*), viola
  continues *dim.* to *pp* — diminuendo by subtraction. (Whole movement: Ex.17-11b, printed p.691.)

**Agent applicability**
- In MIDI, do not rely only on CC1/CC11 ramps for big cresc./dim.: also stagger entries (add tracks one by
  one for a crescendo) and drop tracks out one by one for a diminuendo.
- A written-out ritardando: replace a regular 16th figure with triplet 8ths then 8ths over 2–4 bars instead of
  (or before) a tempo change.
- A piano fermata on a decaying chord → in orchestra give it a measured length (see R19).
- If the generated meter alternates long odd bars (5/4, 7/4), consider re-barring into 2+3 / 3+2 groupings
  matching the accents; the bridge supports `timesig` per tempo change.

## 17 Keyboard to orchestra — principles 4–6 (scan p.673–676, printed p.672–675)

**Rules**
- R16 [MUST] Interpret important piano indications so the transcription respects the composer's intentions
  (principle 4). E.g. *una corda* (soft pedal: the action shifts so hammers strike one or two of three strings)
  → assign mutes (sordinas) to the orchestral instruments in that passage.
- R17 [MUST] Know the music completely — all harmonies and melodic lines — and study the layers of linear
  activity, so that thematic material of inner and outer voices that the piano only sketches can be
  orchestrated more fully (principle 5).
- R18 [MUST] Identify piano writing imposed by the physical limits of one pianist. Chords arpeggiated only
  because of hand span may become block (placed) chords in the orchestra.
- R19 [PREFER] Notes sustained by the piano's damper (resonance) pedal become written-out held note values in
  the orchestra (e.g. dotted quarters) (Ex.17-7); a piano fermata on a decaying chord can be written as a fixed
  number of beats roughly equal to the chord's decay time (Ex.17-8).
- R20 [AVOID] Colourist ideas that obscure the form or alter the musical scheme — notably changing instrument
  colour in the middle of a melodic phrase or fugue subject (principle 6). *Why:* the orchestra's unlimited
  colours tempt the transcriber, but the line must stay intelligible.
- R21 [ALLOWED] Exception to R20: when the texture is itself pointillistic, or a contemporary melodic device
  (e.g. timbre-changing melody) is intended (inferred example: Klangfarbenmelodie).
- R22 [MUST] Rank the layers of the music by importance before orchestrating — this is central whether the
  piece is mainly homophonic or polyphonic.
- R23 [ALLOWED] In the orchestra there is no natural decay to fight: where the pianist must re-strike a chord to
  keep it sounding (e.g. repeating it in another octave), the orchestra can simply hold very powerful sustained
  chords in winds and strings.
- R24 [ALLOWED] Do not imitate a closing piano tremolo, and the orchestral ending need not keep the piano's
  final fermata (Ravel's practice in Ex.17-8).
- R25 [PREFER] Woodwinds doubled at both the upper and lower octave give a fuller, more coloured sound for a
  crescendo (Ex.17-7).

**Examples**
- Chopin, Étude Op.25 No.6 (thirds), bars 39–40 (marked Andante in the example), Ex.17-7: the right-hand
  trilling thirds go to 2 flutes + 2 oboes + 2 clarinets in thirds (octave-spread), *f* → *ff*; the pedalled
  middle notes become dotted-quarter off-beat string chords; 2 bassoons take a bass line; the piano's
  sustain-pedal sonority is now explicit note lengths.
- Mussorgsky/Ravel, *Pictures at an Exhibition*, "The Great Gate of Kiev": piano original last 15 bars vs
  Ravel's last 22 bars (reh.121, Maestoso, 2/2), Ex.17-8: piano fermata chords become 4 half-note beats each;
  where Mussorgsky re-strikes each chord in two octaves (7 bars from the end), Ravel (9 bars from the end)
  holds massive sustained chords in woodwinds and strings; full tutti ff: 3 fl, 3 ob, 2 cl + bass cl, 2 bsn +
  contrabassoon, 4 hn, trumpets, 3 trb + tuba, timpani, triangle, cymbals, bass drum, tam-tam (vibrato),
  chimes, 2 harps, divided strings with grace-note attacks on chord entries; timpani and cymbal (with stick,
  "avec baguette") rolls on the penultimate held chord, then one short accented final chord — no tremolo, no
  final fermata.

**Agent applicability**
- When converting a piano sketch to tracks: (1) list layers (melody, counter-lines, bass, harmony fill,
  rhythm), rank them, (2) assign one timbre per phrase for each melody — switch colour at phrase boundaries,
  not mid-phrase.
- Sustain-pedal regions in piano MIDI (CC64) → convert to explicit held notes in sustaining instruments;
  drop CC64 for orchestral tracks.
- Rolled/arpeggiated piano chords (strum offsets) → quantize to block chords in the orchestra unless the
  arpeggio is thematic.
- *una corda* / soft passages marked as such → use muted (con sord.) patches if available.
- Grand ending: hold the climactic chord with measured length, add timp + suspended cymbal rolls under it,
  end on a short accented tutti chord.

## 17 Famous models (modelos notorios del repertorio): Brahms, Hungarian Dance No.1 (scan p.677–682, printed p.676–681)

Context: the best way to learn piano → orchestra transcription is to study masters' own transcriptions;
the examples are mostly by the composers themselves.

**Rules / techniques shown (Ex.17-9, bars 1–48, Allegro molto, G minor, 2/4; piano 4-hands vs orchestra)**
- R26 [ALLOWED] A transcriber may raise dynamics for intensity: opening passage (from the secondo) mf → *f*;
  woodwind entry at bar 5 (from the primo) *p* → *f*.
- R27 [PREFER] Melody in violins (*espress. e vibrato*), doubled at the lower octave by bassoons.
- R28 [ALLOWED] Add a rhythmic layer absent from the piano: strong pizzicato in basses and cellos carries the
  Hungarian rhythm; in bars 5–6 violas join cellos to articulate the off-beat (contratiempo) rhythm.
- R29 [PREFER] Vary string weight by section: in bars 1–4 the cello distributes (arpeggiates) chord tones
  across each bar; in bars 5–6 it gives full chords on strong beats with double stops, joined by viola. So
  bars 1–4 and 7–10 feel light, bars 5–6 heavier and more stable to support the woodwind arpeggio figuration.
- R30 [PREFER] Woodwind arpeggio with imagination: piccolo + flute 1 play it while the two clarinets
  "flutter" (revolotean) in contrary motion; staccato notes go to flute 2 and bassoon 1, octave-doubled in
  bar 6.
- R31 [MUST] Respect range floors when octave-doubling: the bassoon completes the descending arpeggio to Bb but
  the flute does not, because it cannot (inferred: the flute's octave would be Bb3 = MIDI 58, below the flute's
  C4 = 60 floor). The bassoon's descending arpeggio in bar 5 is an addition not in the piano version.
- R32 [ALLOWED] In the parallel passage (bar 12) the bassoon instead reinforces flute 1's notes — same idea,
  different doubling.
- R33 [PREFER] Horns with timpani roll (and triangle) add colour to the violins' held note and reinforce the
  harmony (bar 5).
- R34 [PREFER] Keep one colour combination consistent through a section (Adler's main point: Brahms keeps the
  same colour combination for long stretches; note the flute ↔ bassoon exchange at bar 24).
- R35 [PREFER] At a structural change (bar 25) change the colour: violas take the secondo's triplets (divided,
  *p*); the primo's mf melody is orchestrated by doubling violins with horns and oboes (oboe *p espress.*,
  horns *p espress.*); cellos pizz. *marcato*, basses arco.
- [era: late-19th-c. orchestra] Score uses horns in C and E♭ (mixed crooks) and trumpet in D; transpose
  accordingly when reading the parts, MIDI stays concert.

**Agent applicability**
- Dance/folk transcription recipe: melody vln I+II (unison) + bassoon 8vb; pizz. cello/bass on the dance
  rhythm; viola on off-beats; winds take decorative arpeggios with picc+fl vs clarinets in contrary motion;
  hn + timp roll + triangle reinforce held notes.
- Always check every octave-doubled note against each instrument's range; let the lower instrument finish a
  line alone when the upper one runs out of range.

## 17 Famous models: Dvořák, Slavonic Dance No.8 (scan p.682–689, printed p.681–688)

**Rules / techniques shown (Ex.17-10, bars 1–48, Presto, G minor, 3/4; piano 4-hands vs orchestra)**
- R36 [PREFER] Use open strings for resonance in the opening tutti chord: violin II grace notes and a cello
  arpeggiated (rolled) chord, both on open strings.
- R37 [ALLOWED] Winds may switch doubling role every two bars: oboe 1 and clarinet 2 move from doubling
  violin II's harmony notes to doubling violin I's melody notes.
- R38 [PREFER] Emphasize the main melodic figure with many doublings: piccolo, flute, violin I, plus oboe 1 and
  clarinet 2 in bars 3–4; full brass chords, timpani, cymbal + bass drum colour the tutti (*ff*).
- R39 [PREFER] Contrast section (bar 9): drop to *p*; the rustic syncopated tune goes to woodwinds only
  (which repeat it *p*); a new sustained countermelody in the high strings (violins *Sul G*, *p espress.*,
  viola) reinforces the off-beats of the Slavonic rhythm — this countermelody does not exist in the piano
  original. Horns (off-beat chords), woodwinds and low strings (cello pizz.) give light accompaniment;
  triangle on beat 1.
- R40 [ALLOWED] On the repeat of the first phrase (bar 17) open up the chord spacing (especially horns and
  middle strings) to give the tutti a slightly different character without changing the basic colour.
- R41 [PREFER] Mirror the piano's imitative entries: the delayed oboe entry at bar 25 imitates the delayed
  left-hand melody entry of the primo; a new colour at bar 29 reinforces the answer's polyphonic nature.
- R42 [PREFER] Diminuendo ff → p (bars 33–35) by withdrawing many doubling instruments (cf. R13).
- R43 [PREFER] Give continuously playing sections a rest: the strings, playing nonstop since bar 1, drop out at
  bars 33–35 — gaining relief and (Adler suggests) preparing their supporting role in the next section
  (bar 41: violin II + viola 8th-note figure *p*, cello/bass pizz.).
- R44 [PREFER] For a soft dynamic, write instruments in registers where they can control it: the piccolo part is
  written an octave lower so the player can drop to *p* more easily; flutes, oboes, clarinets, horns and
  trombones from bar 34 are likewise placed where dynamics are easier to control.
- R45 [ALLOWED] Use *fz* accents in bassoons/cello/horns on the off-beat or second-bar accents of the dance
  (bars 41–48).

**Agent applicability**
- For a soft passage after a loud tutti, transpose piccolo/flute down an octave rather than just lowering CC1:
  high piccolo cannot play truly *p*.
- Thin the tutti for a diminuendo by muting doubling tracks (keep the core melody + bass), and give the
  strings a few bars' rest before an accompaniment section.
- A repeated phrase can be refreshed by re-voicing (wider spacing in horns/violas) with the same instruments.

## 17 Famous models: Bizet, Jeux d'Enfants, "La Toupie" (scan p.690, printed p.689; full score Ex.17-11b on printed p.691, outside this range)

Context: another piano 4-hands original; Bizet's orchestration is simple but delicate and full of reusable
devices.

**Rules / techniques shown**
- R46 [PREFER] Distribute the opening octaves among the string sections so a diminuendo results as the parts
  drop out (bars 2–5; cf. Ex.17-6, R13).
- R47 [ALLOWED] Animate a pedal with a playful rhythm: the E pedal (bars 5–8) is reinforced by the 3rd horn on
  beat 2 — a beat stressed neither in the piano primo nor in the orchestral flute part; from bar 9 this gives
  a light off-beat accent.
- R48 [ALLOWED] Respell enharmonically for the orchestral part: in bar 7 Bizet writes the secondo's E♭ as D♯
  (book: MI♭3 → RE♯3; octave-number convention of the translation not verified [?]).
- R49 [PREFER] Crescendo by adding instruments (from bar 16).
- R50 [ALLOWED] Add motion under a sustained chord: on the held chord of bar 28 Bizet adds a "whispering"
  tritone (6 semitones) in the cellos and a timpani roll — and lowers the marking from *pp* to *ppp* to
  compensate for the added activity.
- R51 [PREFER] Overlap string parts in triplet scales (bar 31) so the motion runs continuously from low to high
  (each section picks up before the previous one ends).
- R52 [PREFER] On a repeat (from bar 37) make small but significant doubling changes for a fuller texture:
  viola 16ths doubled by cellos throughout bars 31–63; flute staccato melody doubled in unison by pizzicato
  violins from bar 40; the clarinets' descending chromatic scale (bars 38–41) doubled at the lower octave by
  bassoons (in the piano it was loose staccato notes in the secondo left hand).
- R53 [ALLOWED] Pass a rhythmic idea to a new colour on the repeat: horn 4 keeps the E pedal while the trumpets
  take the rhythm formerly played by horn 3.
- R54 [ALLOWED] Recall a device with a new colour: ten bars before the end the cello tritone + timp roll
  returns, but the overlapping triplet scale is now in the woodwinds.
- R55 [PREFER] Imitate the piano's sustain pedal with held horn notes: while strings play the 16th pattern in
  octaves, horns 3 and 4 sustain the E pedal.
- R56 [PREFER] Traditional ending: effective spacing and octave doublings in the final chords; high woodwinds
  doubling the strings an octave above in the last two chords give a more colourful ending than the pianists
  could.

**Agent applicability**
- Pedal tones: put them in horns (sustained) and let another instrument articulate a rhythm on the pedal pitch.
- Under a long held chord, add a quiet inner motion (cello tritone oscillation, timp roll) and reduce the
  overall dynamic one step.
- String scale relays: start each section's scale before the previous one finishes (overlap by ~1 beat).
- On repeats, add unison/octave doublings (cello on viola, pizz violins on flute, bassoon 8vb on clarinet)
  rather than new material.
