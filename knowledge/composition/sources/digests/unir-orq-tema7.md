## 7.2 Classification (generalidades) (scan p.4–5)

**Concepts**
- Percussion = sound produced by striking one object against another.
- Pitched (altura determinada: timpani, glockenspiel…) vs unpitched (altura indeterminada: bass drum,
  cymbals…).
- Membranophones (membranófonos: drums, timpani) vs idiophones (idiófonos: wood/metal bodies that sound
  when struck).
- Orchestration-practical grouping:
  - Normal section: timpani, snare drum, bass drum, cymbals, triangle, tambourine, glockenspiel, wood
    block (caja china), tam-tam.
  - Auxiliary: castanets, xylophone, marimba, vibraphone, tubular bells, crotales.
  - Sound effects: sleigh bells (cascabeles), wind machine, ratchet (carraca), whip (látigo), cowbell
    (cencerro), anvil (yunque), siren.
  - "Exotic" (mainly Latin American): maracas, claves, güiro, bongos, tom-toms, temple blocks.

## 7.3 Timpani (timbales) (scan p.5–8)

**Concepts**
- Classical period: **two timpani tuned to tonic and dominant**, unchanged; 19th c.: three. Today 3–4
  (up to 5), retuned within a movement by **pedal**.
- Parts: head (parche), tuning gauge, copper bowl (caldera), pedal.
- Drum sizes and ranges (Fig. 2, Adler), sounding (non-transposing, bass clef):
  - 30": D2–A2 (38–45), optional C2 (36).
  - 28": F2–C3 (41–48).
  - 25": Bb2–F3 (46–53).
  - 23": D3–A3 (50–57).
  - 21": F3–B3 (53–59), optional C4 (60) [top notes approximate].
- Sticks (baquetas): wooden handle; heads of felt, flannel, cotton, wood, cork or sponge; small/medium/large,
  various hardness. Contemporary scores specify the stick.
- Roll (redoble) is written like a trill; state whether it is measured or unmeasured. Hands alternate;
  the stick rebounds; resonance is damped with the fingertips.

**Rules**
- R1 [PREFER] Tune each drum in the **middle of its range** (head neither too tight nor too slack) for the
  best tone.
- R2 [ALLOWED] Hard sticks: louder, clearer articulation. Soft sticks: warmer, more resonant.
- R3 [PREFER] Main role since Classicism: reinforce orchestral tutti, usually tied to the brass.
- R4 [ALLOWED] Exposed timpani alternating with the orchestra for big impact (R. Strauss, *Also sprach
  Zarathustra* opening: C–G strokes alone).
- Style fact: timpani are the only percussion present in orchestral scores of almost every period.

**Agent applicability**
- Classical style: 2 drums, tonic + dominant only (e.g., D major: A2 + D3), played with trumpets on
  cadences (inferred example).
- Modern/film: pick pitches that fall in mid-range of a real drum (e.g., 30" ~E2–G2, 23" ~E3–G3);
  avoid fast pitch changes without time to pedal.

## 7.4 Snare drum (tambor, caja; side/snare drum) (scan p.8–9)
- Snares (bordones/cuerdas) stretched under the lower head vibrate sympathetically. Upper head = batter
  head (parche batido), lower = snare head (parche de cuerdas).
- Normal size about 35–38 cm diameter, 15–20 cm deep; other sizes must be specified in the score
  (Stravinsky distinguishes small/large drums in *L'histoire du soldat*).
- Unpitched; written on a single line.
- Example: Ravel, *Boléro* — a two-bar snare ostinato held from start to finish (crescendo by everything
  else).

**Tags:** #instrument/snare-drum #ostinato

## 7.5 Bass drum (bombo; gran cassa) (scan p.9–10)
- Sizes 60–91 cm head (up to 1 m in bands); orchestral ~73 cm diameter, 40–65 cm wide; no snares;
  mounted on a stand. Felt-headed beater, usually right hand; left hand can damp → **notated durations
  are meaningful**.
- R1 [ALLOWED] Most powerful instrument of the orchestra.
- R2 [MUST] Players anticipate the beat because the drum speaks slightly late (inferred for MIDI: a
  sampled bass drum may need its note start nudged a few ms earlier if the sample has a slow attack).
- Roll: two timpani sticks or a double-headed beater. Written on one line. Effects with other sticks, wire
  brushes, objects.

**Tags:** #instrument/bass-drum

## 7.6 Cymbals (platillos) (scan p.10)
- Players normally have two pairs: 38–40 cm and ~45.7 cm. Sound metallic, brilliant.
- Clash is a glancing (not face-to-face) stroke; held up in the air they ring a long time — notate "let
  ring" (dejar vibrar) or a tie to nothing. **pp is possible** by pulling the plates apart sharply.
- Suspended cymbal (plato suspendido): great variety of colour; struck with wooden stick, timpani stick,
  triangle beater or fingers; chains/metal objects on it for effects; **two sticks give a roll (trino)**.
- R1 [ALLOWED] Suspended-cymbal roll from murmur to incandescent fortissimo — powerful orchestral crescendo
  booster.
- R2 [AVOID] Overusing the cymbal-roll crescendo (the source calls it perhaps overused).
- Written on a single line.

**Tags:** #instrument/cymbals #crescendo

## 7.7 Tam-tam (gong) (scan p.10–11)
- Bronze disk, wider than a cymbal, turned-up rim. Several sizes; composer specifies large/small.
- Soft stroke with a soft beater: a huge number of overtones; struck hard: extraordinary violence.
- Character (Piston): dramatic, evocative, shuddering, heavy. Different beaters = different colours.

**Tags:** #instrument/tam-tam

## 7.8 Small percussion (pequeña percusión) (scan p.11–12)
- Tambourine (pandereta): ~25 cm, single head, pairs of metal jingles in the frame.
- Wood block (caja china): rectangular wooden box with resonating slot; usually three sizes; wooden or
  xylophone mallets; **high, hard, dry** sound; much used by 20th-c. American composers.
- Castanets (castañuelas): orchestral pair mounted on a handle and shaken; not only for Spanish dances.
- Triangle: steel rod bent into an open equilateral triangle, ~16.5 cm; metal beater (wooden for softer
  sound). High, clear, luminous; **audible over the tutti**.
- R1 [AVOID] Overusing the triangle (use with moderation).

**Tags:** #orchestration/percussion #instrument/triangle #instrument/wood-block

## 7.9 Tubular bells (campanas) (scan p.12–13)
- Steel tubes, source says "25 to 51 cm" diameter (probably mm [?]), length varying with pitch; struck with a hammer near the
  top end.
- Range (Fig. 6): **C4 (60) – F5 (77)**, with F#5 and G5 (78–79) as extensions.
- Example: Berlioz, *Symphonie fantastique* V — bells written in double octaves (two staves, bass clef).

**Tags:** #instrument/tubular-bells #ranges

## 7.10 Mallet instruments (láminas) (scan p.13–16)

**Glockenspiel (carrillón)**
- About thirty steel bars in a portable case, laid out like a piano keyboard.
- Range (Fig. 8): written G3–C6; **sounds two octaves higher → G5 (79) – C8 (108)**.
- Mallets: hard or soft rubber, yarn, wood, metal. Usually single melodic lines, one mallet per hand.
- Style fact: in Mozart's time it was a keyboard instrument (*Magic Flute* Act I finale, Papageno's bells).

**Vibraphone**
- Popularised in North American dance bands. Steel bars, tube resonators, electric motor producing
  **vibrato at variable speeds**; damper pedal like the piano's.
- Ranges by model (Fig. 10, Adler), non-transposing: (1) **F3–F6 (53–89)**; (2) **C4–F6 (60–89)**;
  (3) **F3–C7 (53–96)**.

**Xylophone and marimba**
- Xylophone: rosewood bars with resonators below; range varies by model; **sounds an octave higher than
  written**. Technique like glockenspiel; sound dry, cutting, little resonance; usable for solo passages
  (Saint-Saëns, *Carnival of the Animals*, "Fossils").
- Marimba: a larger xylophone; warmer, more expressive. Typical range (Fig. 12): **A2 (45) – C7 (96)**
  (sounding as written).

**Agent applicability**
- Glockenspiel MIDI notes live at 79–108: if a sample library maps glock at written pitch, the agent must
  check whether the patch already transposes (+24).
- Sparkle doubling of a melody: glock 2 octaves up from the written line; xylophone for dry, rhythmic
  doubling; vibes for sustained, soft halos (motor vibrato).
