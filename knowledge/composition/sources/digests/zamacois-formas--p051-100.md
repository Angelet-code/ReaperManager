## Fugue: rules for the answer (respuesta) — §87–§92 (scan p.51–66, printed p.89–117)

**Concepts**
- Subject (sujeto, S), answer (respuesta, R), countersubject (contrasujeto, CS).
- Head (cabeza) of the subject: its opening segment, where tonic/dominant mutation decisions are made.
- Mutation (mutación): the point where the answer departs from exact transposition to keep the
  tonic↔dominant exchange.
- Tonal answer (respuesta tonal): answer with mutation(s). Real answer (respuesta real): exact
  transposition a 5th up (or 4th down). Plagal answer (respuesta plagal): opposes subdominant to tonic.
- Tonic key / dominant key (tono de la tónica / tono de la dominante): in a C major or C minor fugue the only
  two keys considered are C and G; any foreign accidental is read as an altered degree of one of them.

**Rules — which notes get exchanged (§87–§88)**
- R1 [MUST] If the subject begins and/or ends on the tonic, the answer puts the dominant there, and vice
  versa (tonic↔dominant exchange).
- R2 [MUST] If the subject does not start on the tonic but the tonic appears before the end of the head, that
  tonic is answered by the dominant (and vice versa). Later appearances are judged by R4.
- R3 [MUST] If, in the head, tonic is followed by dominant (directly or with notes between), answer with
  dominant→tonic (and vice versa).
- R4 [MUST] Elsewhere: tonic is answered by dominant only if that fragment is in the tonic key; dominant is
  answered by tonic only if that fragment is in the dominant key.
- R5 [MUST] Consider the dominant key "in force" inside the subject only when its characteristic
  accidentals are present or unequivocally implied by the harmonization; otherwise treat the fragment as
  tonic key. Fragments in the tonic key are answered in the dominant key (5th above, +7 semitones) and vice
  versa. *Example:* WTC I/7 (Eb): the second Eb functions as IV of the dominant key and is answered by Ab
  (IV of the tonic); of three Bb's, the first and last are answered by Eb, the middle one is not (tonic-key
  fragment).
- R6 [PREFER] Before writing an answer, bracket the subject into T (tonic-key) and D (dominant-key) fragments.
- R7 [MUST] A subject ending on the 3rd of the tonic (III) gets an answer ending on the 3rd of the dominant
  (VII): major 3rd in major, minor 3rd in minor (never the leading tone). C major: E→B; C minor: Eb→Bb.
- R8 [PREFER] Other opening degrees (Gedalge): II→VI, III→VII, leading tone→III (major) or raised IV
  (minor). General criterion at the subject's start: notes leading to the tonic belong to the tonic key; notes
  leading to the dominant belong to the dominant key. Ending on a degree other than T, D or their 3rds is
  almost unheard of in school fugue.

**Rules — where to place the mutation (§89)**
- R9 [MUST] Dominant key → tonic key anywhere in the subject: the dominant key ends at that dominant note; the
  next note is already tonic key unless characteristic alterations prolong the dominant key. (Confirmed in all
  eras; WTC II/1 is the only departure cited.)
- R10 [ALLOWED] If the dominant is immediately repeated, Bach mutates after the repeated note (respecting the
  repetition); an alternative mutates later to preserve a descending 5th leap.
- R11 [PREFER] If the subject returns almost immediately to the dominant key (Cherubini case), analyse the
  subject in the dominant key from note 2 — fewer mutations.
- R12 [MUST] Tonic → dominant key inside the head (Gedalge): if the last note of the group between the first
  tonic and the first dominant is a repetition of the tonic or its 3rd, the whole mediating group is tonic key;
  otherwise the whole mediating group is dominant key.
- R13 [AVOID] Major-mode subjects with direct mediant→dominant in descending motion: the answer gets a harsh
  major 7th leap (11 semitones). Avoid such subjects or accept the 7th as an exception.
- R14 [ALLOWED] Tonic → dominant after the head: no rule; two tendencies — mutate as late as possible
  (Reicha, De Sanctis) or as early as possible (Bach, Marpurg). The early tendency easily yields plagal
  answers but gives a more solid tonic-key entry. Decide by harmonizing (R18).
- R15 [MUST] If a chromatic-scale fragment (e.g. tonic→dominant chromatically) must be affected, mutate at
  the BEGINNING of the fragment, not in its middle or end. Consequence: a semitone may become a unison or an
  augmented 2nd (3 semitones) — so such subjects usually prefer a real answer (R24d).
- R16 [PREFER] Procedure for chromatic subjects: (a) delete chromatic notes; (b) answer the diatonic
  skeleton; (c) reinsert chromatic notes where they fit; where two notes would form a unison, repeat the
  diatonic note.
- R17 [MUST] The tonic/dominant-key opposition extends to HARMONY: every subject chord in the tonic key is
  answered by the chord on the same degree in the dominant key, and vice versa.
- R18 [PREFER] Harmonize subject and answer as the test of an answer; prefer the answer that keeps the
  subject's harmonic functions (e.g. keeps a subdominant function, keeps a suspension (retardo) as a
  suspension rather than an unsatisfying 6/4). In a 5th leap the two notes are often assigned to different
  keys, so an octave C–C in the subject can become the 7th G–F in the answer.
- R19 [PREFER] When in doubt, choose the answer that best HIDES the mutation — ideally at a rhythmic "dead
  point" (punto muerto) created by rests.
- R20 [MUST] Preserve the melodic character of intervals: do not answer "soft" intervals (e.g. major 3rd)
  with "hard" ones (e.g. tritone, 6 semitones) or vice versa. Subjects rich in augmented/diminished intervals:
  choose the answer that keeps e.g. the augmented 2nd.
- R21 [PREFER] If not defective, prefer the answer that allows a stretto with the subject (Koechlin: always
  in contests unless antimusical; Dubois warns against false answers chosen only for stretto).

**Rules — real answer (§90–§91)**
- R22 [MUST] Real answer when the subject begins AND ends in the tonic key and the dominant is absent or never
  in a position requiring a tonic answer. Accidentals do not force a tonal answer unless they establish the
  dominant key. Ignore a dominant that is a non-chord tone (passing tone, neighbour/bordadura).
- R23 [ALLOWED] Even where rules call for a tonal answer, use a real answer (D answered by II, i.e. exact 5th)
  when the tonal one is melodically poor; the less "classical" the subject, the stronger this tendency.
- R24 [ALLOWED] Typical cases for real answer: (a) dominant reached from diatonic IV (not #IV) in the head or as
  final note (WTC I/6, I/17, I/19); others answer as if IV were #IV; (b) dominant preceded by VI in the head,
  esp. minor (VI–V semitone answered III–II); (c) passing modulations to the dominant key in a subject that
  begins and ends in the tonic, esp. in a sequence (WTC I/14); (d) chromatic subjects of the R15 type (e.g.
  C–C#–D–D#–E–F–F#–G: unanimous real answer); (e) when a tonal answer would destroy a characteristic interval
  (diminished 7th, augmented 2nd); (f) when the real answer avoids a hard interval not in the subject (Fux
  nevertheless accepted the tritone); (g) dominant that is merely the 5th of a tonic-chord arpeggio — real if
  the arpeggio starts on the tonic; old masters prefer tonal if it starts on the dominant; (h) any case where
  salient points (sequence character, a partly chromatic outline) would be lost.

**Plagal answers (§92)**
- R25 [ALLOWED] Plagal answer = answer at the 4th above (subdominant instead of dominant), wholly or partly
  (Bach organ fugue in F, Pachelbel; partly: WTC I/18 answered in c# instead of d#, also I/19, I/24;
  Mendelssohn). Most treatises ignore them; some condemn them, esp. in school fugue.

**Agent applicability**
- Algorithm for generating a fugal answer: transpose +7 semitones; then scan the head for tonic/dominant
  degrees and apply R1–R4 (swap scale degree 1↔5, i.e. answer 5→1 rather than 5→2); place mutations per R9–R15;
  verify with a 4-part harmonization (R17–R18); if result has unisons for semitones or broken characteristic
  intervals, fall back to a real answer (R23–R24).
- Hide the mutation on a rest or weak note (R19).

## Fugue: codetta, countersubject, episodes, strettos — §93–§96 (scan p.66–71, printed p.117–127)

**Concepts**
- Codetta of subject/answer (coda del sujeto/respuesta): free link filling the rhythmic gap (vacío rítmico)
  between the end of one entry and the start of the next when they cannot overlap or follow directly.
- Episode (episodio): non-entry passage between entries. Stretto (estrecho): overlapping entries.
  Closed (cerrado) stretto = entries closer together in time.

**Rules — codetta (§93)**
- R26 [PREFER] Use a codetta only to avoid a rhythmic gap between entries; compose it freely but as a natural
  continuation of the subject/answer.

**Rules — countersubject (§94)**
- R27 [MUST] CS in invertible counterpoint (contrapunto invertible) with subject and answer (it appears above
  and below). With several CS, all mutually invertible.
- R28 [PREFER] CS fits the subject's character but is melodically and rhythmically distinct (easy to identify).
- R29 [PREFER] CS complements the subject: moves in short values where the subject holds long ones and vice
  versa; articulates strong beats when the subject does not.
- R30 [PREFER] CS starts AFTER the subject/answer has attacked, and after the head mutation, so the CS need not
  mutate (WTC I/14, I/24).
- R31 [PREFER] Compose the CS for the subject first; if the answer is tonal and the CS must mutate, place its
  mutations as close as possible to those of the answer (WTC I/23).
- R32 [PREFER] Use suspensions in the CS, but not at points affected by a mutation.
- R33 [PREFER] Anticipate mode changes of subject/answer so the CS needs no or minimal modification.
- R34 [MUST] Multiple CS: each with own personality; they must not start at the same fraction of the bar.

**Rules — episodes (§95)**
- R35 [ALLOWED] Episode material: from subject, answer, CS(s) or fragments already heard (some schools —
  French — require this and forbid the subject head, reserving it for strettos). In free fugue a new element
  may be used for variety; Bach uses the head in episodes (WTC I/2, I/3, I/6, I/7).
- R36 [PREFER] Episodes vary among themselves (elements and realization), each more interesting than the
  previous. (Old fugues often repeat an episode exactly or nearly: WTC I/2 bars 4 & 17; I/7 bars 8 & 13.)
- R37 [MUST] No episode should sound like an inserted block: start its main design BEFORE the preceding entry
  ends and run it into the next entry; otherwise hide seams (soldaduras) with suspensions, imitations.
- R38 [PREFER] Imitations and canons of any kind are excellent episode material.
- R39 [AVOID] Sequences repeated always in the SAME voice, or where all voices merely repeat the model higher
  or lower. [PREFER] pass the design between voices (repetitions become imitations); designs exchanged
  between voices must be in invertible counterpoint. Best: each design appears in all voices (WTC II/17
  bar 27: three designs a-b-c rotated through three voices = triple canon; WTC II/7 bar 46: bass keeps
  its design while upper two exchange theirs = canon).
- R40 [MUST] Episode modulations reach the key of the next entry easily, without anticipating ("spending")
  that key and without abruptness.
- R41 [MUST] (almost all schools) At least two episodes: one leading into the first entry of section 2, one
  into the first entry of section 3. Number/length of the others vary.

**Rules — strettos (§96)**
- R42 [PREFER] Reserve strettos for section 3 (always in school fugue).
- R43 [PREFER] The closer the entries, the more interesting; many schools require each stretto on the same
  material to be closer than the previous (WTC I/1 bars 7–8 vs 14–15).
- R44 [PREFER] If harmony forces altering a voice in a stretto, do not alter the LAST-entering voice (the ear
  follows it most).
- R45 [ALLOWED] The CS may accompany a stretto with modifications or cuts.
- R46 [MUST] If the subject admits stretto with the answer (S–R), use it and do not place it secondarily;
  S–S or R–R strettos are of lesser rank.
- R47 [ALLOWED] Use inversion (movimiento contrario), retrograde, augmentation, diminution to enrich or enable
  strettos.
- R48 [AVOID] A stretto section disproportionate to the earlier sections (d'Indy: school strettos sometimes
  equal or exceed the rest of the fugue).
- R49 [ALLOWED] Stretto entries in any voices; free voices may accompany the first and later entries (some
  treatises reject the first voice entering alone).
- R50 [ALLOWED] Strettos may follow one another directly or be linked by CS-strettos or short episodes, which
  should be imitative and use stretto material.
- R51 [PREFER] School fugue: at least 3 S–R strettos when possible; first and last in the main key, the others
  in the main or neighbouring keys.
- R52 [MUST] (French school) First and last strettos S–R with 4 equidistant entries (S R S R), each in a
  different voice, the last closer than the first; earlier entries may be cut off when the next enters; the 4th
  must be complete. Gedalge example: entries every 2 bars.

**Agent applicability**
- When generating a CS: check invertibility (no parallel 5ths that become 4ths, etc.), rhythmic complement
  (CS active when subject sustains), entry after the subject's first beat.
- Episode generator: take a 1–2 bar motif from S/CS, run a sequence where the motif migrates between voices;
  start it under the tail of the previous entry.
- Stretto planner: compute candidate entry offsets; order strettos from widest to closest offset.

## French school fugue (fuga de escuela, tipo francés) — §97 (scan p.72–73, printed p.128–131)

**Concepts**
- Model codified by Paris Conservatoire contests and treatises (Cherubini, Dubois, Gedalge, Dupré); the book
  follows Gedalge/Dupré and notes older variants in footnotes.
- Counter-exposition (contraexposición): optional second group of entries in the main key.

**Plan (numbered steps; checkable template)**

SECTION 1 — Exposition (exposición)
1. Four entries in order S R S R. 2 voices: voice A = entries 1 & 4, voice B = 2 & 3. 3 voices: one voice
   = entries 1 & 4, the other two = 2 and 3. 4 voices: one entry each.
2. Entries 2–4 begin on the last note of the previous entry or immediately after. Codettas only if
   indispensable — except in 2-voice fugues, which put a fairly long codetta (≈ a small episode) between
   entries 2 and 3.
3. Vocal 4-part fugue (contest type): a high subject is sung by Soprano and Tenor, a low one by Alto and Bass;
   answers by the other voices.
4. Recommended voice orders (entries 1-2-3-4 = S-R-S-R): Sop–Alt–Ten–Bass; Bass–Ten–Alt–Sop;
   Alt–Ten–Bass–Sop; Ten–Alt–Sop–Bass.
5. CS(s) accompany S and R (forms of §63, outside this range).
6. If a CS is present from the first subject entry, do not give it to the voice that enters next with R
   (unless there are as many CS as voices). Single CS placement: S in Sop→CS in Ten; S in Ten→CS in Sop;
   S in Alt→CS in Bass; S in Bass→CS in Alt.
Counter-exposition (optional; falling out of use; advisable only for very short subjects whose range suits
both high and low voices):
7. Short episode. 8. R in main key. 9. S in main key — in voices that did not yet have them (needs >2 voices).

SECTION 2 — Development (desarrollo, episodio, divertimento)
10. Episode (length: Dubois relatively long; Gedalge very short, ≈ 2× subject length; Dupré ≈ subject length).
11. S in the relative key: major fugue → minor key on VI; minor fugue → major key on III.
12. Its normal answer (5th above): major fugue → minor on III; minor fugue → major on natural VII.
13. Episode, somewhat longer than the previous but shorter than the last one of the fugue.
14. S in the key of IV (in its normal mode).
15. Very short episode (optional).
16. S or R in the relative of the previous key: major fugue → minor on II; minor fugue → major on VI. (Not
    required by Cherubini or Dubois.)
17. Episode (length: Gedalge a bit more than 3× subject; Dupré 2× subject).
18. Dominant pedal (the episode may continue over it).
19. Half cadence (semicadencia), with or without fermata. (Steps 17–19 marked optional as a block.)
Obsolete section-2 entries (repeat keys already heard): S in the dominant key (only where it differs from R) to
begin or end section 2 (Gedalge still accepts at the end); S in the main key with mode changed (Gedalge
accepts in major fugues if the subject then regains its mode, and in minor to end the work); R in the main key
as reply to S in IV.

SECTION 3 — Strettos (estrechos)
20. 1st stretto S–R: 4 equidistant entries (S R S R) in the main key, each in a different voice; entries 1–3
    may be incomplete, the 4th complete. (Cherubini/Dubois did not require 4 entries or equidistance.)
21. Short episode or stretto of 2+ entries on the CS (optional; may be replaced by free strettos on S or R).
22. 2nd stretto (S–R or other), 2+ entries, equidistant or not, closer than the 1st; main or neighbouring keys;
    may be a chain of strettos.
23. Episode or CS stretto, similar character, different construction (optional).
24. 3rd stretto S–R, same constitution as the 1st but closer. (Also possible: very close stretto, 1 note
    apart, R–S or of heads only.)
25. Any stretto — preferably the first or last S–R — may lie on a dominant pedal if none ended section 2. The
    last may lie on a tonic pedal or a simultaneous dominant+tonic pedal. Otherwise end with a tonic pedal over
    which voices imitate the head of S/R or one voice states S/R (partially or wholly).

**Rules — general observations (required)**
- R53 [MUST] Do not reduce the number of real voices; a voice that rests does so briefly and with a logical
  melodic close.
- R54 [MUST] A voice re-entering after rest brings S, R, CS or another important design, not harmonic filler.
- R55 [MUST] Every entry begins on a chord tone (nota real).
- R56 [PREFER] Precede every S/R entry with a relatively long rest in that voice; its first note must not be
  in unison with another voice.
- R57 [NEVER] The same voice makes two consecutive S/R entries.
- R58 [PREFER] In a new group of entries, do not assign entries to the same voices as in the previous group.
- R59 [NEVER] A conclusive cadence in section 1. Later ones only if voice motion is not entirely halted.
- R60 [PREFER] Pedals are normally in the bass; under a pedal the voice count increases so that the same number
  of active voices remains above it. Some forbid dominant and tonic pedals in direct succession.

**Agent applicability**
- This is a ready-made section/key map. For a C major 4-voice fugue: Expo C/G/C/G → (episode) → a minor S →
  e minor R → episode → F major S → (episode) → d minor S/R → episode → G pedal → half cadence → 3 strettos in
  C (closing in) → tonic pedal ending. For C minor: Expo c/g → Eb S → Bb R → f S → Ab S/R → …
- Episode length heuristic: 1–3× subject length, growing toward the end of section 2.

## Double, triple fugue; fughetta, fugato; fugues by inversion etc. — §98–§103 (scan p.73–75, printed p.131–135)

**Concepts**
- Double fugue (doble fuga): for the majority (and the author) = a fugue that exposes and develops a 2nd subject
  independently of the 1st, even if later combined. (Some treatises call the subject+CS fugue "double"; that
  is only a naming difference.) The 2nd subject is always less important.
- Fughetta (fugueta): small fugue; exposition has only the indispensable entries; development unimportant.
- Fugato (fugado): rarely a whole piece; a fragment of a larger work (sonata or symphony movement) where a theme
  is exposed with fugal entries and then fugal procedure is abandoned.
- Fugal style (estilo fugado): imitations, answers at various intervals; fugue flavour without fugue structure.

**Rules**
- R61 [PREFER] Double-fugue structure is free; usually section 1 = a simple fugue on subject 1 only.
- R62 [PREFER] Expose subject 2 with the same number of entries or (usually) fewer, to avoid excessive length;
  it may have its own CS; key = main or neighbouring; its answer at the 5th above or elsewhere (more freedom).
- R63 [MUST] Both subjects end up combined, in stretto if possible. *Example:* WTC II/18 (g# minor, 6/8):
  expo S1/R/CS; bar 61 S2 enters; 66 its answer; 71 entry a tone lower; 79; bar 97 first combined entry.
- R64 [ALLOWED] School double fugue (less rigid): Dubois — S2 appears after the S1 entry in the relative key, or
  takes S1's place before the strettos; Richter — S2 after a longer development of S1 ending with a cadence
  on the dominant; both in the main key; Gedalge allows a neighbouring key.
- R65 [PREFER] Gedalge's best double-fugue plan: (a) expo S1; (b) episode; (c) counter-expo (optional);
  (d) episode (if c); (e) S1 in relative of main key; (f) episode and rest on the dominant; (g) expo of S2 in
  main key; (h) episode on S2; (i) S1+S2 combined in key of II (major) or VI (minor); (j) their answers
  combined; (k) episode combining both; (l) S1+S2 combined in the subdominant key; (m) episode + dominant
  pedal with subject elements; (n) strettos and ending. Variants: drop (j) and reach (l) via a short episode;
  or from (d) go to IV, then (short episode) its relative, then (short episode) the dominant of the main key
  where S1+S2 combine.
- R66 [AVOID] Triple/quadruple fugues (3/4 subjects; rare): risk of excessive length; it is acceptable to skip
  the independent exposition of secondary subjects and combine them at once. *Example:* WTC II/14 (f# minor):
  S1 expo; bar 20 S2 expo in main key + entries in other keys; bar 36 S3 expo and development; bar 51 S1;
  from end of bar 54 all three combined, repeated at 60 and 66 to close.
- R67 [ALLOWED] (Old, disused) fugues by augmentation, diminution, inversion, retrograde: 4-entry exposition
  either S / R (in the transformed form) / S normal / R as previous, or S / R normal / S transformed / R
  matching. Fux example (g minor 4/2): S normal, S inverted, R normal, R inverted.

**Agent applicability**
- For a "fugato" inside a sonata/symphony development: 3–4 fugal entries (S R S R) then dissolve into
  homophonic/sequential writing.

## Variation form (La Variación) — §104–§117 (scan p.76–83, printed p.136–150)

**Concepts**
- Variation form: an indeterminate number of short pieces all based on one theme (tema), almost always stated
  first, modified each time intrinsically or extrinsically. "Una variación" = one piece; "Variaciones" =
  the whole work; "variar un tema" = restate with ornamental changes.
- Three types (§105):
  - Ornamental / melodic variation (variación ornamental / melódica; "rítmica" if only note values change): the
    theme itself is altered but keeps enough melodic and harmonic features to be recognized.
  - Decorative / harmonic-contrapuntal variation (variación decorativa / armónico-contrapuntística): the
    changes are in the harmony/counterpoint around the theme, not in it.
  - Amplifying / free / grand variation (variación amplificativa / libre / gran variación): only a fragment or
    detail of the theme survives, from which a new circumstantial idea is built.
- Basic points of a melody (puntos básicos): its highest/lowest chord tones, main metric accents and cadences.

**Rules**
- R68 [MUST] Ornamental variation: keep the melody's basic points, the accompanying harmony, and the period /
  subdivision structure; ALLOWED: add arabesques, figuration, scales, arpeggios, remove accessory notes,
  new rhythms, change meter, mode, tempo. *Example:* Beethoven op.26 (Ab, 3/8): Var. I and V align bar-for-bar
  with the theme.
- R69 [ALLOWED] Decorative variation: keep the melody unchanged in every respect but change harmony/counterpoint
  so each restatement is interesting. *Example:* Beethoven op.14/2 (G, Andante): Var. I puts the theme in an
  inner/lower voice under a new syncopated line.
- R70 [ALLOWED] Amplifying variation: take "something" (pitch contour, rhythm or harmony) of the theme as a
  generating cell and build a new idea that leads one variation; the next variation yields to another idea.
  The theme may be very hard to recognize. *Example:* Beethoven op.109 (E, 3/4 Andante) Var. V built on the
  theme's first notes.
- R71 [ALLOWED] A variation may combine two or all three types (op.109 Var. III, Allegro vivace 2/4: theme in
  left hand, former accompaniment ornamented in right hand, elements swapping hands).

**Rules — overall structure (§110)**
- R72 [MUST] Begin with the theme; it is almost always the only protagonist (a secondary theme is exceptional,
  modern only).
- R73 [PREFER] Theme generally SLOW and sober in line so figuration does not overload it; it is often a complete
  phrase (frase completa) or a short piece (e.g. minuet or gavotte with variations; doubles).
- R74 [ALLOWED] Number of variations is free; each is usually numbered and a clearly closed unit (any link
  between them is an audible seam).
- R75 [MUST] Non-amplifying variations run PARALLEL to the theme: same number of bars, same period lengths,
  essentially the same harmony in each bar. Amplifying variations: total freedom.
- R76 [PREFER] Same key (tonic) for all variations; change of MODE is allowed (historically first a single
  variation marked Maggiore/Minore). The same-key norm is relative: exceptions grow (Beethoven Six Variations
  op.34: F–D–Bb–G–Eb–c–F; Quartet op.127 Adagio, 3rd variation in E instead of Ab).
- R77 [ALLOWED] End a set with a non-variation piece (fugue, fugato) whose material derives from the theme.
  A coda is always optional.
- R78 [ALLOWED] A variation set may be a whole work or one part (a suite piece, a sonata movement).

**Historical types (§111–§117)**
- R79 [ALLOWED] Canzona (Bach and predecessors; rhythmic variation): a song theme in BINARY (duple) meter, then a
  varied restatement in the same key in TERNARY meter (Bach organ Canzona, d minor: C → 3/2).
- R80 [MUST] Double (doble): an ornamented repeat of a dance piece WITHOUT changing meter, tempo or the essentials
  of the harmonic frame (Bach English Suite VI, Sarabande + Double, d minor 3/2). Same family: Spanish
  diferencias (16th-c vihuelists; Antonio de Cabezón, 1510–1566, credited as the first to write variations),
  English divisions, Italian follias (Follias d'Espagna).
- R81 [ALLOWED] Ostinato (basso ostinato): a theme, usually in the bass, repeated constantly; the piece becomes a
  series of decorative variations over it.
- R82 [PREFER] Chaconne/passacaglia ostinatos: short, severe, very tonal, sometimes only a cadential formula;
  usually (not always) 3/4 or 3/2, slow tempo (pausado).
  - d'Indy: passacaglia = theme (usually bass) identical in all variations (purely contrapuntal); chaconne =
    theme usually in top voice, increasingly ornamented (melodic variations). Riemann: chaconne built like the
    passacaglia, on a basso ostinato; old definitions distinguishing them are contradictory.
  - Bach Chaconne (violin, d minor 3/4): ostinato = descending bass D–C–Bb–A (not always in the bass; often only
    one voice); d minor → D major at var. 32 → minor again at var. 52.
  - Bach Passacaglia (organ, c minor 3/4): theme stated alone, almost always in the bass and unchanged.
  - Vitali Chaconne (g minor): ostinato G–F–Eb–D, one bar per note; key changes several times and the ostinato
    is transposed with each change.
  - Handel Chaconne (G): bass ostinato sometimes plain, sometimes ornamented; keeps key, mode, harmony and often
    the top melody; another chaconne on the same ostinato changes mode at var. 9 and returns at var. 16.
  - Exceptions: Handel Passacaglia (Suite 7, g minor) in 4/4, Allegro moderato; Britten Peter Grimes
    passacaglia in quadruple meter with an 11-beat theme, so its metric accent shifts on each repeat (always in
    the bass). Purcell Chaconne (Suite 2, g minor 3/4): ostinato in the bass except 2 variations in the top
    voice and 2 where it passes in fragments between voices.
- R83 [ALLOWED] Classical era (§116): only ornamental variation flourished (from the doubles), enriched by changes
  of meter, tempo, even mode; harmonic frame, cadences, bar count and period structure were respected. Early
  19th c vogue of "tema con variaciones"; often degenerated into scales/arpeggios/virtuoso filler not fused
  with the theme (AVOID).
- R84 [ALLOWED] Post-classical (§117): amplification and transformation of the theme (late Beethoven; seed in
  Bach's chorale variations). Schumann Symphonic Études (c#): theme = 16-bar phrase, cadence every 4 bars, none
  on the tonic chord, structure A A B A; Var. I builds a new idea on the theme's head (theme's first 4 notes
  appear episodically), 16 bars, last 8 repeated; Var. II theme in bass decorated to bar 4, then new idea, bars
  8 and 10 coincide with theme; Var. IV canon (§52) on a new idea from the first 4 notes; Var. VIII 18 bars,
  last 9 repeated; Var. III and XII only share the opening tonic arpeggio; keys: VII in E, XI in g#, XII in Db
  (enharmonic major tonic) and in rondo form.

**Agent applicability**
- To generate "theme + N variations": pick a slow, plain 8- or 16-bar theme; for ornamental variations keep bar
  count, cadences, chord per bar and the melody's contour peaks/downbeat notes; vary surface rhythm
  (8ths → 16ths → triplets), register, texture; put one minor-mode variation late in the set; end with a
  fugato or coda.
- Passacaglia/chaconne generator: 4–8 bar ostinato (e.g. descending tetrachord i–VII–VI–V), 3/4 slow; loop it
  unchanged in the bass while upper voices increase in rhythmic density across variations; optionally
  transpose ostinato to related keys or move it to the top voice once or twice.

## Old suite (Suite antigua): constitution and binary "Suite type" — §118–§122 (scan p.83–85, printed p.151–154)

**Concepts**
- Suite: set of independent instrumental pieces forming a whole, played in succession. Also Ordre (French),
  Partita (German), Sonata (Italian; sonata da chiesa / da camera).
- Basic pieces: Allemande (Alemanda), Courante (Corrente), Sarabande (Zarabanda), Gigue (Giga). Intermezzos
  (intermedios): other non-essential pieces, generally between Sarabande and Gigue; opening pieces
  (Preamble/Preámbulo, Overture/Obertura, Prelude) come first.

**Rules**
- R85 [PREFER] Order: (opening piece) – Allemande – Courante – Sarabande – (intermezzos) – Gigue. A Rondo or a
  variation-type piece sometimes replaces the Gigue. Number, kind and length of pieces not fixed.
- R86 [MUST] All pieces of an old suite in the SAME key (tonic); mode may change at will (Bach suites/partitas
  keep the same mode except some second dances).
- R87 [MUST] Paired dances (Minuet I/II, Gavotte I/II): II usually in another key or mode; they form one piece:
  play I, II without break, then I again WITHOUT its repeats (like the Trio of a sonata minuet).
- R88 [ALLOWED] A Double after a piece = ornamental variation keeping meter, tempo and harmonic frame.
- R89 [MUST] Basic pieces (and most intermezzos) use the binary "Suite type"; Prelude, Rondo, Chaconne/
  Passacaglia keep their own forms inside a suite. Later, surviving pieces moved to ternary (sonata minuet) or
  rondo form ("Gavotte en Rondeau").

**Binary Suite type plan (§122)**
- R90 [MUST] Part I: expose the main thematic element in the home key; develop it (sequences, imitations, maybe a
  secondary element) through modulation to an ample cadence ending Part I; Part I is REPEATED whole.
- R91 [MUST] Part I ends in: (a) the dominant key (major if piece is major; major or minor if piece is minor),
  or (b) the relative major if the piece is minor; (c) the home key — very rare.
- R92 [MUST] Part II: a regressive modulating process, similar to Part I's progressive one, from Part I's
  closing key (or a neighbour) back to the home key, ending there; Part II is REPEATED.
- R93 [PREFER] Part II opens with Part I's opening material, either as is or in (free) inversion (very common in
  Bach). The final cadence of Part II often equals/resembles the end of Part I, transposed to the home key.
  *Example:* Bach French Suite 1 Courante (d minor, 3/2): Part I ends in A (dominant); Part II starts in the
  dominant, ends on a D major chord.
- R94 [ALLOWED] Minor-key pieces ending on a major tonic chord (Picardy cadence, cadencia picarda) — very common
  in Bach's time.
- Historical note: D. Scarlatti apparently first ended pieces with a design derived from the main theme — the
  germ of the recapitulation that turned the binary suite type into the ternary sonata type.

**Agent applicability**
- Binary dance template: ||: A (I → V, or i → III/v), ends with strong cadence in new key :||: A' (opening
  motif, often inverted, modulating through neighbours) → I, closing cadence = Part I's cadence transposed :||.
  Typical lengths 8+8, 12+16, 16+24 bars (inferred: Part II often longer).

## Classical sonata: origin and movement plan — §141–§143 (scan p.91–92, printed p.167–169)

**Concepts**
- Sonata: originally a piece "sounded" on a bowed instrument; later an introduction; then a suite synonym; finally
  the new form for keyboard solo or keyboard + one instrument.
- Galant style (estilo galante): accompanied melody — chords dissolved into accompaniment formulas, free number
  of harmonic parts.

**Rules**
- R99 [MUST] Suite → sonata transformation: max 4 movements, detached from dances; galant style; binary suite
  type replaced by ternary (sonata) type; same-key obligation kept only for the first and last movements.
- R100 [PREFER] Inner movement keys: classics were cautious — in Beethoven's 32 piano sonatas, 15 keep one key
  throughout; of the rest, only one movement is in another key, which in 12 cases is the slow movement
  (Marpurg's 1762 advice).
- R101 [PREFER] Movement count: usually 3 or 4, sometimes 2 (Haydn some 2, mostly 3; Mozart all 3; Beethoven's
  32: ten 4-movement, fifteen 3-movement, seven 2-movement). Scarlatti/Soler "sonatas" = single movements.
- R102 [PREFER] 4-movement order: (a) first fast movement (tiempo vivo inicial; role of the allemande);
  (b) slow movement (tiempo lento; repose and melodic expansion, like the sarabande); (c) Minuet or Scherzo
  (lighter, less important, an intermezzo); (d) final fast movement (tiempo vivo final), different character,
  almost always more brilliant than the first (role of gigue/rondo). The two middle movements may swap.
- R103 [PREFER] 3 movements: most commonly drop (b) or (c) (not absolute). Variation and fugue may also appear as
  sonata movements. Tempo degree depends on the composer.

**Agent applicability**
- Default multi-movement plan: Allegro (sonata form, tonic) – slow (Lied ternary or variations; IV, VI or
  relative key) – Minuet/Scherzo + Trio (tonic) – fast finale (rondo or sonata, tonic).

## First movement: sonata type (tipo Sonata) — §144–§153 (scan p.92–99, printed p.169–183)

**Concepts**
- Ternary sonata type: originally monothematic (one main theme exposed, developed, re-exposed), soon
  bithematic. Themes A and B = I and II = first and second idea. Capital letter = theme or theme group;
  lowercase a', a'', b', b'', b''' = members of a group (Bertelin: A = "rhythmic group", B = "melodic group").
- Bridge (puente, conducción, transición): passage from A to B. Giulio Bas: inseparable bridge (fused to A)
  vs separable.
- Coda (of the exposition; Bertelin: grupo de cadencia).
- Codal development (desarrollo codal): a second development inside the final coda.
- Associated key (tono asociado, Gevaert): the key of theme B.

**Exposition (sección I) rules**
- R104 [PREFER] Contrast A vs B (generally, not absolute): A = vigorous, concise, clear rhythmic and tonal profile
  ("masculine"); B = elegant, lyrical, rhythmically delicate ("feminine"). Early sonatas had little contrast
  (Clementi: B = A transposed).
- R105 [ALLOWED] Secondary complementary ideas: rare after A, very frequent after B (B becomes a group b', b'',
  b'''). Beethoven op.2/2: A = a' (bar 1) + a'' (bar 8). op.2/3 (C): b' bar 27 (g minor), b'' bar 47 (G),
  b''' bar 77 (G). op.2/1: b', b'', b''' at bars 20, 33, 41. op.13: bars 51 (counting intro), 89, 113.
  (Analysts disagree on group boundaries.)
- R106 [PREFER] Use a bridge (direct A→B almost never). Material: (a) own elements of little melodic weight;
  (b) elements of A that seem to continue it; (c) elements of B that sketch and prepare it; (d) any mix.
  Length and number of elements free. op.2/3: bridge = 14 bars, two own elements (bar 13 modulates to G,
  bar 21 stabilizes G).
- R107 [MUST] The bridge is the MODULATING part (A and B stabilize their keys); it starts and ends where its role
  requires — very commonly leading to B's key or to the dominant of B's key. op.2/1 (f minor): bridge bar 8 in f
  → Eb (bar 20) = V of Ab, B's key. op.2/3: from tonic chord of C (bar 13) to tonic chord of G (bar 26).
- R108 [ALLOWED] Introduction: optional, absent in most sonatas; usually very slow; brief or substantial (a small
  slow movement); its material may return in the Allegro. op.13: Grave, 10 bars, theme decisive in the Allegro;
  op.78: Adagio, 4 bars, not reused; op.81a: Adagio, 16 bars, central to the development.
- R109 [MUST] Theme A always begins in the home key; may or may not end there; length free; ending may be
  conclusive or fused into what follows. op.2/1: half cadence in home key (bar 8) + fermata rest before bridge;
  op.2/2: conclusive tonic cadence (bar 32) with bridge starting on that same beat; op.10/1: conclusive
  cadence + one bar of silence.
- R110 [NEVER] Expose theme B in the home key.
- R111 [MUST] (pre-Beethoven classics, very rare exceptions) B in the dominant key if the movement is major; in
  the relative major if minor.
- R112 [ALLOWED] (Beethoven) other keys whose tonic chord shares a note with the home tonic chord, except (for
  special reasons) the subdominant: op.31/2 (d) B in a minor (minor v); op.31/1 (G) B in B major (major III);
  op.106 (Bb) B in G major (VI); op.111 (c) B in Ab (VI).
- R113 [PREFER] All elements of group B in the same key, though not always the same mode (b' in the minor was an
  Italian custom): op.2/3 g–G–G; op.13 (c) eb–Eb–Eb; op.57 (f) Ab–ab–ab.
- R114 [ALLOWED] Exposition coda (optional): from earlier themes and/or own material (op.2/3 coda bar 85 derives
  its rhythm from the bridge's first element); sometimes only a few notes/chords. Ending: mostly conclusive on
  the tonic chord of B's key (op.2/1, 2, 3); or suspensive/modulating toward the home key or the development's
  key (op.10/3 ends on V7 of the home key; op.13 2nd ending on V7 of the development's opening key).
- R115 [ALLOWED] Exposition repeat: long the norm (from the suite); dropped when expositions grew long (Beethoven
  sonatas: repeated in all up to op.57; afterwards not, except op.106).
- R116 [ALLOWED] Formerly development + recapitulation were also repeated together as one unit:
  ||: Exposition :||: Development – Recapitulation :|| (binary like the suite); this fell out of use before the
  exposition repeat.

**Development (sección II, desarrollo) rules**
- R117 [ALLOWED] Structure and tonal plan absolutely free, but the tonal process must be careful, never
  capricious, never a redundant copy of the exposition's.
- R118 [MUST] Treat exposition elements by fragmentation, rhythmic/melodic/modal change, expansion, reduction,
  imitation, superposition.
- R119 [AVOID] Repeating tonal states and formulas already used in the exposition: a theme repeated in the same
  key needs a new harmonization; with the same harmonization it must be in a different key.
- R120 [ALLOWED] New complementary elements are needed; one may become almost as important as the main material
  (Beethoven op.10/2, mvt II, new theme at bar 11 of the development).
- R121 [ALLOWED] Scope ranges from brief (few elements) to extensive (all elements, or exhaustive work on one);
  character from playful to a dramatic "struggle" of themes.
- R122 [ALLOWED] Development starting key completely free (op.2/1 begins with theme A in Ab, the key that closed
  the exposition).
- R123 [PREFER] End the development on a dominant-family chord (V, V7, vii°7 of the home key — inferred
  "familia de la dominante"), carefully prepared: the effect of the recapitulation entry depends on it.
- R124 [PREFER] Classic retransition device: a chord first treated as a tonic (e.g. G in a C-major movement) is
  insisted upon and then reinterpreted as dominant by a simple change of tonal function (op.2/3).
  *Example plan, op.2/3 development:* b''' material modulating (bar 90) → arpeggios on the bridge rhythm over held
  chords (bar 97) → theme A in D, modulating from its 4th bar with a syncopated counter-design (bar 139) → no
  further new material → G chord → recap.
- R125 [MUST] Theme A in its original key AND form = the unmistakable sign that the recapitulation has begun.
  A full statement of A in another key inside the development is a false start (op.10/2: A complete in D;
  real recap only at the change to F).

**Recapitulation (sección III, reexposición) rules**
- R126 [MUST] More or less faithful repeat of the exposition's basic elements, with theme B now in the HOME key.
  (a) A in the home key; (b) bridge same, similar or entirely different, or present in only one of the two
  sections — and it must be adjusted to land B in the home key; (c) B in the home key; (d) coda (if any) may be
  re-exposed, also in the home key, replaced, or followed by a new, usually longer coda — sometimes a codal
  development, important but generally smaller than section II.
- R127 [ALLOWED] Substitute, drop, shorten or expand secondary elements of groups A and B (rarely the main
  element); enrich or simplify texture — not a strict copy.
- R128 [ALLOWED] If B's mode differed from A's in the exposition, the recap may keep or change it (op.2/1: B
  group major in the exposition, home-key MINOR in the recap).
- R129 [ALLOWED] Larger modifications: op.13 recap starts b' in the SUBDOMINANT (f) then modulates to c for b''
  and b'''; op.10/2 recap starts A from a'' (the false recap in D made the opening bars redundant); Mozart
  (a D-major piano sonata) recaps B before A. op.2/3 recap: A bars 9–12 replaced by a new secondary idea;
  bridge's first element dropped; new coda inserted after b''' starting with a deceptive cadence (cadencia rota)
  to VI, a short cadenza on A material, A's first 4 bars, development material, an ample cadential formula,
  then the exposition coda in the home key.
- R130 [PREFER] Final coda (optional) is often more important than in the exposition; it affirms and ends in the
  home key.

**Sonata-type schema (§153)**
- Exposition: [Introduction, optional] → Theme A (home key; rarely >1 element) → Bridge (optional but very
  common; modulating) → Theme B (neighbouring key; almost always b' principal, b'' complementary, b''' conclusive;
  all in one key though not necessarily one mode; key = dominant, or relative major if A minor, or a key whose
  tonic chord shares a note with A's — not the subdominant) → Coda (optional, free).
- Development: free thematic work on all/part of the exposition, tonally steered so as to re-enter the home key
  with →
- Recapitulation: A (home key, same or lightly varied) → Bridge (optional, same/different, must lead to) →
  B in the home key (with or without all secondary elements) → Coda (optional, often bigger; confirms home key).

**Agent applicability**
- Planning check-list for a sonata-form movement: A in I; bridge modulates (ends on V/new key or I of new key);
  B group (b' b'' b''') in V (major) or III (minor); closing on I of new key or V7 for repeat; development starts
  anywhere, avoids exposition's key/harmony pairings, ends on V(7) of home key (pedal or reinterpreted tonic);
  recap A in I, bridge rewritten to stay in I, B in I (minor-key movements may put B in tonic minor); coda in I.
- Validation: assert key(B_expo) != home; assert key(B_recap) == home; assert last development chord ∈
  dominant family of home key.
- Proportion hints from cited examples (inferred): bridge ≈ 14 bars; B elements spaced 10–30 bars apart;
  intro 4–16 bars.

## Slow movement: forms; ternary Lied — §154–§155 (scan p.99–100, printed p.183–185; continues)

**Concepts**
- Slow movement (tiempo lento): the most varied and free movement. Main types: (a) binary Suite type (§122; early
  classical, then disappeared); (b) ternary Lied (tipo «Lied» ternario, see §46 — outside this range);
  (c) developed Lied (Lied desarrollado); (d) sonata type, with or without development.
- Episodic phrase (frase episódica): modulating transitional phrase with a new secondary motive or a brief
  development of the main one.

**Rules — ternary Lied (§155)**
- R131 [MUST] Section I = exposition of the main theme/phrase. Its structure may be primary (one unit), binary
  (second subsection complementary) or ternary; if ternary it is itself almost always a small Lied:
  ||: main phrase (starts in the movement's key, usually ends in a neighbouring key — same choices as Part I of
  the suite type — but may end in the home key) :||: episodic phrase (modulating transition; new secondary
  motive or brief development of the main one), oriented to lead back to → main phrase restated (wholly or
  partly) in the home key AND ending there (so modified if it originally ended elsewhere) :||.
- R132 [MUST] Section II = episodic, secondary relative to Section I; uses new or derived material; its role is
  transition/link; may have any of Section I's structures (one section's structure does not dictate the
  other's); tonally it usually starts in a neighbouring key and ends so as to lead smoothly into Section III.
- R133 [MUST] Section III = total or partial restatement of Section I in the home key, often melodically or
  rhythmically enriched, sometimes extended with a small development.
- *Example:* Beethoven op.28 Andante (d minor, 2/4), Section I as ternary Lied: 8-bar main phrase (repeated)
  d → a; secondary phrase bars 10–17, derived from it, beginning and ending on the dominant of d (suspensive);
  main motive restated as a 6-bar phrase (last 4 bars reoriented, bar-2 rhythm foregrounded), feminine ending
  on the home tonic. [Range ends here; Sections II–III of the example continue on later scans.]

**Agent applicability**
- Slow-movement template (ternary Lied): A = small a–b–a' (8 + 8 + 6–8 bars; a: i → v or I → V; b on V pedal;
  a' closes in I) → B = contrasting episode in a neighbouring key (IV, VI, relative), ends on V of home →
  A' = restatement in I with added ornamentation/figuration, optional short development and coda.
