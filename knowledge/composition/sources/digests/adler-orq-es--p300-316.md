## 9 Introduction: power, history, balance (scan p.300–301, printed p.295–296)

**Concepts**
- Brass section (sección de metal): the orchestra's family with the greatest dynamic power.
- Early brass (pre-1650 and natural instruments): different sound, playing technique and intonation rules;
  the music written for them was shaped by those limitations, which only from mid-19th c. began to resemble today's.

**Rules**
- R1 [PREFER] Treat full brass at full power as able to cover the whole orchestra; always plan how to balance
  brass against woodwinds and strings (detailed in Adler ch. 11). *Why:* brass at full strength easily eclipses the rest.
- R2 [AVOID] Expecting brass to play as softly as woodwinds or strings in general.
- R3 [ALLOWED] An open (unmuted) brass-section pianissimo is a unique orchestral color; use it deliberately (exception to R2).
- R4 [PREFER] Modern brass are agile and versatile; the only real limitation left for the composer is register.
- R5 [PREFER] [era: pre-1650 and natural brass] When writing in an early style, respect the old instruments' limits
  (harmonic-series notes only, different intonation); these peculiarities are irrelevant for modern writing.
- R6 [ALLOWED] [era: early-19th-c.] Ophicleide (oficleido) parts appear in early-19th-c. scores; today they are played on tuba.
- R7 [ALLOWED] [era: post-1955] Trumpet in D: Piston (1955) called it rare in the orchestra; since then many players
  choose it and it appears frequently.

**Examples**
- R. Strauss, *Don Juan*, bars 37–40: 4 horns in E, 3 trumpets in E, 3 trombones + tuba in octave/unison rhythmic
  triplet figures, f growing to ff — demonstrates the section's dynamic power.

## Section layout and score order (scan p.301–303, printed p.296–298)

**Concepts**
- Standard modern symphonic brass: 4 horns (trompas), 3 trumpets (trompetas), 3 trombones (trombones), 1 tuba.
  Enlarged by some composers with cornets (cornetas), euphoniums (bombardinos) and Wagner tubas (tubas wagnerianas).
- Two sub-groups: (1) horns; (2) trumpets, trombones, tubas. Horns also act as auxiliaries of the woodwinds
  because they blend with and reinforce them.
- Mouthpiece: horn = funnel-shaped (embudo) → much sweeter tone; trumpet/trombone/tuba = shallow cup (copa) →
  trumpet much brighter than horn. Forsyth's rule: the shallower the cup, the brighter the tone.
- Bore: trumpets and trombones mainly cylindrical tubing; horns and tubas mainly conical.
- Transposing brass: horns, trumpets, cornets. Non-transposing: trombones, all tubas, euphoniums.

**Rules**
- R8 [MUST] Score order: brass directly below woodwinds, top to bottom: 4 horns, 3 trumpets, 3 trombones, tuba.
  Horns sit above trumpets for historical reasons although they are the section's "altos".
- R9 [ALLOWED] In band (wind band) scores trumpets are often placed above horns (Adler ch. 19).
- R10 [PREFER] Horn and trumpet parts written without key signature, accidentals added as needed (most orchestral
  players prefer it); band parts often carry key signatures.
- R11 [PREFER] Trombone and tuba parts written with key signature (players are used to it, esp. 19th-c. repertoire).
- R12 [ALLOWED] [era: older scores, 18th–19th c.] Brass parts always written in C; the instrument (crook) supplied the key.
- R13 [PREFER] Staves: 4 horns on 2 staves (hn 1–2, hn 3–4); 3 trumpets on 2 staves (tpt 1–2, tpt 3); if the writing is
  too complex for two staves, give each player his own staff (same for horns).
- R14 [PREFER] Trombones: two tenor trombones share one staff; bass trombone on a second staff.
- R15 [AVOID] Putting bass trombone on the tuba's staff. *Why:* parts become harder to read.
- R16 [PREFER] One tuba, on its own staff; extra tubas usually on the same staff. Euphoniums and Wagner tubas go
  between the bass-trombone and tuba staves.

**Agent applicability**
- REAPER track order for brass: HN 1–4 (or HN 1-2 / HN 3-4), TPT 1–3, TBN 1-2, BTBN, (EUPH / WAGNER TUBAS), TUBA,
  inside a BRASS folder bus (empty parent).
- Timbre choice: horn = round/sweet, trumpet = brilliant; use horns to bind with woodwinds.

## Harmonic series and natural brass (scan p.303–305, printed p.298–300)

**Concepts**
- Harmonics (armónicos), harmonic series (serie armónica), partial (parcial): a natural (valveless) instrument is a tube
  governed by one fundamental; longer tube = lower fundamental. The player gets the fundamental with relaxed blowing
  and the upper partials by tightening the embouchure (embocadura).
- An 8-foot tube has fundamental C2 (MIDI 36). Ex. 9-2, C series (concert, this tube): 1 C2 (36), 2 C3 (48),
  3 G3 (55), 4 C4 (60), 5 E4 (64), 6 G4 (67), 7 Bb4 (≈70, flat, printed as a filled note), 8 C5 (72), 9 D5 (74),
  10 E5 (76), 11 F#5 (in parentheses: between F and F#, ≈77–78), 12 G5 (79), 13 A5 (≈81, out of tune), 14 Bb5 (82),
  15 B5 (83), 16 C6 (84); the example continues to about the 21st partial (C#6, D6, Eb6, E6, F6 [?]).
- Pedal note (nota pedal): the fundamental.

**Rules**
- R17 [MUST] [era: natural brass, before mid-19th c.] Natural trumpets/horns can play only the partials of their tube's
  series. With fundamental C2, the notes between partials (and their chromatic alterations) are unplayable in normal
  circumstances (Ex. 9-3 shows a scale of such notes from the low register up to the treble [? exact notes partly
  unreadable]: in practice, below C4 only C2, C3, G3 exist).
- R18 [AVOID] [era: natural brass] Fundamental/pedal for beginners: usually hard to produce.
- R19 [AVOID] Partials above the 16th: theoretically up to the 21st exist, but most players cannot go above the 16th.
- R20 [MUST] [era: natural brass] Partials 7, 11, 13 and 14 are out of tune against equal temperament; the two major
  seconds 8–9 and 9–10 differ in size and must be adjusted by the player.
- R21 [ALLOWED] Modern composers use the untuned natural partials as a special effect (Britten, *Serenade*: horn plays
  the F series without valves, out-of-tune notes marked "x").
- R22 [ALLOWED] [era: Baroque, Bach/Handel/Vivaldi/Telemann] Writing high clarino passages (partials 8–16) for virtuoso
  trumpets; players "found" missing/out-of-tune notes by lipping (embouchure): e.g. written F5 (11th partial) lipped down,
  written A5 taken from a lowered upper partial (the book says the 14th) [as stated].
- R23 [ALLOWED] [era: natural horn] Horn players could also raise pitch by putting the right hand inside the bell
  (shortens the tube) besides lipping.
- R24 [AVOID] [era: Classical, natural brass] Notes above the 12th partial: Classical composers rarely asked for them;
  the 11th needs adjustment.
- R25 [ALLOWED] [era: Beethoven] By Beethoven's time range extended upward to the 16th partial (written C6 on Eb horn);
  near-top notes such as written B5 and D5 needed slight, easy adjustments in that register.

**Examples**
- Britten, *Serenade*, Prologue, bars 1–14: solo horn in F on natural harmonics, p–ff–ppp, untuned partials as color.
- J. S. Bach, *Brandenburg Concerto No. 2*, mvt 1, bars 28–30: trumpet in F (sounds a P4 higher) running on partials 8–16;
  F5 and A5 written are the lipped notes.
- Mozart, *Symphony No. 40*, mvt 3, bars 74–78: 2 horns in G, partials 4–12, 11th adjusted.
- Beethoven, *Piano Concerto No. 5*, mvt 1, bars 43–47: 2 horns in Eb, partials 5–11, thirds/sixths in the series.
- Beethoven, *Symphony No. 3*, mvt 3 (trio), bars 174–181: 3 horns in Eb up to the 16th partial (written C6, concert Eb5 = 75, converted).

**Agent applicability**
- Period-style natural horn/trumpet (inferred): offsets of partials above the fundamental in semitones:
  0, 12, 19, 24, 28, 31, 34(flat), 36, 38, 40, 42(between 41/42), 43, 45(flat), 46, 47, 48. Restrict a "natural brass"
  part to fundamental + these offsets (concert fundamental of a crook = written C2 transposed by the crook interval;
  e.g. horn in F: written C2 → F1 = MIDI 29, so its 8th partial = F4 = 65).
- Prefer partials 3–12 for Classical horn writing, up to 16 only for Beethoven-era high horns; avoid 7/11/13/14 on
  long exposed notes unless the out-of-tune color is wanted.

## Crooks and valves (scan p.306–308, printed p.301–303)

**Concepts**
- Crook (bomba / cilindro de entonación): U-shaped extra tubing inserted to lengthen the tube and change the series;
  invented in Haydn's time. The player could change crook mid-piece given a little time; still limited to the new series.
- Valves (válvulas: rotary = giratorias, piston = de pistón): invented early 19th c., refined enough to be accepted
  by players only around mid-century. Each valve opens extra tubing, doing instantly what a crook change did.
- Valve 1 lowers a whole tone (2 semitones), valve 2 a semitone (1), valve 3 a tone and a half (3); tubas often have a
  4th valve lowering a P4 (5). Combinations add (2+3 = M3, 4 semitones). Valves made brass fully chromatic.
- Trombone slide (vara): two U-tubes, one sliding inside the other; essentially unchanged since the 15th c.;
  continuous length change → perfect intonation and full chromaticism; partials still chosen by embouchure.
- Tenor trombone: 7 positions, each with its own fundamental: I Bb, II A, III Ab, IV G, V Gb, VI F, VII E
  (descending a semitone per position). Position changes are almost instantaneous with practice.
- Equivalence table (valves → slide position → lowering):
  none → 1st → 0; 2 → 2nd → semitone (1); 1 → 3rd → whole tone (2); 3 or 1+2 → 4th → m3 (3);
  2+3 → 5th → M3 (4); 1+3 → 6th → P4 (5); 1+2+3 → 7th → augmented 4th (6).
- Tuning slide (bomba de afinación): pulled out lowers pitch, pushed in raises; bigger corrections use the valve slides too.

**Rules**
- R26 [MUST] [era: crooked natural brass, c.1750–1850] Horns and trumpets were transposing instruments read in C;
  the crook supplied the key. Horn fifths (quintas de trompa) written in C sound a P5 lower on horn in F, a M6 lower on
  horn in Eb, a P4 lower on horn in G (Ex. 9-9; see Adler Ex. 15-13).
- R27 [ALLOWED] [era: crooked natural brass] Change crook (and therefore key/series) only where the player has rests.

**Agent applicability**
- MIDI is written at concert pitch; transposition (horn F −7, Eb −9, G −5; trumpet F +5) only matters for part
  output or for reading examples.
- Natural-horn period pastiche: two-horn "horn fifths" built only from series notes are idiomatic (inferred); exact
  notes of Ex. 9-9 not transcribed.

## Register (scan p.308, printed p.303)

**Rules**
- R28 [PREFER] Range depends on tube length and bore: wider bore → easier low notes/partials; narrower bore → easier
  high partials.
- R29 [ALLOWED] Pedal notes exist on several fundamentals (7 on trombone, one per valve combination on trumpet/horn),
  but are harder than upper partials; in solo and symphonic repertoire they appear mostly on trombone and horn.
- R30 [ALLOWED] [era: late-20th-c., jazz-derived] Trumpet pedal notes, borrowed from jazz, now appear in concert scores.

## Sound production, articulation, breathing (scan p.308–309, printed p.303–304)

**Concepts**
- Lips are the vibrating generator (like the reed for woodwinds). Embouchure relaxed for low notes, increasingly tense
  for high notes.
- Tonguing (ataque de lengua / picado) like woodwinds: single, double, triple tonguing all practical.

**Rules**
- R31 [AVOID] Very fast/complex articulation on very low notes. *Why:* relaxed embouchure makes some attacks problematic.
- R32 [AVOID] Soft attacks and finely controlled articulation in the extreme high register. *Why:* lip firmness and air
  speed needed make them hard.
- R33 [MUST] Brass need far more air than woodwinds and tire quickly: schedule frequent rests so players recover breath
  and lip elasticity.
- R34 [MUST] A slurred phrase is played in one breath: keep slurred phrases within one breath length.
- R35 [MUST] Unslurred notes will be tongued separately by the player.
- R36 [AVOID] Many notes in one breath in a loud, slow passage. *Why:* loud playing uses more air than soft.

**Agent applicability**
- In a brass MIDI line, insert rests (≥ 1 beat gaps) every few bars; at f/ff slow tempos keep phrases ≈ 2 bars (inferred).
- Overlapping notes (legato/slur in libraries) only inside one breath group; separate notes elsewhere.

## Common effects: attacks and tonguing (scan p.309–311, printed p.304–306)

**Rules**
- R37 [PREFER] Use brass for sforzando and forte-piano attacks: they do them better than any other section.
- R38 [ALLOWED] Light, soft, fast staccato tonguing can sound very delicate, not strident.
- R39 [ALLOWED] Double tonguing (picado doble), syllables "tu-ku"/"te-ke", for fast repeated notes in duple groupings.
- R40 [ALLOWED] Triple tonguing (picado triple), "te-ke-te" or "te-te-ke", for fast triplet repeated notes.
- R41 [ALLOWED] Flutter-tongue (frullato) is very effective and easy on all brass; notated like woodwind frullato
  (tremolo slashes on the stem).

**Examples**
- Ex. 9-10 (Adler): 4 horns in F, 3 trumpets in Bb, 3 trombones + tuba: full-brass chords sffz > pp < ff.
- Debussy, *Jeux*, 7 bars after [35]: trumpet in C, pp, light rapid staccato sixteenths, più pp.
- Ravel, *Rapsodie espagnole*, "Feria" at [6] (dotted quarter = 76): 4 horns + 3 trumpets in C, ff double-tongued repeated
  sixteenths in 6/8.
- Rimsky-Korsakov, *Scheherazade*, mvt 3 at [G] (♩ = 63): trumpet in A, *piano ma marcato assai*, triple-tongued triplets.
- R. Strauss, *Don Quixote*, var. II, bars 18–19: all muted, 6 horns in F, 3 trumpets in D, 3 trombones, flutter-tongued
  short chords, mf cresc. (bleating effect).

**Agent applicability**
- sfz-p-cresc: high velocity onset then CC1/CC11 dip to pp and swell back to ff over 1–2 bars.
- Repeated-note fast brass (double/triple tonguing) is idiomatic: use short/staccato articulations with full velocity
  accents on group starts.
- Muted flutter-tongue requires a library patch for it; else don't fake with fast repeats (inferred).

## Glissandi (scan p.311–312, printed p.306–307)

**Rules**
- R42 [ALLOWED] Trumpet and horn lip glissando (ligadura de labio): with normal lip pressure it runs quickly through the
  harmonic-series notes between first and last note.
- R43 [ALLOWED] [era: modern players] Smooth chromatic glissando on trumpet/horn by varying lip pressure.
- R44 [PREFER] Lip slurs/glissandi in the high register (partials closer together) and upward. *Why:* far easier than
  downward.
- R45 [ALLOWED] [era: jazz] Jazz trumpeters play "rips" in both directions.
- R46 [ALLOWED] Trumpet/horn glissandi are exciting dramatic devices and not vulgar.
- R47 [AVOID] Overusing trombone glissando: it is easy in both directions and has been overdemanded; used well it is very
  effective, otherwise exaggerated.
- R48 [PREFER] Tuba glissandi possible but less easy; write them ascending.

**Examples**
- Ex. 9-15 (Adler): ff full-brass glissandi: 4 horns and 3 Bb trumpets rip upward to a held chord; 3 trombones + tuba
  gliss upward into the chord a beat later.

**Agent applicability**
- Upward rips into a downbeat chord: start ~1 beat before, pitch-bend or library rip patch; avoid downward horn/trumpet rips.

## Trills and tremolos (scan p.312, printed p.307)

**Rules**
- R49 [ALLOWED] All brass can trill; most can play some tremolos satisfactorily.
- R50 [ALLOWED] Trills by lip (lip trills/lip slurs) or by valves; some players combine both.
- R51 [MUST] Trombone can trill only by lip trill (between adjacent partials). Per-instrument details: Adler ch. 10.

## Mutes (scan p.312–316, printed p.307–311)

**Concepts**
- Mute (sordina): cone-shaped plug in the bell with cork pads; changes timbre/character, not only volume; can give an
  incredibly soft pianissimo.
- Many mutes exist for trumpet and trombone; only one practical mute for horn and one for tuba; euphoniums and Wagner
  tubas also have mutes.

**Rules**
- R52 [ALLOWED] Full-brass fortissimo with mutes is exciting and widely used for ~100 years.
- R53 [MUST] Horn hand-stopping (tapar con la mano) raises pitch: the player compensates with hand adjustment or by
  fingering a semitone lower. The commercial horn mute corrects pitch, so the written note is played as written.
- R54 [PREFER] Trumpeters carry several mutes; trombonists rarely more than one (big, hard to carry), most likely a straight.
- R55 [MUST] "con sordino" alone = straight mute (sordina straight), the orchestral default for trumpets and trombones.
  Any other mute or muting device must be specified exactly in score and parts.
- R56 [ALLOWED] Straight mute works soft or loud. Fiber straight is less cutting than metal straight, esp. ff.
- R57 [AVOID] Cup mute (sordina de copa) in symphonic writing (associated with jazz bands). Section playing: cup open
  ~25 mm; solo/amplified: almost closed → dark, muffled; can also sound colorless, ghostly, nasal.
- R58 [ALLOWED] Harmon (wa-wa) mute: metal, with stem; stem removed, extended, or bell of stem covered/uncovered by hand
  for wa-wa ("+" = closed, "o" = open). Full harmon on trumpet = penetrating, bright. Rare in orchestra except comic or
  jazzy effects. Much duller in trombone.
- R59 [ALLOWED] Whisper mute (sordina whispa): softest of all, sounds offstage, near-inaudible when very soft.
- R60 [AVOID] Very high notes with whisper mute. *Why:* more effort needed, very high notes very hard.
- R61 [ALLOWED] Solotone mute: nasal, like an old radio or telephone.
- R62 [ALLOWED] Bucket mute ("velvet tone"): blocks most of the sound → soft and sweet.
- R63 [ALLOWED] Plunger (15 cm bowl with handle): muffles; held close to the bell with very fast notes → explosive sounds.
- R64 [ALLOWED] Hat/derby (sombrero): jazz device that reduces trumpet/trombone intensity without distorting, usable in any style.
- R65 [ALLOWED] Hand in front of / inside the bell (trumpet, trombone): muffles, less effective than mutes, alters pitch —
  composer must allow for it; can be used to combine pitch bend and muting.
- R66 [ALLOWED] Playing into the stand (tocar en el atril), bell ~5 cm from the stand: lowers force, brilliance and
  dynamic level (jazz usage).
- R67 [ALLOWED] Cloth/handkerchief in the bell: reduces volume and brightness with no pitch change and full control
  high and low.

**Examples**
- Vaughan Williams, *Symphony No. 6*, mvt 4, bars 39–42: 4 horns, 3 Bb trumpets, 3 trombones all con sord. pp,
  sustained soft chords — muted brass pianissimo.
- Lucien Cailliet's arrangement of *Pop Goes the Weasel*: comic harmon effects.
- Gershwin, *Rhapsody in Blue*: jazzy harmon wa-wa.
- Grofé, *Grand Canyon Suite*, "On the Trail" (small-orchestra arrangement): solotone on trombone solo.
- Ives, *The Unanswered Question*: trumpet with cloth in the bell.
- Additional passages for study: Berg *Lulu* suite (muted brass p→ff crescendo); Copland *Lincoln Portrait* (muted brass mf).

**Agent applicability**
- Muted brass ≠ quieter open brass: switch to a muted patch/articulation (straight by default) instead of lowering CC1.
- Default "con sord." on trumpet/trombone tracks = straight mute; horn mute is one type (stopped horn is a separate color).
- Muted brass can still play pp to ff; a muted ff tutti is a valid climax color.
- For offstage/distant trumpet: whisper or cloth-in-bell character; keep it out of the extreme high register.
