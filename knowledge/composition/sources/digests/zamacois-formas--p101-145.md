## V (cont.) Slow movement of the sonata: Lied forms (§155–156) (scan p.100–103, printed p.184–191)

**Concepts**
- Ternary Lied (Lied ternario): three sections A–B–A used as slow movement. Each section may itself be primary (one part), binary or ternary.
- Developed Lied / five-section Lied (Lied desarrollado, Lied en cinco secciones): rondo plan A–B–A–C–A applied to a slow movement.

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] Section I (exposition) states the principal phrase; when Section I is itself ternary (a little Lied): principal phrase (usually repeated |: :|) starts in the movement's key and usually ends in a neighbour key (or the tonic) → episodic phrase that modulates, on a new or derived motive, and leads back → re-exposition (whole or partial) of the principal phrase **in the tonic, ending in the tonic** (adjust its ending if the first statement ended elsewhere). §155
- R2 [PREFER] Section II (episodic/secondary) uses new or derived material, works as a transition/link, may have any internal structure, usually **starts in a neighbour key**, and ends so as to enter Section III smoothly. §155. The structure of one section never dictates the structure of another (footnote).
- R3 [MUST] Section III = re-exposition (total or partial) of Section I in the home key; often **enriched melodically/rhythmically** (ornamental variation of the repeat), and may be extended with a small development. §155
- R4 [ALLOWED] Coda built from the principal elements of Sections I and II. §155 (op.28 example).
- R5 [MUST] Five-section Lied: I (principal) – II (secondary) – III (= I) – IV (secondary, independent of II though it may reuse its elements) – V (= I); coda optional. §156
- R6 [PREFER] In the five-section Lied, sections are **shorter** than in the ternary Lied. §156
- R7 [PREFER] In the final return (V), keep accompaniment figuration introduced in IV (e.g. 16th-note triplets) as rhythmic enrichment of the theme. §156 (op.13 example)

**Examples**
- op.28 Andante, D minor, 2/4: Sec. I = 8-bar phrase, repeated, D minor → A minor; secondary phrase bb.10–17 begins and ends on the dominant of D (suspensive); principal motive re-exposed as a 6-bar phrase with the bar-2 rhythm foregrounded, feminine ending on the tonic. Sec. II in **D major** (tonic major), itself ternary (8 expo + 4 episode + 4 re-expo) on one thematic element. Sec. III (b.43): repeats written out as ornamental variation (bb.51–58, 73–86). Coda 17 bars (b.87–end) uses elements of I (b.87) and II (b.93).
- op.13 Adagio, Ab major, 2/4 (five sections): I = 8-bar phrase, repeated an octave higher with texture change, ends on tonic. II = two new elements: F minor → Eb; then Eb stabilised and turned into V of Ab. III = principal phrase in tonic, **not repeated** (bb.29–36). IV = new phrase Ab minor → E major (enharmonic Fb, bVI, subdominant function) bb.36–44 + 6 bars to V7 of Ab. V = full re-exposition (bb.55–66) with 16th-triplet accompaniment from IV. Coda affirms Ab.

**Agent applicability**
- Slow movement template A (ternary): A(8+8, I→V or I) | B(neighbour key, transitional, ends on V7) | A'(tonic, ornamented) | coda (A+B motifs).
- Slow movement template B (rondo-Lied): A B A C A, each block shorter; C may go to a remote key (bVI) and return via V7; carry C's figuration into the last A.

## V (cont.) Sonata form in the slow movement (§157) (scan p.102, printed p.188–189)

**Rules**
- R8 [ALLOWED] Slow movement may use sonata form complete, or **without development**, the development replaced by a short link (conducción) from end of exposition to start of re-exposition. §157
- R9 [PREFER] In slow-movement sonata form, Bridge and Theme B have less personality than in a first movement; Theme B often reduced to a single element. §157
- R10 [MUST] Re-exposition bridge starts like the exposition's but bends its tonal path to end on V7 of the tonic (or on the tonic), so B returns in the tonic. §157 examples

**Examples**
- op.10/1 Adagio, Ab, 2/4 (no development): A 16 bars Ab→Ab; bridge bb.17–23 new element, V7 of Bb → V7 of Eb; B b.24 in Eb (b′+b″); codetta b.42 in Eb + one transitional bar turning Eb into V7 of Ab. Re-expo: A b.46 Ab; bridge b.62 same material, ends on V7 of Ab; B b.71 Ab; coda b.91 glosses A.
- op.22 Adagio, Eb, 9/8 (with development): A 12 bars; bridge 6 bars Eb→Bb; B in Bb (b′ b.18, b″ b.22, b‴ b.27); development on A b.31; re-expo A b.47; bridge b.57 ends in Eb; B b.65 in Eb, all elements; no coda.

## V (cont.) Minuet and Scherzo of the sonata (§157bis–160) (scan p.103–106, printed p.190–197)

**Concepts**
- Sonata minuet type (tipo Minué de Sonata): ternary A (Minuet) – B (Trio) – A (Minuet da capo); each section usually itself ternary. Contrast: the suite minuet is binary (§123).
- Trio: Section II; name from three players/dancers; when the mode changes some editions label sections *Maggiore/Minore*.
- Scherzo: "joke"; fast, playful; Beethoven replaced the minuet by it, keeping the minuet form but dropping the dance character.

**Rules**
- R11 [MUST] Minuet (Sec. I) internal plan: |: principal phrase :| |: episode (derived or new) → re-exposition (whole or part) of principal phrase **ending in the tonic** (+ optional codetta) :|. §158
- R12 [PREFER] The principal phrase of the minuet ends **almost always in the dominant** (or the relative major if in minor), occasionally in the tonic. §158
- R13 [ALLOWED] The re-exposition may be condensed: what were two phrases become one conclusive period. Repeat signs may be written out (necessary if details change). §158 footnote
- R14 [MUST] Trio key: same tonic (mode changed or not), or a key whose tonic chord shares a common tone with the main tonic chord; **subdominant preferred** among the latter. §158
- R15 [PREFER] Trio theme contrasts with the minuet (not obligatory). The Trio may end with a conclusive or a suspensive cadence (to lead into the da capo). §158
- R16 [MUST] Section III = re-exposition of I **without repeats**; if unchanged it is not written out: "D.C." at the end of the Trio. A general coda is optional. §158
- R17 [ALLOWED] Sections may be binary (uncommon) or primary (almost never) instead of ternary (op.10/3 Trio has one part; op.31/3 Sec. I binary, principal motive not re-exposed). Exceptions: binary minuet (early), rondo-minuet (op.49/2). §158
- R18 [PREFER] Scherzo: fast tempo, triple meter preferred (3/4 or 3/8), playful. Same ternary plan as the minuet but freer lines, longer, richer (often contrapuntal) writing; **Trio theme usually broad, a dominating melody**. §158bis–159
- R19 [ALLOWED] A movement can have minuet/scherzo form without that title (op.7 III, op.14/1 II, op.101 II "Marcha"); and a piece titled Scherzo can be rondo (op.14/2) or sonata form (op.31/3). §158bis, §160

**Examples**
- op.2/1 Minuet, F minor, 3/4: I: |: phrase f → Ab :| |: derived episode → C (dominant); re-expo with theme in left hand, ends f :|. Trio in **F major**: |: phrase → dominant :| |: episode ends on V; re-expo ends on I :|. III = D.C.
- op.2/3 Scherzo, C major, 3/4: I: |: 16-bar theme, cadence in G :| |: episode on same element, re-expo in C, coda on same element :|. Trio in **A minor (relative)**, theme in the bass, ends in E minor; episode 8 bars; re-expo ends in A minor; written-out repeat ends suspensively on V7 of C. III = D.C., coda extends Sec. I's coda.

**Agent applicability**
- Minuet/Scherzo generator: Minuet ‖: a(8, I→V) :‖: b(4–8, on V) a′(8, →I) :‖ – Trio (IV, parallel mode, or relative) same shape, may end on V7 – Minuet D.C. without repeats.
- For a scherzo use 3/4 at fast tempo (one beat per bar feel), add imitative/contrapuntal texture, give the Trio a long cantabile melody.

## V (cont.) Final fast movement: Rondo and Rondo-Sonata (§161–162) (scan p.106–107, printed p.196–199)

**Concepts**
- Simple rondo: refrain (estribillo), more or less extended, normally in the tonic, alternating with episodes (coplas) in other keys, freely built.
- Rondo-sonata (rondo desarrollado): Copla I built and later re-exposed like a sonata Theme B (with or without bridge); other coplas may act like a development.

**Rules**
- R20 [MUST] Rondo-sonata plan (default, not immutable): E1 (tonic = Theme A) – C1 (optional bridge + Theme B in a related key, **not the tonic**, may have several elements) – E2 (A whole/partial, tonic) – C2 (new element in a new neighbour key, **or** development of earlier material) – E3 (A, complete or not, tonic) – C3 (= C1 re-exposed with sonata re-exposition tonality: **B in the tonic**) – E4 (A in tonic, or development of its element) – coda (optional, free). §161
- R21 [PREFER] If there are more than three coplas, the re-expositive one is usually the **last**, not the third. §161 footnote
- R22 [ALLOWED] The refrain may be rhythmically/melodically modified, shortened (e.g. only first 8 bars) or lengthened; a refrain not in the tonic is possible (op.78 finale: one refrain in the subdominant). §161 footnote
- R23 [PREFER] End coplas on a suspensive gesture (e.g. descending scale onto V7) and invert that gesture into an affirmative one in the coda. §161 (op.13 example)
- R24 [ALLOWED] Finale alternatives: sonata form, variations, fugue. Statistics of Beethoven's 32 sonatas: 19 rondo finales, 9 sonata form, 2 variation, 2 fugue; last 4 sonatas avoid rondo and sonata-form finales. §162

**Examples**
- op.13 Rondo, C minor, 4/4: E1 ends b.17 in c. C1: bridge b.18; B in **Eb** (b′ b.25, b″ b.37, b‴ b.43); repeated b″ leads to V7 of c. E2 exact (bb.61–78). C2: new element in **Ab** (b.79), self-developing, ends on V of c; transition b.107. E3: first 8 bars only (bb.120–128). C3: new bridge from refrain-end material (b.128); B in **C** (tonic, major) b.134 without repeating b″. E4: first 8 bars. Coda b.178: refrain-end element, b″, refrain cell in Ab, ends on tonic with an affirmative descending scale.

**Agent applicability**
- Finale template: A(I) B(III or V) A(I) C(IV/VI or development) A(I) B(I) A(I)+coda; shorten later refrains to their first 8 bars to keep momentum.

**Tags:** #form/rondo #form/rondo-sonata #form/finale

## V (cont.) Post-classical sonata, two-trio scherzo, cyclic sonata (§163–165) (scan p.108–110, printed p.200–205)

**Rules**
- R25 [ALLOWED] Post-classical tendencies: (a) drop the minuet for a scherzo or another piece; (b) heavier finale (weightier rondo, sonata form, other); (c) free keys for middle refrains of a rondo; (d) freer keys for inner movements and for Theme B, even outside common-tone relations; (e) scherzo with two trios; (f) cyclic writing. §163
- R26 [MUST] Scherzo with two different Trios (Schumann; "scherzo in rondo form"): S – T1 – S – T2 – S (+ optional coda). T2 (also "Intermezzo") is freer in everything — a development of T1 or wholly different, **even in meter/tempo**. The last S may be a re-exposition-development. §164
- R27 [ALLOWED] Beethoven variant (outside sonatas): two identical trios, S–T–S–T–S. §164 footnote
- R28 [PREFER] Cyclic sonata (sonata cíclica): unify movements with themes or cells that recur in several/all movements, enlarged, reduced or transformed, in foreground or background. §165

**Examples**
- Schumann Sonata F# minor, scherzo: S in f#; T1 in **A** (relative), ternary; S exact minus last 4 bars; T2 "Intermezzo" in **D**, slow tempo, ternary, then an ad-libitum passage leading to the final S.
- Beethoven op.13: a rising stepwise cell from the introduction reappears in mvt I (b′), II and III. op.57: opening theme split into cells A, B, C → B feeds mvt I b′, C → mvt II theme, A → mvt III.
- Franck Violin Sonata: opening theme returns transformed — episodically in II, twice in III, in canon-like form in IV.

**Agent applicability**
- To unify a multi-movement work, derive each movement's main theme from one 3–5-note cell (transpose, augment, change rhythm) and quote the opening theme in the finale.

**Tags:** #form/scherzo #form/cyclic #sonata/movements

## V (cont.) Chamber music and Symphony (§166–168) (scan p.110–111, printed p.204–207)

**Rules**
- R29 [MUST] Default instrumentation by name: Trio = piano, violin, cello; Quartet = 2 violins, viola, cello. The names count players only; the form is the sonata's. §166
- R30 [PREFER] String quartet writing: the most austere, pure lines, careful polyphony. §166
- R31 [ALLOWED] Ensembles of more than 4 soloists follow sonata form less, closer to the free Cassation (§140). §166
- R32 [PREFER] Symphony = sonata for orchestra, usually **4 movements** (more is exceptional, fewer than 3 even more so); usually purely orchestral, sometimes with piano, organ or voices (Beethoven 9: vocal soloists + mixed choir). "Sinfonía" may also mean overture. §167–167bis

**Tags:** #form/symphony #form/chamber #orchestration/ensembles

## V (cont.) Concerto (§169–176) (scan p.111–114, printed p.206–213)

**Concepts**
- Concerto (concierto): multi-movement work for the display (lucimiento) of one or more soloists with orchestral collaboration.
- Concerto grosso (Corelli): string orchestra split into soloist group **concertino** and the rest **grosso/ripieno**; form between fugue and suite.
- Double exposition (doble exposición): classical concerto first movement — Exposition 1 by orchestra, Exposition 2 by soloist with orchestra.
- Tutti: full ensemble sound, during which the soloist rests or doubles.
- Cadenza (cadencia): unaccompanied solo passage.
- Sinfonia concertante: solos chosen for musical, not virtuoso reasons; orchestra is the protagonist.

**Rules**
- R33 [MUST] Pre-classical and classical concerto: **3 movements fast–slow–fast** (last usually the fastest); only the middle movement may be in a different key. §171–172
- R34 [ALLOWED] The 2nd movement may end on a half cadence and run attacca into the 3rd. §171
- R35 [MUST] Pre-classical (ritornello) first movement: Sec. I orchestra states the principal theme + complements, ends with a cadence in the tonic → Sec. II alternates free solo episodes with appearances of the theme (or its head) in various keys (regular → rondo with refrains in different keys; irregular → like a fugue's middle section) → Sec. III principal theme in the home key, optionally Sec. I's complements and a coda. §171
- R36 [PREFER] Pre-classical roles: orchestra (nearly always alone) states and restates the theme; soloist shines in the episodes, discreetly supported; the soloist also plays in tutti; the orchestra may briefly underline theme fragments inside episodes. §171, §174
- R37 [MUST] Classical concerto first movement = sonata form with double exposition; the orchestra takes Exposition 1, opens the development and re-exposition and closes the movement (boundaries blur later). §172, §174
- R38 [MUST] When Theme B appears in both expositions: in Exposition 2 it is in the sonata key (V or III, §148); in Exposition 1 it is **in the tonic or drifts toward it**. §172.4
- R39 [ALLOWED] Expositions may differ: B (or its main element) omitted in Exp. 1 (compensated by expanding A); A omitted or only hinted in Exp. 2; complementary elements reordered, varied or replaced; soloist may open Exp. 2 with a new element preceding A. The re-exposition is then normally complete, mixing both expositions. §172.2
- R40 [AVOID] Repeating a thematic element identically in both expositions: condense, enlarge, simplify, enrich or swap it between orchestra and soloist. §172.5
- R41 [PREFER] Give the soloist the longest display in the bridge and in b″/b‴ (complementary elements); these are longer than in a sonata. §174
- R42 [MUST] Cadenza placement: the orchestra prepares it with the **cadential 6/4**, falls silent (fermata over a rest marked "Cadenza"), the solo plays, and the orchestra re-enters on **V(7)** to close. In sonata-form movements almost always between development and re-exposition, or at the end of the re-exposition; in rondos before or after the last refrain. §175
- R43 [PREFER] Cadenza content: virtuoso gloss/development of the movement's principal themes. Romantic option: drop it or accompany it with orchestra. §175
- R44 [ALLOWED] Post-classical concerto: lighten or drop the orchestral Exposition 1; instead state A immediately twice (orchestra→soloist or reverse) and likewise B. §173
- R45 [ALLOWED] Soloist may open the work alone (Beethoven 4: piano states A's head as a half-cadence period, then orchestra; Beethoven 5: piano and orchestra together, piano plays an introductory cadenza-like passage before orchestral Exp. 1; Schumann: short energetic solo introduction absent from re-expo). §172–173
- R46 [ALLOWED] Double/triple concerto = 2/3 soloists. §176

**Examples**
- Corelli Concerto grosso in C: Largo (24 bars) + Adagio (4) framing a fugato Allegro (C minor); Grave in F minor ending on half cadence → Vivace (C minor) binary suite-type; Allegro (C) = gigue. Corelli Concerto grosso in F: Preludio, Allemanda, Corrente, Giga, Sarabanda, Gavotta, Minuetto all in F (= suite).
- Vivaldi Violin Concerto in A, I: orchestra states theme with all elements, closes in tonic; soloist enters with theme → episode; theme (orch.) in E; solo episode; re-expo (orch.) in tonic with solo episodes inserted between its three elements.
- Bach Harpsichord Concerto in D minor, I: theme (orch.) – solo episode – theme with half cadence toward A minor; long solo episode on the theme head; theme complete in A minor; episode; theme in G minor (suspensive); longer episode; final section in tonic, last theme statement identical to the first.
- Bach Double Violin Concerto D minor, I: Theme I in 5 fugal orchestral entries; Theme II stated by each soloist in turn (tonic); middle in A minor; return in D minor.
- Mendelssohn Violin Concerto: orchestra gives one bar of A's accompaniment, soloist enters with A; orchestra takes A immediately; reverse for B.

**Agent applicability**
- Concerto first movement plan (classical): Orch. Exp. (A, bridge, B in tonic) → Solo Exp. (A, bridge, B in V/III, virtuoso codetta) → Dev. → Re-expo (all in I) → orch. builds to I6/4, fermata, cadenza, V7 → orch. coda.
- Ritornello plan (baroque): R(I) S R(V) S R(vi or ii) S R(I); solo episodes use figuration on the ritornello head.

## VI Overture and Prelude (§177–185) (scan p.115–117, printed p.214–218)

**Concepts**
- Overture/Prelude (Obertura/Preludio): single-movement piece serving as a portico.
- French overture (Lully): slow–fast–slow. Italian overture/"Sinfonia" (A. Scarlatti): fast–slow–fast.
- Synthesis overture (obertura síntesis de la ópera): programmatic, condenses the drama's themes. Mosaic overture (obertura mosaico): string of opera tunes for effect. Symphonic overture: own material.

**Rules**
- R47 [ALLOWED] Keyboard prelude before a fugue: totally free form, often no theme — a rhythmic/melodic figure with harmony dissolved in arpeggios/scales that establishes the fugue's key; or a persistent theme. §178
- R48 [MUST] French and Italian overtures: three linked movements, the last a re-exposition of the first; French = Lento–Allegro–Lento, Italian = Allegro–Lento–Allegro; the Allegro usually fugato. §181
- R49 [PREFER] Rameau two-movement type (most cultivated): Lento → Allegro. The Lento prepares the Allegro (a few bars up to a broad melodic phrase) and its tonal path always ends so the Allegro enters **in the principal key, same or opposite mode**. §181
- R50 [PREFER] Classical opera overture: the Allegro after the slow intro adopts sonata form, either exposition–re-exposition without development (Mozart, Figaro) or full exposition–development–re-exposition (Don Giovanni). §182
- R51 [ALLOWED] Insert an episode taken from the drama, preferably during or at the end of the development (Beethoven Leonore 3: distant offstage trumpet call). §182
- R52 [PREFER] Synthesis overture: choose and order the drama's themes by their dramatic meaning; solid, balanced architecture, symphonic-quality thematic work (sonata-based or free). §183
- R53 [AVOID] Mosaic overture approach (themes merely placed one after another and combined for external effect, not developed): judged formally much inferior. §183
- R54 [PREFER] Wagnerian/post-Wagnerian opera prelude: short material limited to the theme(s) of the central feeling (Tristan); or just a brief introduction to raise the curtain; don't anticipate the drama with a large symphonic piece. §184
- R55 [ALLOWED] Concert overture: independent piece inspired by an unstaged drama, or any piece with overture form. §185

**Agent applicability**
- Opera/film-style overture: slow intro (ends on V of home key) → Allegro in sonata form → optional quoted "dramatic" episode before the re-exposition.
- French overture pastiche: slow dotted-rhythm Lento → fugato Allegro → short Lento return.

**Tags:** #form/overture #form/prelude #genre/opera #form/french-overture

## VII Symphonic poem, Ballet, Incidental music (§186–197) (scan p.117–119, printed p.219–223)

**Concepts**
- Symphonic poem (poema sinfónico): usually purely orchestral, one movement, develops a literary programme without words (Berlioz, Liszt; peak R. Strauss). Smaller relatives: Cuadro/Leyenda/Cuento sinfónico.
- Ballet: music in one or more parts of variable form for choreographic performance. Ballet-pantomime/Mimo-danza (dance + mime with a plot); Pantomima lírica (mime + music only). French court ballet divided in acts and **entrées** (entradas, = scenes).
- Incidental music (ilustraciones musicales, música de escena): independent pieces as background to spoken theatre (Rousseau, Pygmalion 1773).

**Rules**
- R56 [MUST] Symphonic poem: form is invented per work, adapted to the programme, but the musical structure must not be stifled by the poem — cut, add, enlarge or reduce the story as needed. *Why:* the more the form is subordinated to the poem, the less balance and perfection. §188
- R57 [ALLOWED] Programme detail ranges from bar-by-bar narration to a general idea/feeling only. §188
- R58 [PREFER] Classical (French) ballet music: successive independent, clearly delimited pieces coordinated by an overall idea; the composer chooses each piece's form freely; external effect over inner drama. §194
- R59 [PREFER] Russian ballet (Diaghilev): music conceived like a symphonic poem, programmatic, entirely free form, designed to drive the mime-dance. §195
- R60 [PREFER] Incidental music aims to create atmosphere around the spoken action (evocative, descriptive, picturesque) rather than carry the action itself; its pieces (overtures, intermezzi, dances, songs) keep their own forms. §197

**Agent applicability**
- For a programmatic cue, map story beats to musical sections but keep recognisable thematic returns so the piece stands as music.
- A ballet suite = chain of short, self-contained dance numbers, each with its own form/meter.

**Tags:** #form/symphonic-poem #genre/ballet #genre/incidental-music #programmatic

## VIII Other types: compositions defined by tempo or rhythm — dances (§198–203) (scan p.120–122, printed p.224–229)

**Concepts**
- Walked dances (danzas andadas) vs jumped dances (danzas saltadas); the distinction shows in their music.
- Tanda/Suite de valses: weighted ordering of more waltz sections than one waltz has (key chain, contrast, motif returns).

**Rules**
- R61 [PREFER] Dance pieces (post-suite) use either the sonata-minuet ternary form (§158) or rondo (§127/161); exceptions affect only secondary aspects — the re-exposition(s) of the main part always remain. §198
- R62 [MUST] Dance pieces are made of perfectly defined parts forming a closed whole, joined at most by audible "welds" (soldaduras) or short modulating transitions. Optional Introduction and Coda (from a few bars up to developing a foreign idea or glossing main motifs). §198
- R63 [MUST] March (Marcha): binary or quaternary meter (binary more common), simple or compound. Typical rhythm: beat split long–short — **dotted note + complement (3/4 + 1/4 of the beat) in simple meter, 2/3 + 1/3 in compound** — to accent a firm step. §199
- R64 [MUST] Match march type to tempo/character: funeral (slow), solemn/nuptial, military (brilliant, resolute), *marcha redoblada* / pasodoble. §199
- R65 [MUST] Polonaise (Polonesa): **3/4, moderate tempo**, walked, brilliant/showy. Rhythmic markers: **feminine final cadence between beats 2 and 3**; frequent **dotted 16th + 32nd**. Typical accompaniment: running 8ths, first 8th = harmonic bass, rest = chord; also the bolero/castanet rhythm (8th, two 16ths, then 8ths — i.e. the second 8th of beat 1 split into two 16ths). §200
- R66 [MUST] Mazurka: **3/4, relatively lively**, lighter than the polonaise (jumped steps). Markers: **feminine cadences**; frequent **dotted 8th + 16th (or 8th-rest replacing the dot) on beat 1**. §201
- R67 [MUST] Waltz (Vals): turning dance with some swaying; notated 3/4 or 3/8 but each bar really = one beat (compound binary feel: pairs of bars). Tempo from fast to slow waltz. Rhythm smooth, **no special rhythmic formulas** (no jumps). Melodic sections in long clear values alternate with busy episodic sections for contrast. Accompaniment: **bass on beat 1, chord on beats 2 and 3** (or on one of them). §202
- R68 [AVOID] Mazurka rhythms/cadences (dotted beat 1, feminine endings) in a waltz — they are "totally foreign" to it. §202
- R69 [MUST] Tarantella: Neapolitan, lively, **3/8 or 6/8**; brilliant instrumental piece; continuous 8ths or the very characteristic **quarter + eighth** pattern. §203
- R70 [ALLOWED] Ländler: popular relative of the waltz, tempo close to the mazurka. §202

**Examples**
- Wagner, Tannhäuser march (B major, 4/4): repeated chords on every beat, dotted melody.
- Chopin Polonaise 3 (A major, 3/4): ending with feminine cadence; accompaniment bass 8th + chord 16ths/8ths.
- Chopin Mazurka 5 (Bb, 3/4): end of first part with dotted figure on beat 1.
- Chopin Waltz 6 (Db, 3/4): bass on 1, chords on 2–3, melody with grace notes.
- Rossini/Liszt Tarantella: Vivace 6/8, running 8ths.

**Agent applicability**
- Dance-rhythm checklist when generating MIDI: polonaise → 3/4 ♩≈ moderate, bar 1 bolero figure in accompaniment, cadence melody lands on beat 2–3; mazurka → accent/dot on beat 1, feminine endings; waltz → oom-pah-pah, phrase in 2-bar units, no dotted beat 1; tarantella → 6/8 fast, ♩♪ pattern; march → 2/4 or 4/4 with ♪.♬ (dotted 8th–16th) pickups.
- Wrap any dance in ternary (A–Trio–A) or rondo form with optional intro/coda.

## VIII (cont.) Compositions without special rhythm or form — character pieces (§204–219) (scan p.122–125, printed p.229–235)

**Concepts**
- These titles express a poetic idea, not a structure (like Sonata/Fugue) nor a dance tempo (like Gavotte/Waltz). Titles are loose and interchangeable (an Impromptu could be called Ballade or Nocturne).

**Rules**
- R71 [ALLOWED] Form is independent of title: use any classified form or an original one. In Romantic practice, re-expositive types based on the Lied (ternary) and rondo are by far the most frequent. §204 + footnote
- R72 [PREFER] Fantasia: free; alternate parts of defined structure with rapid figuration (scales, arpeggios); improvisatory feel; frequent changes of meter, tempo and theme. Also = chain of opera tunes ("Fantasia on Norma"); name the source. §205
- R73 [PREFER] Capriccio: formally = fantasia; character of freedom, surprise, wit, daring. Also historically a series of fugal developments on one theme with changing meter. §206
- R74 [PREFER] Toccata (keyboard): opens (oldest masters) with full broad chords, then brilliant virtuoso or fugato fragments; melody interrupted by fast passages, arpeggios, scales, broken chords; tempo changes, sudden themes, truncated developments. After the sonata it is only an archaic evocation. §207
- R75 [ALLOWED] Pastoral: evokes shepherds; historically a dance in **3/4 or 6/8, moderate tempo**, imitating a shepherd's tune on a typical instrument. §208
- R76 [PREFER] Impromptu: sounds spontaneous but is balanced and precise — no motley texture, no missing transitions, no theme-shuffling or tempo changes; ternary re-expositive form preferred (Schubert, Chopin; Schubert op.142/3 is theme and variations). §209
- R77 [PREFER] Song without words (Romanza sin palabras, Mendelssohn): relatively short piano piece, melody dominant and intensely expressive. §210
- R78 [ALLOWED] Ballade/Legend (Balada/Leyenda/Cuento/Fábula): narrative, epic-lyric character; the music unfolds like telling a story. §211–212
- R79 [PREFER] Elegy: plaintive, commemorative. §214. Nocturne (Field; Chopin): serene, poetic-contemplative. §215
- R80 [MUST] Berceuse (lullaby): tender, simple melody that alone carries the piece; **accompaniment always on a rocking rhythm** (cradle). §216
- R81 [MUST] Barcarolle/Veneziana/Gondoliera: rocking rhythm like the berceuse (boat swaying, gentle), but melody of lovers' romance or a lone rower's song (joyful, nostalgic or melancholic). §217
- R82 [PREFER] Rhapsody: freely linked themes/airs of diverse character, aiming at variety and brilliance; commonly **two linked parts slow–fast (Lento–Allegro)**; Liszt: *lassan* (slow, melodic) + *friska* (fast dance), from Hungarian czardas. §218 (Brahms's two rhapsodies: rondo and sonata form.)
- R83 [ALLOWED] Moment musical, Lyric piece, Album leaf: short miniatures, vague titles, any form. §219

**Agent applicability**
- Character-piece default: ternary A–B–A with a contrasting middle; pick texture from the title (rocking 6/8 for berceuse/barcarolle; free arpeggio runs for fantasia/toccata; slow–fast diptych for rhapsody).

## IX Secular dramatic music: Song and Lied (§220–225) (scan p.126–127, printed p.236–239)

**Concepts**
- Song (canción, strict sense): brief lyric piece, verse text, dominant but simple melody, no vocal display. Two groups: popular (anonymous, traditional, modal interest) and original.
- Strophic coplas (coplas): same music for every strophe, text changes. Coplas + refrain (coplas y estribillo): changing verses + repeated verses (origin of rondo, §127).
- Lied (pl. Lieder): original song for one singer, artistic but intimate, no vocal effects, poetry and music fused, **music serving the text**; accompaniment reinforces the word's expression. Schubert consecrated it.

**Rules**
- R84 [MUST] Popular song form: text in metrically equal strophes; the music covers **one strophe** and repeats for each. If harmonised, harmony may repeat or vary. §223
- R85 [ALLOWED] In popular songs word stress often clashes with metric stress (badly accented words) — normal in that idiom. §223 footnote
- R86 [ALLOWED] Lied structure adds nothing new: any form (strophic types of §223 or the ternary "Lied type" of §§31, 32, 46, 155); the "Lied form" name comes from old German folk song, but Lieder aren't bound to it. §225
- R87 [AVOID] Calling a piece a Lied when music dominates the words (French *mélodie*) or when it is bombastic (emphatic romanza). §225

**Examples**
- "Córtame un ramito verde" (Asturian, G major, 2/4): plain coplas type, one melody for all strophes.
- "En el portal de Belén" (Andalusian, A major, 6/8): coplas + estribillo ("Pastores venid…").

**Agent applicability**
- For a strophic song: compose one strophe-length melody (match bar count to the verse's lines) and loop it, changing only lyrics/arrangement; add a refrain block for the coplas-and-estribillo variant.

**Tags:** #form/strophic #form/song #genre/lied #vocal/text-setting

## IX (cont.) Polyphonic song and Madrigal (§226–232) (scan p.127–128, printed p.239–241)

**Rules**
- R88 [MUST] Old polyphonic secular forms (frottola, villanella, caccia = canonic, canzona, canzonetta, ballade, rondeau, vocal pavane/galliard) all reduce to the two popular types: coplas (same music for all strophes) or coplas + refrain; some add instrumental preludes, interludes, postludes. §227
- R89 [PREFER] Modern choral arrangement of folk songs = decorative variation (§107): repeat the melody once per strophe, each time with new counterpoint or harmonisation. §226 footnote
- R90 [MUST] 16th-c. madrigal: a cappella voices, secular poetry, music depicting the text as faithfully as possible; **free structure modelled on the text**; themes short and varied, repeated even when the words aren't; elegant style. *Why:* thematic variety gives it agility versus single-theme forms. §230
- R91 [ALLOWED] Madrigal simple (voices), accompanied (voices + instruments doubling weak parts), instrumental (instrument replaces the vocal soloist → seed of the concerto). §231
- R92 [ALLOWED] Choral poem (poema coral): large-scale a cappella work of high expressive ambition. §232

**Tags:** #genre/madrigal #texture/polyphony #vocal/a-cappella

## IX (cont.) Cantata and Oratorio (§233–235) (scan p.129–130, printed p.242–244)

**Rules**
- R93 [MUST] Cantata/oratorio (now merged): set a whole literary text, generally religious but **not liturgical**; borrow opera's dramatic style, recitative and accompanied melody while keeping polyphonic richness; base on **large choral numbers** with interspersed **recitatives that narrate** the action, followed by **arias, duets etc. that comment** on it; orchestra (sometimes organ) supports throughout. §234
- R94 [PREFER] Cantata: shorter — lyric expression of one feeling about one event; oratorio: a succession of them. Early cantata = simple alternation recitative–aria. §233–234 footnote
- R95 [ALLOWED] A narrator (historicus/recitador) explains the action in early oratorio (later dropped). §235

**Agent applicability**
- Oratorio scene unit: recitative (advance story) → aria/duet (reflect) → chorus (collective statement).

## IX (cont.) Opera and the elements of the stile rappresentativo (§236–251) (scan p.130–134, printed p.244–252)

**Concepts**
- Opera: any staged work fully set to music with sung words (Florence late 16th c.; Monteverdi).
- Grand opera (Meyerbeer): historical plot, constant stage movement (crowds, parades, marches, processions), indispensable large ballet, singer display, effects. Opéra comique: alternates music and spoken dialogue (also operetta, zarzuela). Opera buffa: jocose/grotesque (Barber of Seville). Lyric drama (Wagner): music develops the drama fused with word; no set numbers; orchestra is the main character.
- Leitmotiv (motivo conductor): theme tied to a character/object/idea; in Wagner continually transformed (rhythm, melody, harmony, counterpoint, tempo) and woven into the orchestral fabric.
- Stile rappresentativo: alternation of musical declamation (recitative) close to speech with moments of full song.
- Ritornello: instrumental passage while the voice rests, at start (= introduction) or end (leading back to a repeat); madrigalists' name for the refrain.

**Rules**
- R96 [MUST] An opera cannot be one formal type: divide it into delimited parts linked and combined by an overall idea. Traditional numbers: Recitative and Aria, Duo, Cavatina, Scena and Romanza, etc. §244
- R97 [MUST] Recitative: free tonal course (modulate at will, end where convenient); **no phrase/period structure**; aim at the least possible sensation of "music", approaching natural speech. §245
- R98 [MUST] Recitativo secco: voice nearly free in meter, supported only by chords (originally harpsichord realising figured bass) that define key, steer modulations and make cadences; stock formulas common to all composers. Carries the plot action; arias only comment on moments/feelings. §245
- R99 [MUST] Recitativo accompagnato/expressive (expresivo): **measured** (with flexibility); orchestra with obbligato parts comments, describes and interprets the text while the voice keeps lyric declamation; orchestral interest subordinate to the voice's text. §245
- R100 [PREFER] Recitative acts as a neutral background against which the clear lines of arias stand out. §245
- R101 [MUST] Aria da capo / Gran Aria (A. Scarlatti): ternary A–B–A; A = vocal virtuosity, **even more in the return (ornamented variants)**; B = more melodic and simpler for the singer but richer in harmony/counterpoint; an orchestral ritornello on the main motive serves as introduction. §246
- R102 [ALLOWED] 18th-c. two-part aria: part 1 expressive, more or less slow → part 2 animated with bravura and fioriture; a recitative may precede it or sit between the parts; optional coda; return and ritornello often dropped. §246
- R103 [ALLOWED] Arietta = short aria; Arioso = recitative gradually becoming aria; Cavatina = lighter aria (or aria sung at a character's first entrance, *aria di sortita*). §247–248
- R104 [ALLOWED] Romanza (vocal): sentimental love song, melody preponderant; no fixed form (ternary re-expositive common, also strophic); in opera often prepared by a recitative; the principal solo number. §249
- R105 [ALLOWED] Duo/Terzetto/Quartet: name = number of solo voices, free form; voices alternate (dialogue) or sing together (unison or not). §250
- R106 [PREFER] Concertante (pezzo concertato): placed almost always at the **end of an act**; all (main) characters + chorus + orchestra as real parts, grandiloquent effect. §251

**Examples**
- Mozart, Figaro I/iv (Marcellina): secco recitative — fast repeated 8th/16th syllables on few pitches over sustained chords.

**Agent applicability**
- Secco recitative in MIDI: voice in speech-rhythm 8ths/16ths, minimal range, harpsichord/strings sustain block chords changing only at cadence points; cadence V–I after the vocal phrase.
- Leitmotif scoring: assign each character a 2–4-bar motif and vary its rhythm/harmony/tempo per scene instead of repeating it literally.
- Act finale: stack all voices + chorus + full orchestra (concertato) for the climax.

## X Religious music: generalities (§252–257) (scan p.134–137, printed p.253–258)

**Concepts**
- Plainchant/Gregorian chant (canto llano/gregoriano): originally unaccompanied.
- Versicle (versículo): short, roughly equal-length text unit of liturgical chant.
- Syllabic (one note per syllable) vs melismatic (many notes per syllable) chant.
- Cantus firmus (canto firme; also "Tenor", "Coral", "Motete"): borrowed melody, usually in long equal notes, that is the backbone of contrapuntal combinations.

**Rules**
- R107 [PREFER] Keep the traditional alternations in sacred music: soloist vs choir (or small vs large choir) and monophony vs polyphony (unaccompanied melody followed by a polyphonic passage, or reverse). Organ is preferred over orchestra as accompaniment. §252
- R108 [ALLOWED] Liturgical text may be set with or without its chant melody; group, separate or split versicles into independent-sounding numbers (like suite movements). Magnificat, Te Deum, Stabat Mater have no fixed number of numbers. Title = first word(s) of the text. §253
- R109 [MUST] Parts sung by the officiant use the chant **in its original form, unaccompanied, unmodified**; usually followed by free polyphony by the musicians. §254a
- R110 [ALLOWED] Chant may be rhythmicised, shortened or extended as a theme (for its own or another text), or used as cantus firmus. §254b–c
- R111 [ALLOWED] Cantus firmus treatments: stays in one voice; passes from voice to voice (e.g. soprano → tenor); fragmented as thematic material. §255
- R112 [PREFER] Church style must be sober and elevated; avoid operatic effects and secular/licentious borrowed tunes (Palestrina reform, Missa Papae Marcelli 1565; Pius X Motu proprio 1903). §257

**Examples**
- Frescobaldi organ Kyrie: Gregorian Kyrie as c.f. in long notes in the soprano, then the tenor, with running 8th counterpoint.
- Willaert Magnificat (6th tone): psalm tone as c.f. in whole notes (soprano in one section, tenor in another) under/over homophonic-imitative voices.
- Palestrina Missa Ecce sacerdos magnus, Kyrie: soprano carries the Gregorian antiphon in whole notes throughout the Mass; other voices imitate the Kyrie motive in faster values.

**Agent applicability**
- Cantus-firmus texture: one track holds a chant melody in whole notes; 2–3 other voices imitate a short motif in half/quarter/8th notes against it.

**Tags:** #genre/sacred #technique/cantus-firmus #texture/alternatim

## X (cont.) Chorale and Motet (§258–263) (scan p.137–139, printed p.258–262)

**Concepts**
- Chorale (coral): Lutheran congregational hymn; austere, rhymed, long values, divided into versicles each ending with a **fermata**.
- Motet (motete): medium-length vocal piece on a (generally) religious text, outside the Mass Ordinary; today any independent piece on a religious text.

**Rules**
- R113 [MUST] Chorale melody: long values (often written in doubled values — halves for quarters — especially in figured chorales), phrase = text line, **fermata at the end of each line**. §258
- R114 [ALLOWED] Chorale treatments: (a) simple harmonisation, note-against-note or nearly, melody as top voice of usually **4 parts**; (b) melody as cantus firmus of complex polyphony (coral figurado); (c) extensive organ piece where the chorale's lines alternate without break with free fragments that paraphrase it, **beginning and ending with a free fragment**, melody ornamented and sometimes amplified (coral variado; Bach's great organ chorales); (d) chorale as fugue subject within a work based on the whole chorale. §259
- R115 [MUST] Classic polyphonic motet: each text phrase gets its own musical idea, **exposed successively by the voices** (imitative entries) — the origin of fugue exposition; entries may be accompanied by a secondary line (origin of the countersubject). §263
- R116 [MUST] Motet structure follows the text's expressive development; **no musical re-exposition unless the text repeats concepts**. §263
- R117 [PREFER] Use the chant melody of each successive versicle as the subject of each successive section (Palestrina, Lauda Sion). §263 example footnote
- R118 [ALLOWED] Grand motet (Lully): soloists, choir, orchestra, several numbers like a cantata; petit motet: one movement, one voice. §262

**Agent applicability**
- 4-part chorale: SATB homophony, one chord per melody note, fermata + cadence at each line end, melody in soprano.
- Motet: chain of imitative points, one per text line, overlapping cadences; no recapitulation.

## X (cont.) The Mass and liturgical chant types (§264–273) (scan p.139–142, printed p.262–268)

**Concepts**
- Psalm (salmo): all versicles sung to the same melody. Responsory (responsorio): soloist sings versicles, choir answers with a refrain. Antiphon (antífona): two choirs alternate versicles on the same melody and join for the refrain (chant usually begins with the refrain).
- Antiphon-type chants: Introit, Offertory, Communion. Responsory-type: Gradual, Alleluia, Tract (very melismatic). Tract replaces Alleluia from Septuagesima to Easter and in Requiems.
- Hymn (himno): verse text not taken literally from scripture; popular-style, syllabic, almost no melismas.
- Sequence (secuencia/prosa): text added syllabically to Alleluia melismas; sung after the Gradual; primitive performance as echo between two choirs (choir 1 strophe, choir 2 same melody, …, both join at the end). Post-Trent only five: Victimae paschali, Veni Sancte Spiritus, Lauda Sion, Stabat Mater, Dies irae.
- Proper (Propio) vs Ordinary (Ordinario). Musical Mass = the Ordinary.

**Rules**
- R119 [MUST] Musical Mass movements, in fixed order: **Kyrie – Gloria – Credo – Sanctus (with Benedictus) – Agnus Dei**. §269–272
- R120 [MUST] Full Gregorian-rite order (for context): Introit, Kyrie, Gloria, Gradual, Alleluia or Tract, Sequence (some feasts), Credo, Offertory, Sanctus-Benedictus, Agnus Dei, Communion. §269 footnote
- R121 [MUST] Kyrie text is threefold: Kyrie eleison ×3, Christe eleison ×3, Kyrie eleison ×3 → natural ternary A–B–A′ (first Kyrie, Christe, second Kyrie). §270
- R122 [PREFER] Gloria and Credo have the longest texts: be sparing with word repetition and melismas there; be lavish in the following (shorter) movements. §270
- R123 [MUST] The officiant's intonations (e.g. "Gloria in excelsis Deo") are Gregorian and excluded from the composition; the choir enters with original music after them. §270
- R124 [ALLOWED] Link Kyrie and Agnus Dei by shared themes, or even repeat the Kyrie music in full. §270
- R125 [PREFER] Sanctus and Benedictus flank the elevation and are treated as one unit. §270
- R126 [MUST] Mass form is otherwise absolutely free; each movement's character derives from its text and liturgical moment. §271
- R127 [MUST] Variants: Missa brevis (Protestant) = **Kyrie + Gloria only**; Requiem **omits Gloria and Credo**. §272
- R128 [ALLOWED] Other sacred titles (Miserere, Psalm n, Magnificat, Te Deum, Stabat Mater): single piece (like a motet) or a continuous series of numbers (like a cantata). Magnificat often alternates its Gregorian melody with organ versets (alternatim). §273
- R129 [MUST] Instrumental pieces for services (mostly organ) must fit the ceremony in style and **duration**; prayers (plegarias) must match the elevation of their text. §273

**Agent applicability**
- Mass plan: 5 movements; Kyrie ternary (Kyrie–Christe–Kyrie, Christe in contrasting key/texture); Gloria/Credo mostly syllabic, sectional by text; Sanctus–Benedictus paired (Hosanna may repeat); Agnus Dei can recall Kyrie material.
- Antiphonal effect: two choir tracks alternating phrases, uniting (tutti, octave doubling) on the refrain.

## Appendix: notes A–G (scan p.142–145, printed p.269–275)

**Rules**
- R130 [ALLOWED] Fugue answer (respuesta) rules are not absolute; even Bach answers near-identical subjects sometimes tonally, sometimes really (WTC examples). Dupré's rule: if the subject head moves indirectly from dominant to tonic and the last intermediate note repeats the dominant, treat all intermediate notes as in the dominant key. Old rule: answer degree III with VI and vice versa; basic rule: dominant ↔ tonic. A non-immediate repetition of the dominant is ignored by strict fuguists; moderns lean toward a real answer there. App. C–E
- R131 [MUST] Baroque notation (Bach's time): no duplets in triplet context — dotted or paired 8ths written against triplets are meant to **align with the triplet**; a single dot has no fixed value and may mean a double dot (WTC I/5, b.3). Render accordingly in MIDI. App. F
- R132 [ALLOWED] Large symphonic scherzo (Beethoven 9, App. G): 3/4 (trio in alla breve) with explicit hypermeter changes, "ritmo di quattro battute" vs "ritmo di tre battute" (bars grouped by 4 → 12/4, or by 3 → 9/4). The scherzo section is itself a sonata form: intro 8 bars (cell of A) – A 69 bars as fugal exposition – bridge 15 (cell of A) – B **in C** (d-minor movement) + complements, last codal, 58 – episode/development 113 bars (modulating, on A) – re-expo: A 25 bars (no fugal entries), bridge enlarged to 35, B in **D** + complements 70. Trio (D major): principal theme 8 – complementary theme as episode 16 – re-expo of principal theme with complement on the dominant and its repeat 53 – coda 40. Section III = exact re-expo of I + brief coda.

**Agent applicability**
- When rendering baroque scores, quantise dotted 8th–16th against triplets to the triplet grid.
- For a big scherzo, alternate 4-bar and 3-bar hypermeasures to create metric surprise.
