## 3.2 Orchestral analysis (análisis de orquestación) (scan p.5)

**Concepts**
- Aim (after Piston): find how the orchestra represents the musical thought, i.e. which instrument
  combinations give balance, variety, clarity, darkness, brilliance, expressiveness.
- Analyse both globally and fragment by fragment; texture is what links the two.
- Piston's seven texture types: orchestral unison (unísono orquestal); accompanied melody (melodía
  acompañada); secondary melody (melodía secundaria); part writing (escritura en partes); contrapuntal
  texture (textura contrapuntística); chords (textura en acordes); complex texture (textura compleja).

**Agent applicability**
- Before orchestrating a section, classify it as one of the 7 types; each type has its own recipe below.

**Tags:** #texture/types #orchestration/analysis

## 3.3 Orchestral unison vs tutti (scan p.5–8)

**Concepts**
- Orchestral unison (unísono orquestal): the *same material* played by several different instruments. The
  goal is a **blend of colours**, not a powerful statement of the melodic line.
- Orchestral tutti (tutti orquestal): **all** instruments play, normally each in its **best register**, with
  **different musical material**, to maximise the ensemble sound.

**Rules**
- R1 [PREFER] In a tutti, spread the orchestra over about **four octaves** (wide spacing) with each
  instrument in its best octave. *Example:* Beethoven 9/I.
- R2 [ALLOWED] In a tutti the high flute can act as an upper harmonic (doubling the top an octave up).
- R3 [PREFER] In a tutti, brass need not follow the melody; give them a **fragmented harmonic-support
  pattern** (sustained/rhythmic chord tones) instead.
- R4 [ALLOWED] Woodwinds may switch which line they double for register reasons (changes forced by range).

**Examples**
- Beethoven 9/I (Fig. 1): tutti ff/sf; woodwinds and strings carry the line in octaves, horns (in D and Bb),
  trumpets and timpani play detached harmonic punctuation.
- Debussy, *Le Martyre de Saint Sébastien* (Fig. 2): not strictly a unison but a texture made of a single
  element.

**Agent applicability**
- "Unison" request: double one line across timbres (e.g., fl+ob+vln at unison/octave) and keep dynamics
  moderate; "tutti" request: separate roles (melody, bass, harmony in brass) across ~4 octaves.

**Tags:** #texture/unison #texture/tutti #orchestration/brass-support

## 3.4 Accompanied melody (melodía acompañada) (scan p.8–10)

**Concepts**
- Simple texture: one melody + accompaniment.

**Rules**
- R5 [ALLOWED] Give the melody to a single wind instrument (or unison pair) with a very subtle string
  accompaniment.
- R6 [ALLOWED] The melody may migrate between instruments or be fragmented into motives across
  instruments; the melody/accompaniment split is then less obvious.

**Examples**
- Tchaikovsky 5/I (Fig. 3), Andante, ♩=80: melody in **2 clarinets in A a2, chalumeau (low) register, p**;
  accompaniment = violas, cellos, basses *pesante e tenuto sempre*, p, short chordal punctuation; violins
  join later with *tenuto* chord notes at mf. All other winds/brass rest.
- Brahms 2/I (Fig. 4): melody passes from **horns + bassoons** to **flutes + clarinets + bassoons**.

**Agent applicability**
- Dark/lyrical opening recipe: low clarinet(s) melody at p over low strings (va/vc/cb) short sustained
  chords at p; keep violins out until a later phrase.
- When handing a melody to another group, keep one instrument common to both groups (Brahms keeps bassoons)
  for continuity (inferred from Fig. 4).

**Tags:** #texture/accompanied-melody #winds/clarinet #orchestration/melody-handoff

## 3.5 Secondary melody (melodía secundaria) (scan p.11)

**Concepts**
- Three elements: main melody + secondary melody (countermelody) + accompaniment. The weight of the
  secondary melody varies; the main/secondary distinction may be blurred.

**Examples**
- Beethoven 7/II Allegretto (Fig. 5): a long orchestral crescendo keeps main melody, secondary melody and
  accompaniment throughout, adding instruments.

**Agent applicability**
- Countermelody track: different register and rhythm from the main melody; build crescendos by adding
  doublings to all three layers rather than only to the melody (inferred from the Beethoven example).

**Tags:** #texture/secondary-melody #texture/countermelody #dynamics/crescendo

## 3.6 Part writing (escritura en partes) (scan p.12)

**Concepts**
- Essentially harmonic writing in 3–4 voices, possibly chorale-like.

**Examples**
- Franck, Symphony in D minor/II, bar 230, *Poco più lento* (Fig. 6), four parts:
  A (top) = fl1, ob1, cl1, bsn1, "tr1"; B = fl2, English horn, cl2, ob2; C = bsn2, "tr2", "tr3";
  D (bass) = bass clarinet. ("tr" is likely horns (trompas) [?]: the abbreviation is ambiguous.)
  Each voice is doubled by several mixed timbres; the bass voice is a single bass clarinet.

**Agent applicability**
- For chorale-like wind passages: assign each SATB voice to a mixed-timbre group (e.g., S = fl+ob+cl,
  A = fl2+E.H.+cl2, T = bsn+horns, B = bass cl) rather than one instrument per voice.

**Tags:** #texture/part-writing #texture/chorale #winds/blend

## 3.7 Contrapuntal texture (textura contrapuntística) (scan p.13–14)

**Concepts**
- Superimposed melodic elements, which breaks the melody + accompaniment scheme. Lines must be
  independent; may be fugal or a combination of melodies with different thematic significance.

**Rules**
- R7 [MUST] Keep contrapuntal lines independent (rhythmically and harmonically).
- R8 [ALLOWED] Octave-double each line widely; with only two lines this gives a big sound from few elements.
- R9 [ALLOWED] Break routine pairings (e.g., bassoons need not double cellos/basses) when it helps separate lines.

**Examples**
- Mozart 40/III (Fig. 7): two rhythmically different lines with different dissonances.
  Line A = flute, oboes, cellos, basses; line B = violins I & II, bassoons. Oboes start in unison then
  split into octaves to reinforce. Bassoons go with the violins, not with vc/cb.

**Agent applicability**
- Two-voice counterpoint: give each line a mixed wind+string group spanning several octaves; assign groups
  so timbre distinguishes the lines (e.g., line A: fl/ob/vc/cb; line B: vln/bsn).

**Tags:** #texture/counterpoint #orchestration/doubling

## 3.8 Chordal texture (textura en acordes) (scan p.14–17)

**Rules**
- R10 [MUST] When orchestrating a chord, decide: (1) the desired colour, (2) the vertical order of the
  instruments, (3) the balance of the result.
- R11 [ALLOWED] A chord may be scored for woodwinds, brass, strings or a mix.
- R12 [MUST] Take special care with extreme registers (very high and very low notes) and with special
  timbres (double reeds, mutes).

**Concepts: four ways to combine two instrument families/pairs in a chord (Piston p.422)**
- Concatenation/juxtaposition (concatenación): the lowest note of the upper family = the highest note of
  the lower family (they touch/share a pitch).
- Superposition (superposición): one family entirely above the other, no contact or overlap.
- Interlocking (engranaje): the lower note of the upper pair is below the upper note of the lower pair
  (voices alternate: A1 > B1 > A2 > B2).
- Enclosure/inclusion (inclusión): both notes of one pair lie inside the interval of the other pair.

**Examples**
- Schumann 1/I, bars 3–4 (Bb major, I–IV–V4/3–I, f), reduction from Adler (Fig. 8–9):
  woodwinds chord 1 = superposition (2 Fl over 2 Ob over 2 Cl); chord 2 = concatenation between flutes and
  oboes. Strings: Vln I and Vln II **interlocked** to obtain the greatest blend within the family; violas,
  cellos and basses below, basses doubling the bass at the lower octave. Brass: 2 tpt + 2 hn, 2 bsn
  supporting.

**Agent applicability**
- For maximum blend inside one family use interlocking (alternate the players of two sections/pairs);
  for distinct family colours use superposition; concatenation/inclusion are intermediate options.
- Encode voicing checks: for families X (upper) and Y (lower), compute min(X) vs max(Y): equal →
  concatenation, min(X) > max(Y) → superposition, min(X) < max(Y) with alternation → interlocking,
  X inside Y's span → inclusion.

**Tags:** #texture/chords #orchestration/voicing #orchestration/blend

## 3.9 Complex texture (textura compleja) (scan p.17–19)

**Concepts**
- Either several simple textures superimposed, or an amalgam of many elements none of which is clearly
  primary ("mosaic form", forma de mosaico).
- Micropolyphony (micropolifonía) (Ligeti, from the 1950s): all voices in a dense canon so individual
  voices are lost; the canon governs vertical and horizontal relations alike.

**Examples**
- Stravinsky, *Firebird*, "Magic Carillon" (Fig. 10): harp arpeggios + clarinet and bassoon tremolos +
  descending string scales, all superimposed.
- Ligeti, *Lontano* (1967) (Fig. 11): huge divisi of all instruments; equal voices entering in canon
  separated by a small time offset (period).

**Agent applicability**
- Mosaic texture: build from several independent layers (arpeggio layer, tremolo layer, scale layer), each
  in a distinct register/timbre.
- Micropolyphonic cloud: duplicate one line across many divisi tracks with small onset offsets (canon at
  short delay) so it fuses into a texture.

**Tags:** #texture/complex #texture/micropolyphony #texture/layering
