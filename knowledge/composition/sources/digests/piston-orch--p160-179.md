# Walter Piston, Orchestration — scans s080L–s089R (printed p.160–179)

Conventions: C4 = MIDI 60. "Written" pitches are marked; MIDI numbers are concert (sounding) pitch unless
marked written. Conversions: English horn −7, oboe d'amore −3, heckelphone −12, Bb clarinet −2,
A clarinet −3, bass clarinet in Bb −14 (French notation) or −2 (German notation).

## Ch.8 The Oboe — The English horn, end of section (printed p.160–161)

**Concepts**
- English horn (corno inglés; Fr. cor anglais; Ger. Englischhorn): alto oboe in F, sounds a perfect fifth
  (7) below written.
- Mixed timbre (timbre mixto): two different instruments in unison that fuse into one new colour.

**Rules**
- R1 [PREFER] Use the English horn as a colour resource in combination with other winds and with strings,
  not only as a soloist.
- R2 [PREFER] English horn + solo cello in unison blend as one voice with neither dominating; support with
  widely spaced, pianissimo sustained string harmonies (Debussy, La Mer).
- R3 [AVOID] Writing above written E6 (sounding A5 = 81) in orchestral EH parts unless the note is doubled
  by other instruments (octave inferred from "above E" + example). *Why:* like every auxiliary instrument
  built to extend a family downward, its upper tones are its least effective part.
- R4 [ALLOWED] Written F6 (sounding Bb5 = 82) is produced by good players (Roussel example). Treat as the
  practical ceiling.
- R5 [ALLOWED] The EH equals the oboe in agility; it can take part in all active woodwind figuration.

**Examples**
- Ex.187 Berlioz, *Roman Carnival* overture: long lyrical EH solo, Andante sostenuto, mf espressivo, in
  the middle staff register; the canonical EH cantilena.
- Ex.188 R. Strauss, *Ein Heldenleben*: EH pp, tender and expressive, chromatic line with triplets, long
  legato phrase.
- Ex.189 Debussy, *La Mer*: EH in unison with solo cello, p très expressif et soutenu, over pp widely
  spaced string chords (mixed-timbre model).
- Ex.190 Roussel, *Bacchus et Ariane* Suite 2: EH f in its top register (to written F6), lively
  staccato/accented figure at quarter = 132; shows the high limit and agility.

**Agent applicability**
- EH MIDI track: keep the melodic core roughly E3–E5 (52–76) concert; cap at A5 (81), hard max Bb5 (82),
  and double anything above 81 (inferred from R3/R4).
- For a "fused" solo colour, put EH and solo cello on the same pitches, same phrasing, same dynamic
  curve; string pad pp with wide spacing.

## Ch.8 — The Oboe d'amore (printed p.161–162)

**Concepts**
- Oboe d'amore (oboe de amor; Fr. hautbois d'amour; Ger. Liebesoboe): between oboe and EH in size; in A,
  sounds a minor third (3) below written. Bulb bell like the EH; no low Bb key (lowest written B3, sounding
  G#3 = 56).
- Tone gentler than oboe and EH, subtly different colour.

**Rules**
- R6 [era: Baroque] Common instrument of the Baroque, much used by J. S. Bach; use it to evoke Bach-era
  obbligato colour.
- R7 [era: 1955] In modern orchestration it is known chiefly from Strauss's *Symphonia Domestica*
  (written range up to high F, = written F6, sounding D6 = 86, octave inferred), Ravel's *Boléro* and
  Debussy's *Gigues*. A rare, special-colour instrument.
- R8 [NEVER] Write below written B3 (sounding G#3 = 56).

**Examples**
- Ex.191 Debussy, *Gigues* (Images): oboe d'amore solo, at first unaccompanied, p doux et mélancolique,
  6/8, tenuto notes with grace-note ornaments, in the upper staff register.

**Agent applicability**
- If an oboe d'amore patch is unavailable, an oboe played softly in its low-middle register (about
  G#3–A5 concert) is the closest stand-in (inferred).

## Ch.8 — The Heckelphone (printed p.162)

**Concepts**
- Heckelphone (heckelfón; Ger. Heckelphon): invented by Wilhelm Heckel, 1904; a baritone oboe sounding an
  octave (12) below the oboe, with one extra semitone down to A: lowest written A3, sounding A2 (45).
  Keys placed high, German-oboe fingering, bulb bell with a side vent.
- Transposing: sounds an octave below written.

**Rules**
- R9 [PREFER] Use it as the bass of the oboe family; rich, full, reedy tone in its lower octave.
- R10 [AVOID] Its upper tones; it can reach at least written E above the staff (written E6, sounding
  E5 = 76, octave inferred) but is hardly used there.
- R11 [era: 1955] Very rare (Strauss, *Elektra*, *Salome*); few orchestras can supply one. Do not count on
  it in realistic orchestrations.

**Agent applicability**
- For a double-reed bass line below the EH, prefer bassoon; reserve heckelphone colour for Straussian
  late-Romantic writing, roughly A2–C5 concert (45–72) (inferred).

## Ch.9 The Clarinet — construction and tone production (printed p.163–164)

**Concepts**
- Clarinet (clarinete; Fr. clarinette; It. clarinetto; Ger. Klarinette): cylindrical bore, bell flaring
  slightly more than the oboe's; grenadilla wood, ebonite or metal; five sections (mouthpiece, barrel,
  upper joint, lower joint, bell). "Beak" mouthpiece; single cane reed held by a metal ligature.
- The reed rests on the lower lip; tone and volume depend on how much reed is in the mouth and lip
  pressure; the tongue touches the reed tip for each articulation.

## Ch.9 — The clarinet family (printed p.164)

**Concepts** (transpositions = sounding relative to written; MIDI offset in brackets)
- Sopranino in Ab: m6 above [+8]. Sopranino in Eb: m3 above [+3]. Sopranino in D: M2 above [+2].
- Soprano in C: as written [0]. Soprano in Bb: M2 below [−2]. Soprano in A: m3 below [−3].
- Alto clarinet in Eb: M6 below [−9]. Basset horn in F: P5 below [−7].
- Bass clarinet in Bb: M9 below [−14]. Bass clarinet in A: octave + m3 below [−15].
- Contrabass clarinet in Bb: two octaves + M2 below [−26].
- Alto and bass instruments have upturned bells and bent metal mouthpipes.

**Rules**
- R12 [era: 1955] Only four are regular orchestral members: small Eb clarinet, the pair in Bb and A, and
  bass clarinet in Bb.
- R13 [era: 1955] Ab piccolo clarinet is rare even in bands; D clarinet parts are usually played on Eb.
- R14 [era: 18th–19th c.] Many classical/romantic scores call for C clarinets; today [1955] these are
  played on Bb or A. The C clarinet is obsolete. For period colour, C-clarinet parts may still be written.
- R15 [era: 1955] Alto clarinet sometimes plays basset-horn parts; used in military and dance bands.
  Bass clarinet in A is virtually never seen; contrabass clarinet not yet adopted by composers.

## Ch.9 — Transposition and fingering system (printed p.165–167)

**Concepts**
- All clarinets share one fingering system; the player reads as if for C clarinet and the instrument's
  size does the transposing. Piston names registers and notes in **written** pitch from here on.
- Standard clarinet = Bb (the counterpart of flute and oboe); A clarinet somewhat less used.
- Clarinet basic six-hole scale starts on written G3 (vs. D for flute/oboe): written G3–F#4 (Fig.51a).
  The cylindrical tube overblows only odd harmonics, so the next series sits a twelfth (19 semitones)
  higher, written D5–C#6 (Fig.51b), leaving a gap.
- Gap fillers: left-thumb hole (written G4), keys for G#4 and A4 (left first finger), register key also
  used for Bb4; extension down to written E3 so the first overblown note is B4. Right thumb supports the
  instrument.
- Boehm-system clarinet: up to 7 ring keys and 17–21 other keys; many alternate fingerings, especially
  duplicating the little-finger keys.
- Fig.52 (fingering chart, Boehm): thumb hole closed except for throat tones G4, G#4, A4, Bb4 and one F#
  fingering; register (speaker) key held for every note from written B4 upward.
- Side keys 9 and 10 (right hand first finger) give the trills Bb4–C5 and Bb4–Cb5 (written).
- Fingerings above written F#6 [?] are not standardized (except A6); players find their own.

## Ch.9 — Registers (printed p.167, Fig.53; all written pitch)

**Concepts**
- Chalumeau (chalumeau / registro grave): written E3–F#4 (52–66). Bb clarinet sounds D3–E4 (50–64);
  A clarinet C#3–Eb4 (49–63).
- Throat tones (notas de garganta): written G4, G#4, A4, Bb4 (67–70); fundamentals on short tube.
- Clarino (registro clarín): written B4–C6 (71–84), 3rd harmonic of E3–F4 fingerings.
- High (agudo): written C#6 up to about G6 (85–91), from 5th harmonics.
- Extreme high (sobreagudo): written above G6 to C7 (92–96), 7th/9th harmonics (Fig.53 boundaries partly
  [?]).

## Ch.9 — The chalumeau (printed p.167–169)

**Rules**
- R16 [PREFER] Use the chalumeau for dark, menacing, dramatic or atmospheric colour; it is unlike any
  other orchestral sound and unlike the clarinet's other registers.
- R17 [PREFER] Repeated-note accompaniment figures and accented rhythmic patterns in the chalumeau; they
  are of excellent effect.
- R18 [ALLOWED] Exploit the register contrast: first clarinet high, second clarinet in the chalumeau, so
  the pair sounds like two different instruments.

**Examples**
- Ex.192 Respighi, *Fontane di Roma*: Bb clarinet melody p espressivo entirely in the chalumeau, ending on
  the lowest notes; atmospheric.
- Ex.193 Stravinsky, *Le Baiser de la Fée* (Divertimento): Cl I–II low repeated staccato sixteenth dyads
  p, Cl III accented low sustained line; chalumeau accompaniment texture.
- Ex.194 Mozart, Symphony in Eb K.543 (Trio): Cl I sings in the clarino while Cl II plays continuous
  eighth-note arpeggios in the chalumeau, flute answering, light strings, horns/bassoons punctuating;
  two-register contrast inside one section.

## Ch.9 — Throat tones (printed p.169)

**Rules**
- R19 [ALLOWED] Use throat tones (written G4–Bb4) freely. They are paler and less vibrant than other
  clarinet notes, a distinct resource rather than inferior notes. The awkwardness of G#4/A4 fingering is
  greatly overstated.
- R20 [PREFER] For an important, exposed sustained melody note whose written pitch falls on a throat tone,
  consider the other clarinet so the note becomes a full clarino tone (see R32–R33).

**Examples**
- Ex.195 Debussy, *Prélude à l'après-midi d'un faune*: Cl I in A, flowing p–f line that sits on and around
  the throat tones; shows their expressive use.

## Ch.9 — The break (printed p.170)

**Concepts**
- Break (paso / quiebre del registro): strictly the move between written Bb4 (70, whole tube open) and
  B4 (71, whole tube closed plus register key), either direction; not the whole throat zone.

**Rules**
- R21 [ALLOWED] Crossing the break smoothly is routine for good players; slurred lines across it are fine.

**Examples**
- Ex.196 Sibelius, Symphony no.3: Cl I in A, pp legato line weaving back and forth across the break,
  rising to ppp; shows smooth crossing.

## Ch.9 — Clarino, high and extreme notes (printed p.170–171)

**Rules**
- R22 [PREFER] Clarino (written B4–C6) for bright, incisive, warm, expressive melody.
- R23 [AVOID] Forte above written C6 unless shrillness is wanted: the high register turns shrill and
  piercing in f, somewhat flute-like when soft.
- R24 [MUST] Treat written G6 as the safe top note for orchestral clarinet parts: Bb clarinet sounding
  F6 (89), A clarinet sounding E6 (88).
- R25 [AVOID] Written G#6–C7 (Bb: sounding F#6–Bb6, 90–94). Little sonorous value; G#6/A6 appear
  occasionally (modern French scores), usually in tutti or doubled. Passages like Casella's are very
  exceptional.
- R26 [ALLOWED] A good clarinettist keeps the registers homogeneous, so widely traversing melodic lines
  across all registers are possible.

**Examples**
- Ex.197 Beethoven, Symphony no.7 Allegretto: Cl I–II in A, dolce, melody then thirds/sixths in the
  clarino.
- Ex.198 Stravinsky, *Symphony in Three Movements*: Cl I–II in Bb, mf, angular lines spanning registers,
  later in sixteenth-note parallel motion; register homogeneity.
- Ex.199 Casella, *Pupazzetti*: Cl I–II in A, ff–fff, repeated notes and runs up to the extreme top;
  marked as exceptional.

## Ch.9 — Dynamics (printed p.171–172)

**Rules**
- R27 [ALLOWED] The clarinet has the widest dynamic range of the woodwinds throughout its compass; most
  remarkable in pianissimo, where it can fade to near inaudibility. Markings: subtone, echo tone.
- R28 [PREFER] Use clarinet for niente entries/exits and pppp echo effects.

**Examples**
- Ex.200 Berlioz, *Symphonie fantastique* (Scène aux champs): Cl I in Bb marked Echo, pppp, a short
  dotted phrase in the clarino.

**Agent applicability**
- Clarinet is the woodwind whose CC11/CC1 can go lowest without losing pitch focus; fade to ~10–20
  (library dependent) for niente (inferred).

## Ch.9 — Use of the A clarinet (printed p.172–173)

**Concepts**
- The A clarinet is not an auxiliary; it shares the "standard clarinet" role with Bb (unequally). Players
  bring both and may switch within a movement (moving the mouthpiece to avoid a dry reed).
- Traditional three reasons for A: (a) easier fingering in sharp keys (B major written D major on A vs
  C#/Db major on Bb); (b) slightly warmer, less brilliant tone; (c) low written E3 gives sounding C#3 (49).

**Rules**
- R29 [era: 1955] Reason (a) is misleading: key signature does not predict difficulty in 20th-c.
  chromatic music, and mechanism and technique have advanced.
- R30 [era: 1955] Reason (b): the tone difference between A and Bb is not striking, often inaudible, though
  the longer tube helps the player get a desired tone.
- R31 [MUST] For sounding C#3 (49) write for A clarinet; that is the only sure way. Bb clarinets with a low
  Eb key exist but are not universal [era: 1955].
- R32 [era: 1955] Many clarinettists play A parts on Bb, transposing down a semitone; sometimes the reverse,
  e.g. sounding Ab4 (68): written throat Bb4 on Bb clarinet vs full clarino B4 on A clarinet.
- R33 [PREFER] Choose the clarinet so that an important exposed melody note avoids the throat tones (from
  R32 example) (inferred).

**Agent applicability**
- Concert range for MIDI: Bb clarinet D3–F6 (50–89), A clarinet C#3–E6 (49–88). Only an A clarinet track
  may play C#3 (49).
- Concert pitches that land on throat tones: Bb clar F4–Ab4 (65–68); A clar E4–G4 (64–67). Avoid long
  exposed melody notes there, or switch instrument.

## Ch.9 — Trills and tremolos (printed p.173)

**Rules**
- R34 [ALLOWED] All major and minor trills are good on the Boehm clarinet.
- R35 [PREFER] Tremolos (fingered) are very effective, especially in the chalumeau.
- R36 [NEVER] Tremolo intervals wider than M6 (9) in the chalumeau; wider than P4 (5) above the break;
  wider than M3 (4) above the staff (written above F5) — the safer limit there.

**Examples**
- Ex.201 Kodály, *Psalmus Hungaricus*: Cl I–II in A, ff measured tremolos (thirty-seconds) of thirds and
  fourths in the clarino/high register, in parallel thirds.

## Ch.9 — Tonguing and attack (printed p.173–174)

**Rules**
- R37 [PREFER] Clarinet staccato is less pointed than oboe staccato but can be drier and sharper than
  flute staccato.
- R38 [PREFER] Relieve fast single-tongued passages by slurring two or three notes at suitable places.
- R39 [ALLOWED] Double and triple tonguing only as an emergency expedient.
- R40 [ALLOWED] Clarinet has the most varied attacks of the reeds: from explosive sforzando to a soft
  start like louré bowing.

**Examples**
- Ex.202 Mendelssohn, *Italian* Symphony (finale, Presto): Cl I–II in A imitate the flutes' light
  triple-tongued triplets, p leggiero, in thirds.
- Ex.203 Debussy, *Première Rhapsodie*: solo Bb clarinet; dreamy rising run p, en serrant, then
  scherzando staccato, f accents, and a chain of trills fading più p; range of attack and character.

**Agent applicability**
- Clarinet staccato note length ~50–60% of the grid value rather than oboe-short (~35–45%) (inferred).
- Fast repeated sixteenths: group slurs of 2–3 notes (legato overlap) between tongued notes.

## Ch.9 — Virtuosity (printed p.174–175)

**Rules**
- R41 [ALLOWED] Agility second only to the flute: rapid scales, arpeggios and figuration of all kinds.
- R42 [PREFER] Wide legato leaps between registers upward rather than downward; upward is easier.

**Examples**
- Ex.204 Schoenberg, *Pierrot lunaire*: Cl in A from low written E3 sfpp, rising through registers to a
  high f note; wide upward legato traversal.
- Ex.205 Ravel, *Rapsodie espagnole*: cadenza ad lib. for two Bb clarinets in flowing legato
  thirty-seconds, mostly in thirds/sixths, ending Très ralenti; clarinet legato flexibility.

## Ch.9 — Orchestral uses (printed p.176–177)

**Rules**
- R43 [PREFER] Use clarinets as soloists, as doublers of other melodic voices, and as a harmonic/colour
  section. Their unobtrusive tone doubles and combines with almost every instrument.
- R44 [PREFER] Clarinet accompaniment figures (trills, quick arpeggio flourishes, repeated notes) add life
  and vibration to the background texture.
- R45 [ALLOWED] Chalumeau clarinets with harp and solo violin make an evocative atmosphere (Berg).
- R46 [ALLOWED] [era: 20th c.] Some modern composers notate clarinets at sounding pitch in the score, with
  transposed parts printed for players.

**Examples**
- Ex.206 Stravinsky, *Petrushka* (q = 138): three Bb clarinets, f, passing trills and quintuplet/sextuplet
  flourishes among themselves in overlapping relay; a shimmering background figure (study in full score).
- Ex.207 Berg, Violin Concerto (opening, Andante): two clarinets (I for Bb, II for A, score in C) with
  arch-shaped arpeggios in the low register pp, bass clarinet sustained notes p, harp arpeggios, solo
  violin answering in the same arch shape on open-string-like fifths.

## Ch.9 — The Bass Clarinet (printed p.177–179; continues past p.179)

**Concepts**
- Bass clarinet (clarinete bajo; Fr. clarinette basse; It. clarinetto basso, clarone; Ger.
  Bassklarinette): in Bb, an octave below the soprano Bb clarinet. Upturned bell, curved mouthpipe (nickel
  silver), neck cord.
- Bass clarinet in A exists on paper; parts for it are played on the Bb instrument transposed down a
  semitone. Purpose of A parts was the low sounding C#2 (37).
- Modern Bb bass clarinets add a low Eb key (right little finger): written Eb3, sounding C#2 (37), a semitone
  above the cello's open C2 (36). The instrument then has the length of the bass clarinet in A.
- Two register keys; otherwise Boehm mechanism as the soprano.

**Rules**
- R47 [MUST] Treat sounding C#2 (37) as the lowest note you can count on.
- R48 [era: 1955] Instruments to written D (sounding C2 = 36) exist; Russian scores imply written C
  (sounding Bb1 = 34), the range Rimsky-Korsakov gave; a Boston Symphony model (Rosario Mazzeo) goes to
  written Bb (sounding Ab1 = 32). Use only when you know the instrument is available; today low-C bass
  clarinets are common (modern note, not Piston).
- R49 [ALLOWED] Throat tones can be overblown at the twelfth for fuller tone around written high D–F
  (likely D5–F5, sounding C4–Eb4 = 60–63) [?]; few players do it.
- R50 [MUST] Mark which notation a bass clarinet part uses. French notation: treble clef throughout,
  sounds a M9 below written (−14), same reading as Bb clarinet. German notation: bass clef normally, treble
  only for high passages; sounds a M2 below written (−2) in either clef.
- R51 [PREFER] When a part starts in treble clef, add a footnote such as "sounding a major ninth (or major
  second) lower" to remove ambiguity. Players show no general preference between systems.

**Concepts — tone qualities (p.179)**
- Low tones rich in odd partials; resultant major triads often audible when the instrument plays alone.

**Rules**
- R52 [PREFER] Use bass clarinet as the bass of the woodwind group, with or without bassoon doubling.
- R53 [PREFER] Use its low-register pianissimo; it is much softer than the bassoons' and a valued
  resource.

**Examples**
- Fig.54: one passage shown as sound, French notation and German notation (illustrates the −14 vs −2
  offsets).
- Ex.208 Shostakovich, Symphony no.7: expressive bass clarinet solo (German notation), p espr., low
  register, very lightly accompanied by flutes with harp above; the written low C# (sounding B1 = 35)
  is beyond most instruments.

**Agent applicability**
- Bass clarinet MIDI floor 37 (C#2); allow 34–36 only if the target library/ensemble has a low-C model.
- Woodwind bass for soft chords: bass clarinet pp under clarinets/flutes, bassoons optional.
