# Adler, El estudio de la orquestación — scans 196–215 (ch.7 Woodwinds, cont.)

Pitch convention: C4 = middle C = MIDI 60; Adler's own octave numbering matches (he calls the clarinet's
lowest written note MI3 = E3 and the oboe's best register FA4–DO6). Scan number = printed page + 9; printed
pages 187–206 are continuous (scan 209 = printed 200, page number not visible). Range charts are small: where
the top note of a chart could not be read reliably it is marked `[?]`. Written vs. concert is stated each time.
The expected "requinto / clarinete bajo" sections are not in this range (the clarinet section only starts).

## 7 Flute — extended techniques, end of section (scan p.196, printed p.187)

**Examples**
- R. Strauss, *Salome*, Scene 2 at [41]: three flutes in flutter-tongue (frullato), *ff*, chromatic triplet runs
  under an accelerando — flutter tongue as a frenzied, hissing colour, not a melody.
- C. Polin, *La muerte de Procris*: a chart of seven flute multiphonics, each with its own fingering diagram —
  shows that multiphonics must be notated with fingerings.
- Additional study: Berio *Sequenza* (multiphonics, key clicks), Gilbert, Jolas *Fusain* (quarter tones),
  Musgrave *Narcissus* (flute + digital delay), Sollberger *Riding the Wind* (buzz/whistle tones).

## 7 Multiple flutes (Flautas múltiples) (scan p.196–198, printed p.187–189)

**Concepts**
- Flute section (sección de flautas): Classical orchestras varied the count; from Beethoven a pair was the norm;
  three flutes became normal for most orchestras only by the mid-19th c.; modern sections reach 4–5 players
  (e.g. two piccolos, or piccolo + alto flute).

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] [era: Classical, c.1760–1810] Use 1–2 flutes: Haydn normally two (three in *The Creation*);
  Mozart and Schubert (Symphony 5) sometimes only one.
- R2 [ALLOWED] [era: early 19th c., e.g. Rossini] Have the 2nd flute double on piccolo; or use two
  independent flutes plus piccolo.
- R3 [PREFER] Give multiple flutes one of five textures: (1) doubling at the unison or octave; (2) parallel
  intervals (3rds, 6ths, triads); (3) antiphonal exchange; (4) relaying each other in long, fast or sustained
  passages (so no player must breathe mid-line); (5) fully independent parts.

**Examples**
- Tchaikovsky, *Nutcracker*, "Dance of the Toy Flutes" (Danse des mirlitons), bars 3–6: three flutes in
  parallel close triads, *p* staccato, D major — the classic parallel-chord flute trio.
- Ravel, *Daphnis et Chloé* at [165]: piccolo sings a sustained expressive melody (*f très expressif*) while
  flute and alto flute play rapid interlocking 32nd-note figuration — independent and parallel at once.
- Additional study (2–3 flutes): Bach Cantata 106, Chabrier *España*, Debussy "Fêtes", Mendelssohn
  *Midsummer Night's Dream* Scherzo, Stravinsky *Fireworks*, *Petrushka*. Doubling with other instruments: ch.8.

**Agent applicability**
- For a flute choir in MIDI, write 3 parts in close position (e.g. 3rds/triads, top voice ≤ ~C7/96) with
  identical rhythm and staccato; for long runs, split the line between two flute tracks with overlaps at
  hand-offs (relay).

## 7 Piccolo (El flautín; ottavino, petite flûte, kleine Flöte) (scan p.198–200, printed p.189–191)

**Concepts**
- Auxiliary instruments (instrumentos auxiliares): each of the four main woodwinds has at least one; they
  extend the range and intensify colour at the extremes, and are now self-sufficient members of the section.
- Piccolo: extends the flute (and thus the whole orchestra) one octave up; same mechanism, versatility and
  agility as the flute; popular in 20th-c. scores.

**Rules**
- R4 [MUST] Range: written D4 (62) – C7 (96) [top ?]; sounds an octave higher: concert D5 (74) – C8 (108).
  Instrument in C; no model plays below written D4.
- R5 [ALLOWED] [era: military band practice, obsolete] A Db piccolo still appears in some military bands, increasingly rare.
- R6 [AVOID] Using the piccolo's lowest octave (written D4–~D5, concert D5–~D6) unless the orchestra is thin:
  it is empty, uneasy and very soft and gets covered.
- R7 [PREFER] Above D5 (Adler's "RE5"; the text does not say whether written or sounding, most likely written =
  concert D6/86 (inferred)) the piccolo dominates the orchestra.
- R8 [AVOID] Lingering in the extreme top: very penetrating, whistle-like, tiring to hear and exhausting to
  produce. Use it for peaks, not sustained lines.

**Register characteristics (written; boundaries approximate [?])**
- Low (D4–~G4): calm, hollow, uneasy. Middle-low (~G4–D5): soft, melodious. Upper (~D5–A6): brilliant, clear.
  Top (~A6–C7): shrill (estridente).

**Examples**
- Mozart, *Die Zauberflöte*, Act II, "Alles fühlt der Liebe Freuden", bars 5–9: piccolo *pp* in fast 16th
  runs — a light, quick, quiet piccolo in an opera orchestra.
- Prokofiev, *Lieutenant Kijé*, mvt 1, bars 9–17: piccolo solo *p*, staccato march tune with grace notes over
  military (snare) drum; flute 1 enters later lower — the fife-and-drum colour.
- Gluck, *Iphigénie en Tauride*, Act I, Sc. 3, bars 1–8: two piccolos in unison, Allegro, with acciaccaturas —
  barbaric/martial (Scythian) colour.
- Additional study: Debussy *Ibéria*, Kodály *Háry János*, Prokofiev *Scythian Suite*, Shostakovich 6 and 15,
  Smetana *Bartered Bride*, Stravinsky *Petrushka* (2 piccolos + 3 flutes in "Russian Dance").

**Agent applicability**
- MIDI notes for a piccolo track: 74–108; prefer 86–100 for solo lines; keep the 101–108 zone for short
  climaxes; drop the piccolo entirely in tutti passages that sit below MIDI ~84.

## 7 Alto flute (La flauta en SOL; flauto contralto, flûte en sol, Altflöte) (scan p.200–201, printed p.191–192)

**Concepts**
- First downward extension of the flute family; important from the 1890s, popular with Stravinsky and Ravel.
  Often wrongly called "bass flute". Accepted orchestral member but used in few works. Wider, longer tube.

**Rules**
- R9 [MUST] Transposing in G: sounds a P4 (5 semitones) below written. Written C4 (60) – ~C7 (96) [top ?];
  concert G3 (55) – ~G6 (91) [top ?]. Same fingering/mechanism as the flute.
- R10 [ALLOWED] All flute techniques are possible, but it needs more air (bigger instrument and bore) — give
  more rests/breath room than for flute (inferred from the air demand).
- R11 [PREFER] Write it in its low register: low notes are much richer than the flute's and project more.
- R12 [AVOID] Its upper register: ordinary, without the flute's brilliance; flute and piccolo cover that
  region equally well.

**Register characteristics (written; boundaries approximate [?])**
- Lowest (C4–~F4): deep, rich, sonorous. Middle (~F4–~C5): full, very effective. Upper: plain, lacks brilliance.

**Examples**
- Holst, *The Planets*, "Saturn", bars 53–62: alto flute solo *pp*, legato, low register — dark, hollow solo
  line.
- Stravinsky, *The Rite of Spring*, "Rondes printanières" at [56]: flutes 1–2 hold high trills (*p*, after
  *sff* attacks) while the alto flute plays the folk-like melody *p* low — alto flute as solo voice under a
  high shimmering flute layer.
- Additional study: S. Albert *In Concordiam*, Ravel *Daphnis* Suites 1–2, Sessions *The Black Maskers*,
  Varèse *Amériques* (opening alto-flute solo).

**Agent applicability**
- Alto flute track: MIDI 55–~79 is the useful zone; above ~79 hand the line to C flute.

## 7 Bass flute (La flauta baja; flauto basso, flûte basse, Bassflöte) (scan p.202, printed p.193)

**Concepts**
- Boehm attempted one; first practical model by Rudall, Carte & Co. (1930), Boehm system, headjoint bent
  180°; a light metal rest lets the seated player balance it on the right thigh.

**Rules**
- R13 [MUST] Transposing: sounds an octave below written. Written C4 (60) – ~C7 [?]; concert C3 (48) – ~C6 (84) [top ?].
- R14 [PREFER] Use the low register: very rich; tone muffled, singular, warm.
- R15 [AVOID] Writing it into a standard orchestral part without checking availability: not harder to play
  than the alto flute, but expensive and few orchestras own one. Found in solo/chamber music, some band and
  film scores.

**Examples**
- Zandonai, *Francesca da Rimini*, bars 186–190: bass flute solo *p*, slow, low register with triplets and
  hairpins; part shown written (treble) and sounding (bass clef).
- Additional study: Chihara *Willow, Willow*.

**Agent applicability**
- Bass flute: MIDI 48–~72 for solos; keep the accompaniment very light (it is soft and easily covered).

## 7 Oboe (El oboe; hautbois, Oboe/Hoboe) (scan p.202–207, printed p.193–198)

**Concepts**
- Lyric double-reed instrument with the most singular personality of the woodwinds ("prima donna" of the
  section) — due to its peculiar sound, not its place in the section.
- Oboe family (pictured): oboe, oboe d'amore, English horn, bass (baritone) oboe, heckelphone.
- Reed (caña): the most volatile part; professionals make their own; must be thin enough to vibrate easily
  but not so thin that pitch and tone become uncontrollable; always moist; affected by temperature and weather.

**Rules — breath and endurance**
- R16 [ALLOWED] Very long held notes and long phrases on one breath (the oboist releases air very slowly).
- R17 [MUST] Still give frequent rests: breath and embouchure control on the sensitive reed make rest
  periods frequent and obligatory.

**Rules — range and registers (non-transposing; concert = written)**
- R18 [MUST] Range: Bb3 (58) – A6 (93). A6 is extremely hard; many professionals cannot play it.
- R19 [PREFER] Most effective register: F4 (65) – C6 (84).
- R20 [ALLOWED] C6 (84) – F6 (89): thin, slightly penetrating, very beautiful for a delicate effect, but hard
  to control (especially for non-professionals).
- R21 [ALLOWED] [era: late 20th c.] Above F6 up to A6 only for specialist players (Babbitt *Relata II* reaches
  A6; Trojahn's *Divertissement* for oboe uses trills on G6/G#6). Not for standard orchestral parts.
- R22 [NEVER] Write *pianissimo* for the oboe in its lowest fifth (Bb3–F4, MIDI 58–65): it is dense, full and
  tends to honk, even for the best players.
- R23 [PREFER] Contrast with flute: the flute gets brighter as it rises, the oboe loses penetration as it
  rises; the flute is weak in its low octave, the oboe strong. Pair them accordingly (oboe low/mid, flute high).

**Register characteristics (approximate [?])**
- Bb3–~F4: dense, strong. ~F4–~C6: warm, prominent, reedy ("caño"), penetrating. ~C6–F6: weak but clear.
  Top (~F6–A6): constricted, ineffective.

**Representative solo passages**
- Bach, *Brandenburg Concerto No. 2*, mvt 2, bars 9–23: lyrical oboe line with trills in a contrapuntal trio —
  Baroque obbligato oboe.
- Tchaikovsky, Symphony 4, mvt 2 opening: oboe solo *p* "in modo di canzone", Bb minor, middle register,
  long singing phrase with hairpins — the archetypal melancholy oboe tune.
- Shostakovich, Symphony 1, mvt 3 at [4]: oboe solo *f*, Lento, chromatic and wide-ranging — intense,
  expressive solo.
- Additional study: Bartók *Concerto for Orchestra* IV, Beethoven 3 and 6, Bizet Symphony in C, Rossini
  *La scala di seta*, Schubert 8 and 9, Schumann 2.

**Rules — articulation and tonguing (articulación y picado de lengua)**
- R24 [ALLOWED] Very fast single-tongued staccato (thin, flexible reed). Double/triple tonguing is rarely needed.
- R25 [AVOID] Very fast repeated notes: not idiomatic (Mendelssohn, ex. 6-10, gives the fast repeated figure
  to all winds except the oboes).
- R26 [ALLOWED] Scales and fairly wide leaps: a good player is very agile despite R25.

**Examples**
- Mozart, *Sinfonia concertante* K.297b, mvt 3, bars 192–200: oboe *p* staccato 16ths with arpeggiated
  leaps, Eb major — agile detached writing.
- Ex. 7-37 (Adler's own): "not too fast" passage with wide leaps across the whole range in 7- and 5-note
  groups — leaps are fine if tempo is moderate.

**Rules — trills and tremolos (trinos y trémolos)**
- R27 [ALLOWED] On modern instruments trills/tremolos are possible almost everywhere except Bb3–B3 (58–59).
- R28 [AVOID] Semitone trill C4–C#4 (60–61).
- R29 [AVOID] Wide-interval tremolos on notes above the staff (> ~F5/77) and tremolos wider than a P5
  (7 semitones) anywhere.

**Timbral effects (efectos tímbricos)**
- Key clicks, and blowing air through the tube with no tone; blowing through the reed (noteheads "x");
  with the reed removed, air through the tube for a plain wind-noise effect (contemporary scores).
- R30 [ALLOWED] Quarter-tone glissando up or down by changing embouchure or slightly pulling the reed out.
- R31 [MUST] Multiphonics: easy to produce but often strident and not every oboist can; consult the player for
  the best available one and always print the fingering with it (see ex. 8-43, 8-45).

**Rules — multiple oboes (oboes múltiples)**
- R32 [PREFER] Standard section: 2 oboes + English horn; sometimes 3+ oboes + EH.
- R33 [ALLOWED] [era: early Classical] Two oboes holding tonic and dominant pedals, often in octaves with
  two horns, plus melodic passages — the default early-Classical wind texture.
- R34 [PREFER] When the oboe doubles another instrument, it adds articulation and penetration (nasal colour);
  reserve such doublings for an important melodic line, so the doubled instrument is not eclipsed (ch.8).

**Examples**
- Berlioz, *Symphonie fantastique*, mvt 5, bars 460–467: two oboes, *p leggiero*, staccato chromatic
  6/8 line — light, grotesque oboe unison.
- Kodály, *Háry János* Suite, mvt 2, bars 47–51: two oboes *ff* in parallel thirds with triplets and grace
  notes — folk-like, pungent paired oboes.
- Bartók, *Concerto for Orchestra*, mvt 5, bars 249–254: three oboes in close triads, staccato chords,
  Allegro 2/4 — oboe triad block.
- Additional study: Stravinsky *Petrushka* ("Russian Dance": 3 oboes + EH; "Wet-Nurses' Dance").

**Agent applicability**
- Oboe track: MIDI 58–89; solos centred 65–84. Never set velocity/CC1 to pp-level on notes 58–65.
- No fast repeated-note figures on oboe; give them to flutes/clarinets instead.
- In Classical pastiche, oboes I–II sustain tonic/dominant in octaves with horns I–II.

## 7 English horn (El corno inglés; corno inglese, cor anglais, Englischhorn) (scan p.208–210, printed p.199–201)

**Concepts**
- No standard oboe-family member extends upward; at least three extend downward. The EH is the alto, the
  most popular: conical tube, double reed, both slightly longer than the oboe's, and a bulb bell (campana
  d'amore) that gives a more sonorous, melancholy, almost shivering tone.
- Name: probably from French "cor anglé" (angled horn, after curved early models) mistranslated as "anglais".

**Rules**
- R35 [MUST] Transposing in F: sounds a P5 (7 semitones) below written. Written B3 (59) – ~G6 (91) [top ?];
  concert E3 (52) – ~C6 (84) [top ?].
- R36 [ALLOWED] [era: Baroque] The ancestor oboe da caccia was common; [era: Haydn to Wagner, esp. Germany]
  the EH was practically abandoned (exceptions: Berlioz, Meyerbeer); from the mid-19th c. it holds a
  distinguished orchestral place.
- R37 [PREFER] Use its lowest 5th–6th (concert ~E3–C4, MIDI 52–60): very beautiful, rich, expressive, with
  tremendous projection.
- R38 [AVOID] Its upper register for a characteristic EH colour: it weakens as it rises and there sounds so
  much like the oboe that it loses its personality.
- R39 [AVOID] Same trill/tremolo limits as the oboe; keep tremolos to small intervals, especially above the
  staff.
- R40 [ALLOWED] As agile as the oboe, including the hardest figures and recent effects (Holliger: multiphonics,
  microtones, mostly solo repertoire).

**Register characteristics (written; approximate [?])**
- B3–~G4: deep, rich, intense. ~G4–~D6: soft, reedy, sonorous. Top: weak, constricted.

**Examples**
- Berlioz, *Le carnaval romain* overture, bars 21–37: EH solo, Andante, *mf espressivo*, long lyrical tune.
- Wagner, *Tristan und Isolde*, Act III, Sc. 1, bars 5–11: unaccompanied EH solo (shepherd's lament),
  *p* < *f*, dim., wide range.
- Sibelius, *The Swan of Tuonela*, bars 18–32: EH solo, Andante molto sostenuto, from *ff* to *p*, long held
  notes — the EH as a dark, mythical narrator.
- Additional study: Berlioz *Fantastique* III (oboe–EH duo), Copland *Quiet City*, Debussy "Nuages", Dvořák 9
  mvt 2, Franck Symphony mvt 2, Rossini *William Tell*, Stravinsky *Rite*, Tchaikovsky *Romeo and Juliet*.

**Agent applicability**
- EH track: MIDI 52–~84, solo sweet spot 52–72. For a mournful/pastoral solo choose EH over oboe when the
  line lies around 52–67.

## 7 Other oboe-family members (scan p.210–213, printed p.201–204)

### Oboe d'amore (oboe de amor; hautbois d'amour)

**Rules**
- R41 [MUST] Transposing in A: sounds a m3 (3 semitones) below written. Written B3 (59) – ~E6 (88) [top ?];
  concert G#3 (56) – ~C#6 (85) [top ?].
- R42 [ALLOWED] [era: Baroque] Very popular; Bach and others sometimes wrote sounding pitch in the part
  (e.g. *Christmas Oratorio*). Modern practice: write the transposed part.
- R43 [ALLOWED] [era: after Baroque] Fell out of use (replaced by oboe and EH as halls grew); reappears in
  some 20th-c. works but is still uncommon.
- R44 [PREFER] Use its low notes: full, dark, beautiful; tone much sweeter than the oboe (bulb bell).
- R45 [AVOID] Upper register: rather weak and almost useless. Strauss (*Sinfonia domestica*) demands F6
  [written/sounding not stated]; Schuller (*Concerto for Orchestra No. 2*) writes above the staff systematically
  but doubles it with other instruments.

**Examples**
- Bach, *Christmas Oratorio*, Sinfonia, bars 9–11: two oboi d'amore + two oboi da caccia (alto clef, at
  sounding pitch) form a pastoral reed choir that alternates with flutes + strings + continuo (antiphonal
  choirs, 12/8).
- R. Strauss, *Sinfonia domestica*, bars 156–164: oboe d'amore solo *p* "sehr zart" over 2nd violins (desks 1–3)
  in *ppp* tremolo chords; bars 209–215 "Munter", *fp*, lively.
- Additional study: Bach Cantata 37, Debussy "Gigues", Mahler "Um Mitternacht", Ravel *Boléro* (bars 77–84),
  Schuller, Strauss *Der Rosenkavalier*.

### Oboe da caccia

**Rules**
- R46 [ALLOWED] [era: Baroque] Direct ancestor of the EH; transposes a P5 down. Bach often wrote its sounding
  pitch in alto clef (C clef on line 3); older French notation used the C clef on line 4 [?].
- R47 [PREFER] If used today (practically obsolete), notate it like the English horn: same range and
  characteristics. It is being revived with early-music practice (like the sackbut, built in at least seven
  sizes in the 16th c.).

### Heckelphone (heckélfono) and baritone/bass oboe (oboe barítono o bajo)

**Concepts**
- Heckelphone: pseudo bass oboe perfected c.1904 by Wilhelm Heckel (Biebrich); built by a bassoon maker,
  looks like a bassoon. Baritone/bass oboe: same range, transposition and very similar sound; built by oboe
  makers, looks like a large EH with bulb bell.

**Rules**
- R48 [MUST] Sounds an octave below the oboe and adds one semitone at the bottom: concert A2 (45). Written
  A3 (57) – ~E6 (88), with an optional higher note ~G6 [?]; concert A2 (45) – ~E5 (76) [top ?].
- R49 [AVOID] Specifying either in a normal orchestral part without checking: very few instruments exist and
  few orchestras could supply one. All heckelphone parts are playable on the bass oboe.
- R50 [PREFER] When available, use it as the bass of the oboe family (double-reed choir: oboe/EH/heckelphone).

**Examples**
- Delius, *Dance Rhapsody* (No. 1), bars 1–10: EH melody *pp* with the bass oboe beneath in low triplet lines,
  over divided cellos and pizzicato basses — double-reed duet in the low-mid register.
- R. Strauss, *Salome* at [326]: heckelphone solo, Andante cantabile, *f* dim. to *p* then cresc.— lyrical
  baritone reed line. Strauss also used it in *Elektra*; Delius used it too.
- Additional study: Chávez *Sinfonía de Antígona*.

**Agent applicability**
- Low double-reed colour without a heckelphone in the template: use bassoon or EH low notes; reserve
  heckelphone patches (if any) for MIDI 45–~72.

## 7 Clarinet (El clarinete; clarinetto, clarinette, Klarinette) (scan p.214–215, printed p.205–206)

**Concepts**
- Cylindrical tube with a bell slightly wider than the oboe's; mouthpiece (boquilla, "pico") with a single
  reed; five sections.
- All clarinets share one fingering system, so clarinettists play every size. Pitch depends on size: Bb, Eb, A.
  Family pictured: Eb (requinto), Bb, A, alto in Eb, basset horn (corno di bassetto) in F, bass in Bb,
  contrabass in Bb.
- Register break (cambio de registro / "paso"): between written Bb4 (open tube, no keys) and written B4 (all
  holes closed plus the left-thumb register key). The clarinet overblows at the twelfth (19 semitones), other
  woodwinds at the octave, so its break is more pronounced.
- Registers named after ancestors: chalumeau (lowest; from the medieval single-reed cylindrical pipe) and
  clarion (clarín; third and widest register; from the high Baroque trumpet). "Clarinete" is a diminutive of
  "clarín".

**Rules**
- R51 [ALLOWED] [era: modern] The C clarinet is rarely used today.
- R52 [MUST] All clarinets share the same written range: lowest written E3 (52) (except specially built bass
  clarinets), top ~C7 (96) [?]. Concert: Bb clarinet sounds M2 (2) lower = D3 (50) – ~Bb6 (94); A clarinet sounds
  m3 (3) lower = C#3 (49) – ~A6 (93) [tops ?]. Check the transposition table (Adler p.169) for other sizes.
- R53 [AVOID] Exposed, fast or slurred crossings of the break (written Bb4↔B4; concert Ab4↔A4 = 68↔69 on Bb
  clarinet, G4↔G#4 = 67↔68 on A clarinet) for inexperienced players; specialists handle it, and the
  problem fades with experience.
- R54 [ALLOWED] Full dynamic range, from the softest *pp* to the strongest *ff*, in every register, including
  the lowest and highest: the most homogeneous register of all woodwinds.

**Register characteristics (written; approximate [?])**
- Chalumeau, E3–~F#4: deep, rich. Throat tones (sonidos guturales), ~G4–Bb4: rather faint. Clarion, B4–~C6:
  brilliant, incisive, expressive. Top (~C6–C7): penetrating, shrill.

**Agent applicability**
- Bb clarinet track: MIDI 50–~94; throat tones (concert ~65–68) are weaker, so raise CC1/velocity slightly there
  or avoid giving them the melodic peak (inferred).
- Unlike oboe (R22), clarinet low notes may be marked *pp*: use it for soft low pads/subtones.
