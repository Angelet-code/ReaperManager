# Walter Piston, Orchestration — scans s020L–s029R (printed p.40–59)

All ranges concert pitch (the violin does not transpose). Open strings: G3 (55), D4 (62), A4 (69), E5 (76).
Running head on every page is "The Violin"; the range starts mid-chapter (first-position fingering) and
ends mid-section (Harmonics).

## The Violin — first position (printed p.40)

**Concepts**
- first position (primera posición): hand at the nut; fingers 1–4 stop the four diatonic notes above each open string.
- First-position notes (Fig. 16): G string G3 A3 B3 C4 D4 (55–62); D string D4 E4 F4 G4 A4 (62–69); A string
  A4 B4 C5 D5 E5 (69–76); E string E5 F5 G5 A5 B5 (76–83). First-position compass therefore G3–B5 (55–83).

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] On the E string the 1st finger plays F5 a semitone above the open string (finger drawn back to the nut); on the other strings 1st finger is a whole tone up.
- R2 [ALLOWED] Cross-string finger relations (same position): 1st finger → 2nd finger on next higher string = m6 (8); 1st → 4th finger on next higher string = P8 (12); 2nd finger → 3rd finger on next lower string = P4 (5); the same finger on adjacent strings = P5 (7) (fifths "lie across the strings").

**Agent applicability**
- Notes 55–83 are the easiest, most secure violin register for fast or exposed passagework.

## Open strings (cuerdas al aire) (printed p.40–41)

**Rules**
- R3 [ALLOWED] In first position the 4th finger duplicates the open-string pitches A, D and E (on the next lower string), so the player can choose stopped or open.
- R4 [PREFER] Open-string tone is clearer, richer in overtones and more prominent than stopped tone, but cannot be shaded by the left hand (no vibrato) and its pitch cannot be adjusted. *Why:* string vibrates against the hard nut, not the fingertip.
- R5 [AVOID] Open strings in expressive melodic phrases.
- R6 [ALLOWED] Open strings in fast scales and figures (regular practice), and may be exploited for their characteristic ringing sound.
- R7 [ALLOWED] Intonation compromises of open strings (fixed pitch in enharmonic contexts, natural harmonics tied to the open string) are tolerated as a necessary evil.

**Examples**
- Ex.36 Wagner, *Tannhäuser* Bacchanale (Vn I, ff): open G stands in for F-double-sharp in a chromatic run — enharmonic use of an open string.
- Ex.37 Brahms, Symphony 4 (Vn I+II, fp dim.): figuration deliberately built on open-string notes (marked o) for their resonant string colour.

**Agent applicability**
- For a cantabile violin line, avoid long held notes exactly on 55/62/69/76 when a sample library offers a separate open-string/non-vibrato layer (inferred); in fast figuration they are fine and add ring.

**Tags:** #orchestration/strings/open-strings

## Accidentals and chromatic progression (printed p.41–43)

**Rules**
- R8 [MUST] A note with an accidental uses the same finger as its natural: on the G string the 1st finger plays Ab3, A3 and A#3; the 1st finger also plays G#3 (sharpened open string).
- R9 [ALLOWED] Traditional fingering: two notes a chromatic semitone apart are taken with the same finger, so a legato bowing produces some unavoidable portamento (Fig. 18a/b).
- R10 [PREFER] For rapid chromatic passages, use a separate finger for each chromatic step (Fig. 18c, 1-2-3-4): cleaner articulation with no slide. Piston says this has partly superseded the traditional method.
- R11 [ALLOWED] At slow tempo the traditional same-finger fingering keeps the harmonic distinction (e.g. G–G#–A read in G vs. G–Ab–A in Ab) and the slides typical of "violin style"; choose fingering for musical, not mechanical, reasons.
- R12 [ALLOWED] Détaché bowing removes the audible finger slides whatever the fingering.

**Agent applicability**
- Fast chromatic violin runs are idiomatic; on slow slurred chromatic lines a legato patch with slight portamento is stylistically correct.

## Intonation (afinación) (printed p.43)

**Rules**
- R13 [ALLOWED] String players do not follow any fixed tuning system (equal, Pythagorean, just, mean-tone); they adjust by ear continuously.
- R14 [PREFER] Leading tones and chromatically raised notes are played sharp, close to the note they resolve to; flattened notes are played low.
- R15 [ALLOWED] The "vibrancy" of a unison string section comes partly from tiny pitch differences among players.

**Agent applicability**
- Section patches already contain this spread; layering a solo + section patch, or two slightly detuned section tracks, imitates it (inferred). Do not quantize-detune solo lines by hand.

**Tags:** #orchestration/strings/intonation

## Position technique (posiciones) (printed p.43–45)

**Concepts**
- position: where the hand sits on the neck; position n puts the 1st finger n diatonic steps above the open string.
- Fig. 20 (fingers 1–4):
  - G string: 2nd pos B3–E4, 3rd pos C4–F4, 4th pos D4–G4.
  - D string: 2nd pos F4–B4, 3rd pos G4–C5, 4th pos A4–D5.
  - A string: 2nd pos C5–F5, 3rd pos D5–G5, 4th pos E5–A5.
  - E string: 2nd pos G5–C6, 3rd pos A5–D6, 4th pos B5–E6.

**Rules**
- R16 [AVOID] Very high notes on the lower strings: they get harder to produce as the string becomes short relative to its thickness.
- R17 [ALLOWED] On the E string, notes can be played to the end of the fingerboard and beyond, but the diatonic intervals become smaller than a fingertip. Fingering at the top is also hard because the hand is extended and the strings are spaced wider near the bridge.
- R18 [PREFER] Practical upper limit for orchestral violin parts, excluding harmonics: E two octaves above the open E = E7 (MIDI 100).
- R19 [ALLOWED] Half position (media posición): 1st finger a semitone above the nut, 2nd finger takes notes normally played by the 1st. It is convenient for some low chromatic combinations.
- R20 [ALLOWED] Fourth-finger extension: in first position, stretch from 1st to 4th finger up to an augmented 4th (6), even a P5 (7); in higher positions, reaching the 4th finger beyond the position is common (Fig. 21).

**Examples**
- Ex.38 Strauss, *Also sprach Zarathustra* (pp, 9/4): a G# in the 13th position on the E string, ≈ G#7 (MIDI 104) (inferred from the position). It is played by two desks of violins, doubled at the unison by piccolo, and supported an octave below by flute, Eb clarinet and two more desks. This is how to secure an extreme violin note: few players, plus woodwind doubling.
- Ex.39 Stravinsky, *Apollon musagète* (Vn I, mp, Allegro): low chromatic figures around G#3–A3 that lie best in half position.

**Agent applicability**
- Cap orchestral violin notes at MIDI 100. Above that, use only a few desks or a solo patch and double at the unison with piccolo/flute.

## Shift of position (cambio de posición) (printed p.46–47)

**Rules**
- R21 [PREFER] The commonest shifts are 1st→3rd, 3rd→5th positions, a slide of a third, usually on the 1st finger (Fig. 22).
- R22 [ALLOWED] The guiding (sliding) finger need not be the finger that stops the new note: e.g. the 1st finger slides B→D while the 4th finger stops G (Fig. 23a).
- R23 [ALLOWED] The slide can be inaudible, but is often allowed to sound for expressive intensification.
- R24 [PREFER] Ascending shift: when style allows, the finger that plays the second note makes a slight portamento just before arriving. Descending: keep the portamento on leaving the upper note minimal (the thumb guides).
- R25 [ALLOWED] Slides are idiomatic; a fingering is sometimes chosen precisely to produce them.

**Examples**
- Ex.40 Strauss, *Der Rosenkavalier* waltz (Vn I, Vn II, Va, Vc, mf with hairpins): upward leaps of 6ths/octaves in a Viennese waltz melody where suppressing portamento would be wrong. The cello also has marked gliss.

**Agent applicability**
- For late-Romantic or Viennese-waltz lyricism, use legato/portamento articulation on wide upward slurred leaps; use plain legato or détaché for Classical style (inferred). `[era: late-Romantic portamento style, c. 1890–1930]`

**Tags:** #orchestration/strings/portamento #style/romantic

## Glissando and portamento (printed p.47–49)

**Concepts**
- glissando (glissando): one finger, one string, legato bow; every intermediate pitch between the limits sounds.
- portamento (portamento): a connecting slide between melodic notes. Composers do not reliably distinguish the two terms.

**Rules**
- R26 [MUST] A true glissando stays on one string. A slide spanning more than one string's range cannot be executed literally; players substitute a fast chromatic scale or partial slides on lower strings.
- R27 [PREFER] Notate exactly the effect wanted (string, start/end, how complete the slide). A straight line between notes is the usual portamento sign but leaves the slide's completeness unclear.
- R28 [MUST] A portamento down from a note too high for the designated string is impossible; at most a small slide at the start and end of the descent.
- R29 [ALLOWED] String indications (sul IV, sul II…) can be added to fix where the glissando is made.

**Examples**
- Ex.41 Ravel, *Daphnis et Chloé* (pp<ff>): glissando up and down two octaves in fast tempo by Vn I, Vn II, Va, half the celli and basses in unison/octaves, all on open A strings — a whole-section sweep.
- Ex.42 Bartók, *Dance Suite* (Vn I con sord., p): glissandos designated sul IV and sul II, the only strings they could be made on.
- Ex.43 Mahler, Symphony 4 (Vn I, pp, cantando): lines between notes, readable as real one-finger glissandos or just as a style of emphasized shift portamentos; includes sul D.
- Ex.44 Mahler, Symphony 4 (Vn I, ppp→ff→p, sul G): a marked slide from a high C down to E on the G string can only be partial.
- Ex.45 Ravel, *La Valse* (Vn I, ff): glissando from a very high note down to G3 (55) followed by a pizz. chord; past the open E it must become a fast chromatic/partial glissando.

**Agent applicability**
- MIDI: render a string glissando with a dedicated gliss/portamento articulation, or pitch-bend, only within one string's span (≈ an octave plus a 5th above the open string). For wider sweeps, write a fast chromatic run or chain partial slides (inferred).

**Tags:** #orchestration/strings/glissando #orchestration/strings/portamento

## Wide skips (saltos amplios) (printed p.49–51)

**Rules**
- R30 [MUST] Judge a leap's difficulty by the strings involved plus the position displacement, not by the interval size alone.
- R31 [ALLOWED] A huge leap that crosses strings may need little shift. A smaller leap on one string can need a large shift: Mahler's tenth F#→A needs first→seventh position, the same shift as Brahms's two octaves plus a M6.
- R32 [MUST] A slurred (legato) leap must use a single string or two adjacent strings. *Why:* the bow cannot stay legato while jumping over an intervening string.
- R33 [ALLOWED] A skilled player can fake a legato skip across intervening strings by lightening and slowing the bow at the moment of crossing; attentive listeners still notice.
- R34 [ALLOWED] Open strings can be used inside passages where the hand stays in a high position.

**Examples**
- Ex.46 Brahms, Symphony 3 (Vn I, f/sf, Andante): leap of two octaves + M6 from C4 (G string, 1st pos, 3rd finger) to A6 (E string, 7th pos, 4th finger). This equals a M7 slide on one string.
- Ex.47 Holst, *The Planets* (Vn I, fff): leaps that cross two strings without changing position (6th position). The opening B is better on the more brilliant E string in 3rd position.
- Ex.48 Mahler, Symphony 9 (Vn I div., p→f→ff, Allegro assai): double-stop figures, then single-string leaps such as F#→A a tenth up with a large shift.
- Ex.49 Reger, *Romantic Suite* op.125 (Vn I, ppp, Vivace): slurred leaps from low notes up to harmonics. These must use one or adjacent strings.
- Ex.50 Berg, *Lyric Suite* (Vn I mit Dämpfer, ff): very wide slurred atonal leaps in triplets, a stress case for R32/R33.
- Ex.51 Hindemith, Symphony in Eb (Vn I, f→ff, half note = 84–88): a high-position passage that alternates with notes on low/open strings.

**Agent applicability**
- When a violin legato line leaps more than about a 12th, check that its two notes can lie on the same or adjacent strings. If not, break the slur (use a non-legato articulation) or reduce the leap.

## Choice of fingerings (printed p.51–52)

**Rules**
- R35 [ALLOWED] Fingering may be chosen to avoid string crossings and simplify bowing.
- R36 [PREFER] For a melody, a fingering with more shifts is often chosen so the line stays on one string, keeping its uniform colour, or to give an expressive turn.
- R37 [ALLOWED] Since about 1955, players have tended to free fingering from the strict position system and fit it to their own hand. Piston notes that "correct" forced positions can hurt intonation. `[era: mid-20th-c. pedagogy trend]`

## Characteristics of the strings (printed p.52–55)

**Concepts**
- Timbre varies a lot across the four strings, and their pitch ranges overlap. So violin registers are not described as low/middle/high like woodwind registers; colour depends on which string plays the note.

**Rules**
- R38 [ALLOWED] String materials as of 1955: E steel (changed from gut in the first quarter of the 20th c., criticized for losing a silky, mellow quality). A gut, some players metal. D gut or gut wound with aluminium. G always wound (silver or copper over gut). All-metal D and G also exist. Metal strings need a fine-tuner on the tailpiece. `[era: 1955 string materials; gut E = pre-c.1925]`
- R39 [PREFER] E string: the most carrying power. Forceful, even strident, at loud dynamics; ethereal, luminous and clear when soft.
- R40 [PREFER] A string: fairly strong in first position, losing brilliance and power in upper positions. For a soft expressive phrase in that range, move up the A string rather than crossing to the E.
- R41 [PREFER] D string: the least powerful string; quiet and subdued compared with the G; suited to calm expression.
- R42 [PREFER] G string: second to the E in sonorous strength; excellent for broad melodies up to the 7th–8th position, but hoarse at its extreme top.
- R43 [PREFER] Cautious upper limit for a melody on the G string: an octave + P4 above the open string = C5 (MIDI 72).
- R44 [PREFER] To keep volume and force in the D-string range, have the melody played on the G string (sul G / IV).
- R45 [ALLOWED] Loud accented notes on the G string can sound ferocious.
- R46 [PREFER] Mark *sul G* (or D, A) with a dotted extension line only when a special effect is intended; otherwise the player picks the string.
- R47 [PREFER] When a phrase crosses strings, the player reconciles their differences to keep colour and intensity continuous.

**Examples**
- Ex.52 Milhaud, Second Symphonic Suite (Vn I, p, souple et animé): a soft melody high on the E string with an ethereal, luminous sound.
- Ex.53 Brahms, Symphony 3, Poco allegretto (Vn I, mezza voce, espress.): an expressive soft melody in the C5–G5 area kept on the A string.
- Ex.54 Beethoven, Piano Concerto 4 (Vn I, pp, Allegro moderato): a calm melody around B4–C5, typical subdued D-string colour.
- Ex.55 Mahler, Symphony 3 (Vn I, pp, "G-Saite", molto espr., morendo to ppp): a broad, expressive G-string melody.
- Ex.56 Bartók, *Concerto for Orchestra* (Vn I+II, f, sempre f, IV): a melody in the D-string range played on the G string to keep power.
- Ex.57 Roussel, Symphony in G minor (Vn I, mf, Allegro vivo): repeated down-bow accented low notes on the G string, a fierce effect.

**Agent applicability**
- Soft, glowing high melody → violins around 76–96 (E string). Warm, intense melody → violins 55–72 (G string), marked as an intense or sul G articulation if the library has one. Calm, veiled colour → 62–74 at low dynamics.
- In mapped terms: loud low violin melody below about A4 is strong (G string); a soft melody in 74–81 is gentler than the same notes forced onto the E string.

## Double stops and chords (dobles cuerdas y acordes) (printed p.55–58)

**Rules**
- R48 [ALLOWED] The bow can sound two adjacent strings at once in any left-hand position: double stops are always bowable if the left hand can stop them.
- R49 [ALLOWED] Three strings sound together only with pressure that pushes the middle string level with the others. This is used for loud, short three-note chords.
- R50 [MUST] Other three- and four-note chords are broken: the lower note(s) are played as grace notes, by custom before the beat, and the top one or two notes are held.
- R51 [ALLOWED] To avoid that anticipation, write the arpeggiation out with the lowest notes on the beat (Fig. 25e). Usually composers write the plain chord and rely on the players.
- R52 [MUST] One finger can stop two adjacent strings (a P5) but not three or four.
- R53 [PREFER] Put higher-numbered fingers on higher strings (outward-slanting hand). The reverse (Fig. 26b) twists the hand, damps the E and A strings and is less sure, though playable.
- R54 [PREFER] Simple finger patterns: complex ones need more time to set and are more likely out of tune.
- R55 [ALLOWED] Open strings are common in chords and double stops and can combine with stopped notes in higher positions (Fig. 27).
- R56 [PREFER] A unison of a stopped note with the same pitch on an open string is very resonant, especially in tremolo.

**Examples**
- Ex.58 Beethoven, *Eroica* opening (tutti f, staccato Eb chords). Vn I plays a four-string chord as near-simultaneously as possible to match the winds' staccato; Vn II and Va play double stops; wind/brass/timpani share the chord; then strings p with a cello theme.
- Ex.59 Prokofiev, *Chout* (Vns I+II non div., ff, Allegro marziale): repeated chords using stopped + open unisons for maximum resonance.

**Agent applicability**
- Section chords in MIDI: in a sample-based violin section, write divisi (separate notes) rather than relying on real multiple stops (inferred).
- For a solo or tutti "chord hit" effect in fff, roll lower notes about 20–40 ms early and hold the top note (inferred).
- Keep any notated violin chord playable: at most 4 notes on 4 adjacent strings, with the lowest note at least G3 (55).

**Tags:** #orchestration/strings/double-stops #orchestration/strings/chords

## Arpeggios across strings (printed p.58)

**Rules**
- R57 [ALLOWED] The finger positions of three- or four-note chords are the usual basis for arpeggio figures across the strings.
- R58 [ALLOWED] The bow can play such arpeggios legato back and forth (slurred) or let the bow bounce on its own elasticity (bounced, ricochet-like staccato).

**Examples**
- Ex.60 Wagner, *Die Walküre*, Siegmund's Liebeslied (Vn I, f, più f, dim.): slurred 32nd-note arpeggios rocking across the strings.
- Ex.61 Rimsky-Korsakov, *Capriccio espagnol* (solo violin, Vivo e strepitoso): a bounced arpeggio across all four strings, with staccato dots.

**Agent applicability**
- Arpeggio figuration for violins should outline a chord fingerable in one position (notes on adjacent strings, about 4 notes spanning up to ~2 octaves). Use legato articulation for slurred figures and spiccato for bounced ones.

**Tags:** #orchestration/strings/arpeggio #orchestration/strings/bowing

## Harmonics (armónicos) (printed p.58–59; continues past p.59)

**Rules**
- R59 [ALLOWED] Harmonics are mainly a colour resource, but the octave harmonic is often used as an ordinary melodic note, marked or not.
- R60 [PREFER] Upper limit for natural harmonics: the 5th harmonic, two octaves + M3 above the open string. That is B5 (83) on G, F#6 (90) on D, C#7 (97) on A and G#7 (104) on E (computed).
- R61 [ALLOWED] Higher harmonic pitches are in practice taken as artificial harmonics (armónicos artificiales), up to about the 4th–5th position on the E string; even higher ones have been written.
- R62 [ALLOWED] High pedal notes in harmonics, often with bowed tremolo, are common; mutes enhance the effect.
- R63 [PREFER] Soft chords in harmonics for divided, muted violins make an atmospheric background colour.
- R64 [ALLOWED] Artificial harmonics in quick succession (legato or détaché) need continual shifting, like playing a melody with one finger. Possible, but not agile.
- R65 [ALLOWED] A harmonic glissando works with a body of strings: the 3rd/4th finger lightly brushes the upper half of the string, harmonics sound at the nodes, and no portamento is heard between them.

**Examples**
- Ex.62 Schubert, Symphony 5, Menuetto (Vn I, p): an octave harmonic (o, 4th finger) inside the melodic line.
- Ex.63 Copland, Symphony 3 (Vn I, ff, quarter note = 76): repeated accented high chords (>>>), cited for very high harmonics [?: exact pitches/harmonic notation not fully legible].
- Ex.64 Milhaud, Symphony 2 (Vn I, pp, 8va, half note = 100): a slow melody in artificial harmonics.

**Agent applicability**
- Atmospheric pads: divided violins in harmonics (or a "harmonics" articulation), pp, muted, sustained or bowed tremolo, roughly 84–100.
- Keep harmonic melodies slow.
