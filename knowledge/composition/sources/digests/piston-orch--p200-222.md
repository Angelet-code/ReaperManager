## Ch.10 The Bassoon — orchestral uses, end of section (scan s100L–s100R, printed p.200–201)

**Rules**
- R1 [PREFER] Use the bassoon (fagot) freely inside accompaniment figures with other woodwinds and horns; it blends unobtrusively.
- R2 [PREFER] The bassoon is especially good at melodic doubling.
- R3 [ALLOWED] `[era: Classical, Haydn/Mozart]` Double a first-violin melody with bassoon one octave (12 semitones) below. Classical composers liked this.
- R4 [ALLOWED] A unison of several bassoons in their high register with English horn and horn makes a mixed timbre in which each colour is still heard. The English horn is not swallowed by the others.
- R5 [PREFER] A group of bassoons (2–3) suits neat, rhythmic music that mixes staccato and slurred notes.

**Examples**
- Haydn, Symphony in G "Surprise" (Paukenschlag), Menuetto (Ex.230): Bn I, *p*, plays the tune an octave under Vln I. Shows classical octave doubling.
- Berlioz, *Roméo et Juliette* (Ex.231): Largo espressivo *mf*, 4 bassoons + English horn + horn in unison in the high bassoon register (tenor clef), with violas added later at *sf*. Shows a composite unison timbre.
- Bartók, *Concerto for Orchestra*, Allegretto scherzando (Ex.232): solo trio of 3 bassoons, *p*. Bn I–II move in parallel with staccato and slurs, and Bn III plays running staccato 16ths. Shows the bassoon section as a light, rhythmic chamber group.

**Agent applicability**
- For classical-style melody, add a bassoon track that copies Vln I minus 12 semitones at a softer dynamic (CC1/velocity about one step lower).
- A bassoon trio works well for scherzo-like staccato passages: velocity-driven shorts with occasional 2-note slurs.

## Ch.10 — The Contrabassoon (contrafagot) (scan s100R–s102R, printed p.201–205)

Names: Fr. *contrebasson*, It. *contrafagotto*, Ger. *Kontrafagott*; also called double-bassoon.

**Concepts**
- Construction: 18 ft 4 in air column. Heckel's design has 4 parallel wooden tubes and a brass bell pointing down. Other models point the bell up, and some French ones were all brass. A floor peg carries the weight.
- It uses the bassoon's technique and range, one octave lower.

**Rules: range (converted to concert MIDI)**
- R1 [MUST] Lowest standard note: Bb a whole tone below the five-string double bass's low C (C1 = 24), i.e. **Bb0 = MIDI 22**.
- R2 [ALLOWED] Strauss wrote **A0 = 21**. Low-A extensions exist but are rarely seen.
- R3 [MUST] Notation: write it as a transposing instrument, sounding one octave below written, like the double bass. This lets the bassoonist use familiar fingerings. Debussy (*Iberia*) and Wagner (*Parsifal*) are exceptions that wrote it at pitch. (MIDI: always write concert pitch.)
- R4 [ALLOWED] If a work needs no note below (written) C, players remove the extension pipe and bell to lighten the instrument. (Inferred: written C2 = sounding C1 = 24.)
- R5 [AVOID] Upper register. The tone is weak from the first overblown note F# upward (inferred: written F#3 = sounding **F#2 = 42**).
- R6 [PREFER] Upper limit: the F above the staff is a wise limit (inferred written F4 = sounding **F3 = 53** [?]). Mahler goes to the A beyond (≈ A3 = 57 [?]), and Ravel asks for B in *Ma Mère l'Oye* (≈ B3 = 59 [?]). Those notes are playable but sound poor next to the same pitches on the bassoon. Use the bassoon there instead.
- R7 [ALLOWED] When doubling the double basses, the contrabassoon is often taken higher than it would go on its own.

**Rules: tone and use**
- R8 [PREFER] The lower octave is its most useful range. The tone is rougher than the bassoon's, and in the low register the buzz of the slow vibrations is often audible.
- R9 [PREFER] Contrabassoon in octaves with the bassoons gives a full, sonorous woodwind foundation at soft or loud dynamics. It balances the modern string and brass sections, which bassoons and bass clarinet alone cannot do.
- R10 [PREFER] Use it to reinforce and sharpen the double-bass line, or as a deep bass under brass groups.
- R11 [MUST] Low notes use a lot of breath, so give the part frequent rests.
- R12 [AVOID] Nimble or agile writing. Slow response and inertia make fast passages sound clumsy, even though anything playable on the bassoon is theoretically playable. A doubling can cover the clumsiness.
- R13 [AVOID] Fast repeated tonguing, because the air column responds slowly. Its staccato is dry and heavy.
- R14 [ALLOWED] Solo use for characterization, descriptive or grotesque effects, and also for purely musical phrases.

**Examples**
- Ravel, *La Valse* (Ex.233): solo contrabassoon, *ff*/*f*, accented low downbeats and leaping chromatic eighth-note lines. Shows the low register's weight and its waltz-bass role.
- Strauss, *Till Eulenspiegel* (Ex.234): accented *ff* eighth-note line in 6/8 inside a noisy tutti. Shows staccato kept at a tempo the tongue can manage, in characteristic style.
- Mahler, Symphony No. 9, Ländler "Sehr gemächlich" (Ex.235): Bn I and Hn II share the tune in unison with different dynamics (bassoon *sempre p*, horn cresc. to *f molto espr.*). The bassoon line briefly drops out under the horn. The contrabassoon has a staccato/trill bass line at *f*/*sf*. Cellos hold an open-string double pedal (fifth C–G), and the harmonies come from that pedal. Shows a coloristic chamber-like texture.
- Strauss, *Elektra* (Ex.236): long *pp* legato contrabassoon melody one octave below a unison of 2 Wagner tubas and a contrabass tuba, all muted. Shows the contrabassoon as a sub-octave under soft brass.

**Agent applicability**
- Contrabassoon range for MIDI: practical 22–53; 21 only by special request; avoid above ~42 except when doubling the basses.
- Default role: double the bassoon bass or the double bass an octave below, with sustained or moderate rhythms. Leave a rest of at least 1–2 beats every few bars.
- Avoid 16th-note runs and fast repeated notes. If a fast bass line is needed, double it with basses or bassoons so the clumsiness is masked.

## Ch.10 — The Sarrusophone (scan s102R, printed p.205)

**Concepts**
- The sarrusophone is a family of 9 brass double-reed instruments with a wide conical bore, from sopranino in Eb to contrabass in Bb. It was used in European military bands. Keys and tone holes resemble the saxophone's, and the fingering resembles flute, oboe and saxophone. The contrabass is folded like a contrabassoon, with its bell pointing up.

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] `[era: French orchestral practice, late 19th–early 20th c.]` Some French composers used the contrabass sarrusophone in C instead of the contrabassoon. It is the only member ever used in the orchestra.
- R2 [PREFER] `[era: 1955]` Parts marked sarrusophone are now played on the contrabassoon. The contrabassoon has improved, and the sarrusophone tone suits open air more than a concert hall.

**Agent applicability**
- If a score calls for sarrusophone, write the part on a contrabassoon track.

## Ch.11 Brass Instruments — introduction and section make-up (scan s103L–s104L, printed p.206–208)

**Concepts**
- Brass types are less standardized than woodwinds. They differ by country, and players often use instruments other than the ones named in the score. In older works, such as Beethoven's Ninth, today's brass sound is not the sound the composer imagined. `[era]`
- Study plan: (1) principles of construction and technique common to all brass; (2) the instruments that have played these parts, including natural horns and trumpets and other obsolete instruments needed to understand classical scores; (3) current practice.
- The modern section has 4 subgroups: horns, trumpets, trombones, tubas. Auxiliary instruments do not form families with these groups the way woodwinds do.

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] `[era: 1955]` Write horns (French horns) in F, even though players may use Bb horns or F/Bb double horns.
- R2 [PREFER] Trumpets are in Bb or C. D trumpet and bass trumpet appear rarely.
- R3 [AVOID] Treating cornets as trumpets. They are hardly members of the trumpet family.
- R4 [PREFER] Trombones are tenor trombones, except that one may be a bass or a tenor-bass instrument.
- R5 [PREFER] Tuba means the bass tuba. The tenor tuba (euphonium) is used only occasionally.
- R6 [ALLOWED] Wagner tubas are special instruments, closer to horns than to tubas.
- R7 [PREFER] Standard brass section for a score with woodwinds by threes: **4 horns, 3 (or 2) trumpets, 3 trombones, 1 tuba**. Relate every variant to this standard. Personal taste and the character of the piece decide the variants.
- R8 [ALLOWED] `[era: Classical]` Classical convention: 2 horns + 2 trumpets.
- R9 [ALLOWED] `[era: late 19th–early 20th c.]` Expanded sections, as below.

**Examples (section make-ups)**
- Berlioz, *Symphonie fantastique*: 4 hn, 2 cornets, 2 tpt, 3 tbn, 2 bass tubas.
- Holst, *The Planets*: 6 hn, 4 tpt, 3 tbn, 1 tenor tuba, 1 bass tuba.
- Wagner, *Götterdämmerung*: 8 hn, 4 Wagner tubas, 3 tpt, 1 bass tpt, 3 tbn, 1 contrabass tbn, 1 contrabass tuba.
- Schoenberg, *Gurrelieder*: 10 hn, 4 Wagner tubas, 6 tpt, 1 bass tpt, 1 alto tbn, 4 tenor-bass tbn, 1 bass tbn in Eb, 1 contrabass tbn, 1 contrabass tuba.

**Agent applicability**
- Default orchestral template brass: HN 1–4, TPT 1–3, TBN 1–2 (tenor) + TBN 3 (bass), TUBA. For a Haydn/Mozart sound, use 2 HN + 2 TPT only, with no trombones in symphonies (inferred from "classical convention").

## Ch.11 — Tone production and mouthpieces (scan s104L–s104R, printed p.208–209)

**Concepts**
- The player's lips are the sound generator, held against a cup-shaped metal mouthpiece. Lip vibration drives the air column.
- The mouthpiece's interior shape affects tone and how easily low or high notes come.

**Rules**
- R1 [PREFER] A shallow cup gives brilliance and force by bringing out the upper partials (trumpet, trombone).
- R2 [PREFER] A cone-shaped (funnel) mouthpiece that merges gently into the bore gives softness and mellowness (horn).
- Fig.61 shows the average cross-sections for horn (long funnel), trumpet (shallow cup), trombone (cup) and tuba (large deep cup). No universal standard exists.

## Ch.11 — Overblowing and the harmonic series (scan s104R–s105L, printed p.209–210)

**Concepts**
- Lip tension isolates the harmonics (partials, armónicos) of the tube's fundamental. Tube length sets the fundamental.
- Fig.62, harmonic series of an 8-foot tube (fundamental C2 = 36), with harmonic number → note (MIDI): 1 C2 (36), 2 C3 (48), 3 G3 (55), 4 C4 (60), 5 E4 (64), 6 G4 (67), 7 Bb4 (70), 8 C5 (72), 9 D5 (74), 10 E5 (76), 11 F#5 (78), 12 G5 (79), 13 A5 (81), 14 Bb5 (82), 15 B5 (83), 16 C6 (84). The "chord of nature" (acorde de la naturaleza).
- Intervals between successive harmonics shrink steadily. The m3 between harmonics 6 and 7 is smaller than the m3 between 5 and 6.

**Rules**
- R1 [AVOID] The 7th harmonic sounds flat, so brass playing regularly avoids it.
- R2 [AVOID] Harmonics 7, 11, 13 and 14 are out of tune with equal temperament. The major seconds 8–9 and 9–10 are also unequal.
- R3 [ALLOWED] Lips can bend pitch within small limits. The trombone slide and the horn player's right hand in the bell adjust more.
- R4 (context) Brass intonation needs constant adjustment to the other instruments. Pitch is not absolute.

**Agent applicability**
- For natural-brass writing `[era: natural horn/trumpet]`, use harmonics 3–6, 8–10, 12 and 16 of the crook's series. Avoid 7, 11, 13 and 14 unless a deliberate "out-of-tune natural" colour is wanted.

## Ch.11 — Range (scan s105L–s105R, printed p.210–211)

**Rules**
- R1 [PREFER] Think of a brass instrument's range as its tube length (which fixes the fundamental) plus the span of harmonics it can produce.
- R2 (fact) A shorter tube makes the highest partials harder to sound.
- R3 (fact) At equal length, a wider bore makes low harmonics easier and a narrower bore makes high ones easier. Mouthpiece width and depth and the player's skill also matter.
- R4 [AVOID] Notes above the 16th harmonic. Some players can sound them, but they are difficult and insecure in pitch.
- R5 (fact) Fundamentals are called pedal tones (notas pedal). They are not always obtainable or may sound too poor to use. "Half-tube" instruments cannot sound their fundamental; "whole-tube" instruments can.

**Tags:** #orchestration/brass #orchestration/range

## Ch.11 — Crooks and slides (scan s105R, printed p.211)

**Rules**
- R1 (fact) `[era: Classical, natural brass]` Horns and trumpets played only the harmonic series of their current fundamental. Players changed the fundamental by fitting crooks (tonos/cuerpos de recambio), which took time. A U-shaped tuning slide gave fine adjustment.
- R2 (fact) The trombone works on the slide principle, which dates back at least to the 15th century.
- R3 (fact) Tenor trombone slide positions I–VII (Fig.63), each lowering the fundamental by one semitone: I Bb1 (34), II A1 (33), III Ab1 (32), IV G1 (31), V Gb1 (30), VI F1 (29), VII E1 (28). The total span is a diminished fifth (6 semitones), so there are seven harmonic series.
- R4 [AVOID] Writing as if position changes were instantaneous. They take time and create technical problems; distant slide moves in fast legato are hard (inferred).
- R5 (fact) The slide allows ideal intonation, since it can be adjusted freely by ear.

**Agent applicability**
- Write natural horn/trumpet parts in classical style from the crook's series only (see harmonic series above).
- For fast trombone passages, prefer notes whose positions are close together (inferred).

**Tags:** #orchestration/brass #orchestration/trombone #era/natural-brass

## Ch.11 — Valves (scan s106L–s107L, printed p.212–214)

**Concepts**
- A valve opens an extra loop of tubing, lowering the pitch at once. It does what changing crooks used to do. There are two types: rotary (lever turns a drum a quarter turn) and piston (moves up and down in a cylinder), both returned by springs (Fig.64).
- Brass instruments change pitch by lengthening the tube. Woodwinds do it by shortening the air column through open holes.

**Rules**
- R1 (fact) `[era: valves established in the orchestra only well after 1850]` Valves were invented early in the 19th c. but were slow to be accepted. (Use natural brass for earlier styles.)
- R2 (fact) Three valves, played by the first three fingers of the right hand. The horn is fingered with the left hand. Valve 1 lowers a whole tone (2 semitones), valve 2 a semitone (1), valve 3 a tone and a half (3).
- R3 (fact) Valve combinations and their trombone-position equivalents: none = 0 (1st pos.); 2 = −1 (2nd); 1 = −2 (3rd); 3 or 1+2 = −3 (4th); 2+3 = −4 (5th); 1+3 = −5 (6th); 1+2+3 = −6 (7th).
- R4 [AVOID] Valve combinations, especially those with valve 3, are out of tune. Each valve tube is the right length only for the open tube, so combined tubes come out too short.
- R5 [PREFER] On trumpets and cornets, valve 3's tube is made a little long. Valve 3 alone is then flat and must be lipped up, so players prefer 1+2 over 3 alone.
- R6 (fact) Players correct pitch with the free hand on valve slides, rings or levers, automatic compensating systems, or extra valves on larger instruments.
- R7 (fact) Extra valves also extend the low range or transpose the whole instrument (details per instrument later). The ascending valve on some French-made horns is an exception to the lengthening principle. Hand stopping on the horn lowers pitch without lengthening the tube.

## Ch.11 — Fingering (scan s107L–s107R, printed p.214–215)

**Concepts**
- The 8-foot C harmonic series, transposed down by semitones six times, gives every note the seven valve combinations can theoretically reach. Fig.65 is a descending chromatic scale from harmonic 16 (C6 = 84) down to the pedal range, with valve numbers and harmonic numbers.

**Rules**
- R1 [PREFER] Players choose the fingering that adds the least tubing, because the open series and the nearest combinations give the purest tones.
- R2 [ALLOWED] High notes occur in several series. Players sometimes pick an alternate fingering for its special sound or for convenience next to neighbouring notes (e.g., trills).
- R3 (fact) Harmonics 12–16 are close enough to play with the lips alone, but valves help intonation and articulation.
- R4 (fact) Lower down, the gaps between open tones widen and valve 3 is needed more. This is why bass instruments (tuba) have more intonation trouble and extra valves.
- R5 [NEVER] On a 3-valve instrument, write nothing between the fundamental and the augmented fourth above it (8-ft C: no notes between C2 and F#2, i.e. MIDI 37–41). All three valves lower only a diminished fifth. Filling the gap needs extra valves.
- R6 (fact) Pedal tones extended theoretically down from 8-ft C would need a tube nearly 12 ft long. No brass instrument covers this whole scale; transpose it to each instrument's tube length.

**Agent applicability**
- For a valved brass instrument with fundamental F (MIDI f), avoid MIDI f+1 … f+5 unless the instrument has a 4th valve or is a trombone with an F attachment (inferred generalisation of R5).

## Ch.11 — Embouchure (scan s107R–s108L, printed p.215–216)

**Rules**
- R1 (fact) Lips are loose for low notes and tight for high notes. High notes and tone control need lip strength.
- R2 (fact) Horn players specialise in either high or low notes and do not switch. (The usual high-1/3, low-2/4 layout is not stated here; see horn chapter.)
- R3 [AVOID] Wide leaps across several harmonics of one series. Fig.66a, 8-ft scale: C4 → C5 (harmonic 4 → 8) risks cracking onto the three harmonics in between, and too much tension gives D5 (harmonic 9).
- R4 [AVOID] Slurs to a lower-numbered harmonic of a higher fundamental. Fig.66b: D4 (5th harmonic of Bb1, valve 1) slurred down to C4 (4th harmonic of C2, open) is awkward.
- R5 [PREFER] Slurs between adjacent harmonics of the same series give the best brass legato. Fig.66c: C4–E4–G4 open.
- R6 [ALLOWED] Slurs between non-adjacent harmonics (Fig.66d, e.g. C4–G4) need more skill to avoid intermediate tones and to minimise a break.

**Agent applicability**
- For brass legato lines, prefer stepwise motion or small leaps. Treat leaps over an octave in slurred passages as exposed (fine for a heroic horn call, but not repeated quickly).

## Ch.11 — Tonguing (scan s108L–s110L, printed p.216–220)

**Concepts**
- Brass uses all the woodwind tongue strokes. The woodwind *tu* becomes something like *ta* in upper brass and *dü* (French *du*) in trombone and tuba.
- Double-tonguing (doble picado) is t-k. Triple-tonguing (triple picado) is t-t-k or t-k-t. Flutter-tonguing (frullato) is also used.

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] Brass has more variety of soft and sharp attacks than woodwinds.
- R2 [PREFER] *sforzando*, sharp accents and *forte-piano* suit the brass especially well. Write exactly the accent you want.
- R3 [ALLOWED] Fast single-tongued staccato works on all brass, but tubas and the low registers of horns and trombones respond less promptly than smaller tubes.
- R4 [PREFER] For light fast tonguing, trumpets (or cornets) are the best of the brass.
- R5 [ALLOWED] Double-tonguing can be used by the entire brass section (Ex.240).
- R6 (fact) Players disagree between t-t-k and t-k-t for triple-tonguing; most favour t-t-k. Triplets can also be double-tongued by alternating the starting stroke (t-k-t, k-t-k…).
- R7 [PREFER] Triple-tonguing sounds best on the higher-pitched brass.
- R8 [PREFER] Flutter-tonguing is easy on horn and trumpet and less easy on trombone and tuba. It is sometimes notated like a bowed tremolo (slashes).

**Examples**
- Dvořák, Symphony No. 5 "From the New World" (old numbering), Largo opening (Ex.237): sustained chords at *ppp* for clarinets, bassoons, 4 horns, 2 trumpets, trombones, bass trombone + tuba, swelling to *f dim.* Shows soft attacks at extreme pianissimo in a homorhythmic chorale.
- Strauss, *Don Juan* (Ex.238), brass only: horns, trumpets and trombones + tuba in rhythmic unison, *f*/*ff*, triplets, dotted rhythms and accents. Shows sharp attacks in forte.
- Debussy, *Jeux* (Ex.239): 4 trumpets in C, Scherzando 3/8, *p joyeux et léger* rising lines, then repeated staccato chords at *mf*. Shows light, fast trumpet tonguing.
- Rimsky-Korsakov, *Scheherazade* (Ex.240): whole brass section (4 hn, 2 tpt, 3 tbn + tuba), Vivo 2/8, *f*, repeated chords in fast double-tongued groups with accents and *sf*. Shows section-wide double-tonguing.
- Debussy, *La Mer* (Ex.241): muted horns (*sourdine*) and muted trumpets in F, Allegro 3/4, fast repeated staccato figures, *f* → *p* → *più p* → *pp*. Shows fast repeated tonguing on muted brass at soft dynamics.
- Strauss, *Don Quixote* (Ex.242), all muted (6 horns, 3 trumpets in D, 3 trombones): flutter-tongued chords notated with tremolo slashes, *mf cresc.*, Langsam. Shows flutter-tongue as a descriptive effect (the bleating sheep, inferred).

**Agent applicability**
- Brass chorales at *ppp*: low velocity, slow CC1/CC11 swells, slightly early note starts for slow-attack samples.
- Accents: use sfz/fp shapes (velocity spike + quick CC1/CC11 dip) on brass rather than on strings when a sharp bite is needed.
- Fast repeated notes (16ths at fast tempo): give them to trumpets first and horns second. Keep tuba and low trombones on longer values or every other note.
- Flutter-tongue: use a library articulation (or tremolo keyswitch). Prefer horns and trumpets.

## Ch.11 — Breathing (scan s110R–s111L, printed p.221–222)

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] Brass needs more wind than woodwinds, and players must always keep a reserve. Give brass parts regular rests. Condensed water must be drained from time to time (water key, pulling slides), which also needs short breaks (inferred).
- R2 [PREFER] Phrase marks work as for woodwinds. Composers should mark breathing places, which are not usually marked, and not leave them to players or conductor.
- R3 (fact) In homorhythmic brass chord progressions (Ex.237, Ex.238), a break for breath sounds clearly different from a tongued re-attack. Phrasing must be planned.
- R4 (fact) The diaphragm controls the wind. Puffing out the cheeks is not allowed, because it ruins tone control.

**Agent applicability**
- In brass chorales, place deliberate short gaps (e.g., 1/8–1/4 beat) at phrase ends for breaths. Within a phrase, re-attack (tongue) with overlapping-free note boundaries rather than gaps.

## Ch.11 — Dynamic range (scan s111L, printed p.222; continues on p.223)

**Rules**
- R1 (fact) Full brass can obliterate the rest of the orchestra.
- R2 [NEVER] Do not force strings and woodwinds to compete with full brass. Their loudness has a physical limit, and forcing gives an ugly sound while the brass still dominates.
- R3 [MUST] In big climaxes, measure brass volume against what the strings and woodwinds can produce, or there is no balance.
- R4 [AVOID] Marking brass at a lower dynamic than strings and woodwinds as a fix for bad balance or poor scoring. Reserve that for special purposes.
- R5 (fact) Good brass players treat dynamics as relative, not absolute. The orchestrator places the voices so the result is as close as possible to what was imagined. (Balance also depends on the conductor and orchestra; a first performance of a new score is a poor test.)

**Agent applicability**
- In tutti climaxes, cap brass CC1/velocity so strings and woodwinds at their maximum stay audible. Check this by rendering and measuring per-stem level. Otherwise solve balance through scoring (doublings, register) rather than through lower brass dynamics alone.

## Ch.11 Brass Instruments — end: soft dynamics, mutes, score markings (scan s111R–s112L, printed p.223–224)

*Added by the orchestrating session after synthesis (these two pages fell between chunks).*

**Rules**
- R-a [ALLOWED] Brass pp is softer than brass p but never as soft as a string or woodwind pp; it is a useful pp "in brass terms". *Why:* the brass's own soft dynamic is a distinct colour.
- R-b [ALLOWED] Muted brass is softer still and can fade to inaudibility; loud muted brass (metallic, close to an oboe, cutting when forced) is a common orchestral effect. Horn gets a similar sound by hand-stopping.
- R-c [era: 1955] Coloristic mutes (solotone, harmon, whisper, cup…) came from popular music and were not yet standard symphonic equipment; the ordinary straight cone mute was.
- R-d [MUST] Score order: horns above trumpets, just below the bassoons; four horns on two staves (two per staff). Three trumpets may share a staff unless rhythms differ (then trumpet 3 gets its own). Trombones 1–2 share a staff (usually tenor clef); trombone 3 shares with the tuba (bass clef).
- R-e [era: natural brass] Horn and trumpet parts were written in C without key signature, with the crook named; omitting key signatures for horns and trumpets is still common, and players like it when transposing at sight. Trombones and tuba take the strings' key signature.

**Agent applicability**
- Track/folder order for brass: Hn 1-2, Hn 3-4, Tpt 1, Tpt 2-3, Tbn 1-2, Tbn 3 + Tuba.
- A "distant" brass pp is a real option (low CC1 on the open patch); for near-silent brass use a muted patch, not CC1 alone.
