## 1.1 The string group and its make-up (printed p.3)

**Concepts**
- String group (*cuerda*): violins, violas, 'cellos, double-basses; the dominant element of the symphony
  orchestra throughout its history. Many composers treated winds and brass as accessories and gave the
  essential material mainly to strings.
- Why strings dominate (author's claims): tireless; can play virtually any kind of music; wider dynamic range
  and far more expressive capacity than winds; tone colour fairly homogeneous from top to bottom (register
  differences far subtler than in winds); most versatile in kinds of sound; rich in overtones, so close and
  open spacing both work; the ear tires of string tone much later than of wind tone (hence a large
  string-orchestra repertoire).

**Rules**
- R1 [PREFER] Give the essential musical material (main line, harmony carrying the piece) to strings by default;
  winds and brass colour/reinforce. *Why:* endurance, expressiveness, homogeneity. [era: classical–romantic
  symphonic practice; still Piston's default in 1955]
- R2 [ALLOWED] Any spacing (close or open) of chords within the strings sounds well. *Why:* overtone-rich tone.
- R3 [ALLOWED] Strings can carry long stretches alone (string-orchestra textures) without listener fatigue;
  winds cannot.
- R4 [PREFER] Standard section size: 16 first violins, 14 second violins, 12 violas, 10 'cellos, 8 basses
  (16-14-12-10-8). Proportions vary with the conductor or stage size. [era: mid-20th-c. full symphony orchestra]

**Agent applicability**
- For a "full orchestra" template, size string patches/ensemble choices for a 16-14-12-10-8 section; the
  low strings are fewer players, so a big 1st-violin line is naturally the loudest string voice.
- When a section must sustain for minutes, keep it in strings, not winds.

## 1.2 Tuning (afinación) (printed p.4, Fig. 1)

**Rules / data** (open strings, low to high; concert pitch)
- R5 [MUST] Violin: G3 D4 A4 E5 = MIDI 55, 62, 69, 76 (perfect fifths). Lowest note G3 = 55.
- R6 [MUST] Viola: C3 G3 D4 A4 = MIDI 48, 55, 62, 69. Top string A = 440 Hz (A4) in "present-day tuning".
- R7 [MUST] 'Cello: C2 G2 D3 A3 = MIDI 36, 43, 50, 57 (an octave below the viola).
- R8 [MUST] Double-bass: written E2 A2 D3 G3 (tuned in fourths); **sounds an octave lower**: E1 A1 D2 G2 =
  MIDI 28, 33, 38, 43. Lowest standard note E1 = 28.
- Physics (concepts): pitch rises with tension; heavier strings vibrate slower, so lower strings are thicker
  and wound with fine copper, silver or aluminium wire over gut or steel; halving the sounding length raises
  the pitch an octave. [era: gut/steel strings, 1955]

**Agent applicability**
- Hard lower limits for MIDI: VLN ≥ 55, VLA ≥ 48, VC ≥ 36, DB ≥ 28 (concert). Notes below these are impossible
  (no extension/scordatura assumed in this range of the book).
- Write bass parts at concert pitch in MIDI; if a bass "line" is copied from a cello part an octave lower,
  check it does not drop below 28.

## 1.3 Fingering and positions (digitación, posiciones) (printed p.4–6, Fig. 2–4)

**Concepts**
- Stopping a string against the fingerboard shortens its sounding length (finger → bridge) and raises pitch.
- Octave of an open string = halfway nut–bridge; the next octave = three-quarters of the length from the nut
  (Fig. 3: 'cello C string, open C2 → C3 at the midpoint → C4 at 3/4).
- A given interval covers less string the closer the hand is to the bridge: equal intervals take proportional,
  not equal, distances. A major second above the open string ≈ 1¼ in. on violin, ≈ 1½ in. on viola,
  ≈ 2⅝ in. on 'cello, ≈ 4¼ in. on bass; in a very high viola position < ½ in.
- In one hand position the fingers can stop notes on all four strings without moving the hand.
- Position (*posición*): numbered by the diatonic step of the first finger above the open string. The
  index finger is "1st finger" (thumb not counted), in strings and winds alike.
- Fig. 4 (violin, A string): 1st position = open A4, fingers 1–4 B4 C5 D5 E5; 2nd position = fingers 1–4
  from C5 to F5; 3rd position = fingers 1–4 from D5 to G5.

**Rules**
- R9 [PREFER] Figures that stay within one hand position (spanning the four strings) are easiest; large leaps
  along one string need shifts. (inferred from the "fingers reach all four strings" statement)
- R10 [AVOID] Wide stretches on the 'cello and especially the bass: the same interval needs much more string
  length (a whole tone ≈ 4¼ in. on bass vs 1¼ in. on violin). (inferred) Fast chromatic/diatonic runs are
  physically larger moves on the low strings.

**Agent applicability**
- Expect high-position violin/viola notes to be finger-close (fast, agile passages up high are feasible);
  on bass, keep fast passagework simpler and stepwise.
- Fingering systems per instrument come in later chapters (not this range).

## 1.4 Double-stops and chords (dobles cuerdas, acordes) (printed p.6, Fig. 5)

**Concepts**
- Double-stop (*doble cuerda*): two notes stopped on **adjacent** strings and bowed together. Triple- and
  quadruple-stops (chords) are possible, but three or four notes cannot be sustained together by the bow.
- Fig. 5 shows violin double-stops and 3–4-note chords with fingerings (open strings and 1–4 fingers);
  exact notes partly [?] in the scan.

**Rules**
- R11 [MUST] A double-stop's two notes must lie on two adjacent strings.
- R12 [NEVER] Write a sustained 3- or 4-note chord for one string player; three/four-note chords are struck
  (broken/arpeggiated quickly), only the top two (or one) can be held. (Piston: "cannot be prolonged by the bow")

**Agent applicability**
- For a single solo string track: sustained notes ≤ 2 simultaneous pitches on adjacent strings; 3–4-note
  chords only as short accented hits (short note values), optionally rolled by a few ms low→high.
- For section patches, write chords as divisi (next section) instead of multi-stops when they must sustain.

**Tags:** #orchestration/strings #technique/double-stops

## 1.5 Divided strings (divisi) (printed p.6–7)

**Concepts**
- *divisi* / *div.* (Fr. *divisés*, Ger. *geteilt*): double notes split between the two players of a desk;
  the **right-hand (outside) player takes the upper note**. Return marked *unisono* / *unis.* (Fr. *unis*,
  Ger. *nicht geteilt*).
- *non div.* (Ger. scores: *Doppelgriff*): every player plays all notes as double-stops; written only where
  there is more than one note, to prevent dividing.
- *div. in 3* (Fr. *div. à 3*, Ger. *dreifach geteilt*): more than two parts; requires marking which player
  takes which part; often written on separate staves for legibility.
- Division by desks (It. *da leggìi*, Fr. *par pupitres*, Ger. *pultweise*): odd-numbered desks take the upper
  part — better for complex parts.
- *half* (It. *la metà*, Fr. *la moitié*, Ger. *die Hälfte*): only half the section plays (left/inside player at
  each desk rests) until *all* (It. *tutti*, Fr. *tous*, Ger. *alle*).

**Rules**
- R13 [PREFER] Simultaneous notes in a section part = divisi by default; mark *non div.* only if you want
  double-stops from everyone (heavier, more biting sound, e.g. Ex. 8).
- R14 [PREFER] Complex divided parts: divide by desks, not within the desk.
- R15 [ALLOWED] Use *half* section for a thinner, more transparent sound without changing the line.

**Agent applicability**
- Divisi halves the players per note: a div. a 2 chord in 1st violins = two lines of ~8 players each; balance
  each divisi line lower (CC1/CC11) than a unison line, or use a smaller-ensemble patch.
- *non div.* (Ex. 8) = full section on every note of the chord: the strongest string attack available.

**Tags:** #orchestration/strings #technique/divisi

## 1.6 Vibrato (printed p.7)

**Concepts**
- Left-hand vibrato: hand/wrist/arm impulses giving an almost imperceptible pitch oscillation; gives life and
  warmth.

**Rules**
- R16 [MUST] Vibrato is the default on all stopped notes except short ones; it needs no marking.
- R17 [ALLOWED] *senza vibrato* for the special, colder tone quality obtainable only by suppressing vibrato
  (must be marked).

**Agent applicability**
- Albion longs: keep CC21 (vibrato) up on sustained notes by default; drop it to minimum for an explicit
  "senza vibrato"/glassy effect. Shorts need no vibrato control.

## 1.7 Bowing basics (arco, golpes de arco) (printed p.7–8, Fig. 6)

**Concepts**
- Bow: Pernambuco stick curved toward the hair, tensioned by the screw at the heel (frog); horsehair
  (over a hundred hairs), rosined. Nylon "tried in recent times". [era: 1955]
- Contact point: normally about halfway between bridge and end of fingerboard. **Nearer the bridge** = louder,
  more brilliant, and the normal place for high notes; **nearer the fingerboard** = softer.
- Hair flat (all hairs) or tilted on edge (normal; lets the player control hair amount). Violin/viola tilt
  away from the player, 'cello/bass toward.
- Upper part of the bow = near the point; lower part = near the heel.
- Down-bow (Fr. *tiré*, sign ⊓) = heel → point; up-bow (Fr. *poussé*, sign V) = point → heel.

**Agent applicability**
- High, loud string writing is naturally brighter (bow near bridge); soft passages near the fingerboard
  darken — when a library has "sul tasto"/"flautando" patches, they fit soft, veiled passages.

**Tags:** #orchestration/strings #technique/bowing

## 1.8 Legato bowing (printed p.8–11, Ex. 1–4, Fig. 7–8)

**Rules**
- R18 [MUST] With no slur, **each note takes a new bow direction**; a good player changes bow with practically
  no break in tone, so unslurred long notes can still sound connected (Ex. 1).
- R19 [MUST] All notes under one slur are played in one bow (no change of direction). Slur = bow phrase,
  not necessarily the musical phrase.
- R20 [PREFER] Up-bow for upbeats (anacrusis), down-bow for downbeats (Ex. 1, Ex. 2). Not applicable to every
  note, but a constant principle of good bowing.
- R21 [PREFER] A vigorous attack on a long note followed by a down-bow chord/accent: start down-bow, change
  inaudibly to up-bow during the note, so both attacks get the stronger down-bow (Ex. 3, marked ⊓V).
- R22 [PREFER] Natural tendency: **up-bow → crescendo**, **down-bow → diminuendo** (leverage of the bow).
  Plan bowings so these help the intended dynamics.
- R23 [AVOID] Unequal note values in alternating down/up bows (e.g. 4 sixteenths down, 2 up): the bow must move
  at uneven speed and the shorter bow strokes sound louder (Fig. 7: 'cello Adagio 6/8, beats 3 and 6 pop out).
- R24 [PREFER] Balance slurs so up and down bows carry equal quantity and rate of motion (Fig. 8: regrouped
  slurs give an even tone).
- R25 [ALLOWED] Any melody can be bowed effectively in many ways; bow changes are chosen by intensity, dynamic,
  tempo, bow length, fingering. Concertmasters disagree and re-mark even Beethoven.

**Examples**
- Ex. 1 Franck, *Symphony* (Allegro non troppo): VN I+II unison, *ff sostenuto*, half/quarter notes unslurred
  — separate bows per note with continuous tone; downbeats suggest down-bow.
- Ex. 2 Schubert, *Symphony no. 5* (Andante con moto, 6/8, E♭): VN I *p*, slurred two-/three-note groups starting
  on an upbeat — begins up-bow.
- Ex. 3 Beethoven, *Coriolanus Overture* (Allegro con brio): VN I *ff* tied long note marked ⊓V, then a
  fermata chord down-bow — the hidden bow change of R21.
- Fig. 7/8 'cello Adagio 6/8 sixteenth arpeggios: uneven vs balanced slurring (R23/R24).
- Ex. 4 Beethoven, *Symphony no. 4* (Adagio, 3/4, E♭): VN I *p cantabile* long slurs of varied length with
  *cresc.*/*sf* — legato phrasing where bowing choices are debatable.

**Agent applicability**
- Legato patches: overlapping notes = notes under one slur. Re-attack (gap or non-overlap) = bow change; for
  sustained lines without slurs, keep notes nearly touching so the bow change sounds seamless (R18).
- Upbeat figures can carry a slight CC1/CC11 rise into the downbeat (up-bow crescendo tendency).
- Avoid generating uneven accent patterns in fast slurred passages unless wanted; if you want "beats 3 and 6"
  accents, that is what the uneven bowing produces naturally.

## 1.9 Nonlegato / détaché (printed p.11–13, Ex. 5–8)

**Concepts**
- *détaché* (nonlegato): a bow change per note, with the bow changes audible but notes not separated enough to
  be called staccato. The commonest stroke. Normally in the middle or upper third of the bow.
- At the point (Fr. *à la pointe*, It. *a punta d'arco*, Ger. *an der Spitze*): extreme lightness.
- At the heel (Fr. *au talon*, It. *al tallone*, Ger. *am Frosch*): added weight.

**Rules**
- R26 [PREFER] Default stroke for unslurred moving notes = détaché: audible articulation, little gap.
- R27 [PREFER] Light, soft, fast passagework → *punta d'arco* (Ex. 6, *pp*).
- R28 [PREFER] Heavy, very loud short strokes → *am Frosch* (Ex. 7, *fff*).
- R29 [PREFER] For vigorous emphasis with **equal stress** on every note: a series of down-bows near the heel,
  lifting the bow back between notes (Ex. 8, all ⊓, *non div.*, *sempre stacc.*). Needs time between notes
  (moderate tempo).

**Examples**
- Ex. 5 Schubert, *Symphony no. 8* (Andante con moto, 3/8): viola *ff* running 32nd notes — détaché in the
  middle part of the bow.
- Ex. 6 Bartók, *Concerto for Orchestra* (Presto, 2/4): VN II div. in two staves, *pp*, chromatic sixteenths
  *punta d'arco* — light, rustling point détaché.
- Ex. 7 Strauss, *Don Quixote* (♩=116): VN I div., *fff am Frosch*, short sixteenth+eighth hits alternating
  between the halves — heavy heel strokes.
- Ex. 8 Stravinsky, *Le Sacre du Printemps* (Tempo giusto, 𝅗𝅥=50, 2/4): VN II, VA, VC, DB *non div.*
  repeated eighth-note chords, all down-bows, *f*, *sempre stacc.*, with irregular accents — the
  equal-stress down-bow series.

**Agent applicability**
- Détaché ≈ a "sustain"/"détaché"/"short-legato" patch with re-triggered notes; give each note ~85–95% of its
  slot (inferred), velocity/CC1 even.
- Ex. 8 type ostinato: short marcato/staccato patch, all notes same high velocity (no strong/weak beat
  variation), accents only where marked; full section on every note.
- Punta d'arco = soft, light shorts or détaché at low dynamics; am Frosch = strongest shorts (highest velocity
  layer).

**Tags:** #orchestration/strings #technique/bowing #articulation/detache

## 1.10 Louré (portato) (printed p.13–14, Ex. 9)

**Concepts**
- *louré*: mix of legato and nonlegato — a dash on each note under a slur; notes in one bow with a gentle break
  between them, made by first-finger pressure on the stick and a slight wrist slowing of the bow.

**Examples**
- Ex. 9 Sibelius, *Symphony no. 2* (Allegretto, 6/4): full strings (VN I, VN II, VA, VC div., DB) *mf*, repeated
  quarter-note chords in louré with hairpins — pulsing, soft-edged accompaniment.

**Agent applicability**
- Louré on a legato patch: separate notes by a tiny gap or dip CC11 slightly (≈ a few units) at each note start
  within one phrase curve (inferred); on samples, a "portato" articulation if available. Repeated-chord
  accompaniment in louré is a classic soft pulsing string bed.

## 1.11 Staccato: off-string and on-string (printed p.14–19, Ex. 10–17)

**Concepts**
- Two families: **off the string** and **on the string**.
- *spiccato* / *saltando* (Sp. *saltado*) / off-the-string staccato: in moderate tempo and *p*–*mf*, the bow is
  dropped in a semicircular motion at the middle of the bow and rebounds, partly lifted by the wrist.
  Terminology is disputed; the composer only writes **dots** + dynamics + tempo and players choose the stroke.
- Fast staccato: no lifting; short wrist strokes in the middle of the bow make the stick bounce and the hair
  springs off the string each stroke (Ex. 11).
- *jeté* (thrown): near the point, the bow dropped with a slight down-bow motion and allowed to bounce on its
  own; groups of **2–6 notes**; written as dots under a slur, sometimes with *saltando*/*saltato*.
- *martellato* (Fr. *martelé*, hammered): on-string staccato; very swift stroke, abrupt start and stop, the bow
  stays on the string; each note like an "oblong block with square ends", at *p* or *f*. Usually upper half;
  at the heel for extra vigour.
- Dots under a slur: either bouncing (usually up-bow, Ex. 14) or **slurred staccato** (bow held on the string,
  sharp stops made by the wrist, Ex. 15).
- Dotted rhythm (dotted eighth + sixteenth) in fast tempo is usually played as slurred staccato even when not
  marked; notation = slur with a dot on the sixteenth, but **the first (long) note is the one shortened**.
  Same for quarter + eighth in 6/8.

**Rules**
- R30 [MUST] Mark staccato with dots plus dynamic and tempo; let these define spiccato vs on-string. Agent
  equivalent: choose the patch from tempo + dynamic (below).
- R31 [PREFER] Moderate tempo, *p*–*mf*, dotted notes → spiccato/off-string (Ex. 10).
- R32 [PREFER] Fast tempo, light dynamic, running staccato → bouncing wrist stroke in mid-bow (Ex. 11).
- R33 [PREFER] Hard, square, loud (*ff marcatissimo*) staccato → martellato, on string (Ex. 13); heel for most
  vigour.
- R34 [ALLOWED] Groups of 2–6 quick repeated/ricochet notes as one thrown stroke (jeté/saltato, Ex. 12).
- R35 [PREFER] Fast dotted-eighth+sixteenth figures: shorten the dotted note (gap before the sixteenth),
  sixteenth tied in the same bow direction (Ex. 16).
- R36 [PREFER] For a light, crisp dotted rhythm: at the point of the bow with up-bow on the beats (Ex. 17).
- R37 [PREFER] Practice imagining the bowing of every string phrase; printed bowings are often editorial and
  not followed literally (composer's markings show intent, performers decide).

**Examples**
- Ex. 10 Beethoven, *Symphony no. 1* (Andante con moto, 3/8, F): VN I *p*, dotted sixteenth figures
  — spiccato in moderate tempo.
- Ex. 11 Rossini, *Overture to William Tell* (Allegro vivace, 2/4, E): VN I *p* running dotted sixteenths
  — fast bouncing staccato from the wrist.
- Ex. 12 Rimsky-Korsakov, *Capriccio Espagnol* (𝅗𝅥=66, 3/4, A): VN I, VN II, VA, VC *saltato* triplet eighths
  (dots under slur) ending on an accented note, *mf*; DB *pizz. p* on beats — thrown/ricochet strokes over a
  pizzicato bass.
- Ex. 13 Bruckner, *Symphony no. 9* (Vivace, 3/4): VN I, VN II (div. then unis.), VA *ff marcatissimo*,
  wide-leaping dotted notes; viola repeated eighths *ff* before — martellato.
- Ex. 14 Mahler, *Symphony no. 4* (Moderato, 4/4): VA, VC, DB *pp legg.*, dotted slurred sixteenth groups,
  bowings ⊓/V marked — bouncing up-bow staccato in the low strings.
- Ex. 15 Stravinsky, *Orpheus* (Andante sostenuto, ♪=96, 2/4): VN I, VN II, VA *p à la pointe*, slurred dotted
  sixteenths — slurred (on-string) staccato at the point.
- Ex. 16 Berlioz, *Fantastic Symphony* (Allegretto non troppo, 𝅗𝅥=72; likely "March to the Scaffold",
  inferred): violins *ff* dotted eighth+sixteenth, bowings ⊓⊓VV ⊓⊓VV *simile* — hooked dotted rhythm.
- Ex. 17 Weber, *Euryanthe Overture* (Allegro marcato, 𝅗𝅥=88): 'cello *pp* dotted rhythms with ⊓/V marked,
  ending *mf* accented halves — crisp point bowing with up-bow on beats.

**Agent applicability**
- Patch choice by tempo/dynamic (Albion names): moderate *p–mf* dotted notes → "Short Spiccato"; fast light
  running notes → spiccato with short lengths (≈ 40–50% of slot, inferred); *f–ff* hammered → "Short Staccato
  Combo"/marcato with high velocity; ricochet groups of 2–6 → spiccato notes with slight decreasing velocity
  (inferred).
- Dotted rhythms at speed: shorten the dotted note (end it ~ one sixteenth-triplet early, inferred) and play the
  sixteenth slightly lighter, leading into the next beat.
- Pizzicato bass under saltato upper strings (Ex. 12) is a ready-made light-tutti texture.

## 1.12 Accents (acentos) (printed p.19 — continues beyond range)

**Concepts**
- Accents are made mainly by the bow, but also by the left hand: sudden quickening of vibrato, a more forceful
  finger stroke, or both — depending on whether the accented note is made by lifting a finger or dropping it
  onto the string (Fig. 9a/9b, p.20, not in range).

**Rules**
- R38 [PREFER] In vigorous rhythmic passages, left-hand and bow accents are combined (nearly always).

**Agent applicability**
- Accented notes on sustained patches: raise velocity (attack) and add a short CC21/CC1 bump at the onset
  (inferred), not only velocity.

**Tags:** #orchestration/strings #articulation/accents
