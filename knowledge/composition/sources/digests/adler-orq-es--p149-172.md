## Contrapuntal string writing — repertoire examples (scan p.149–155, printed p.136–142)

Context from p.135 (just before this range): when writing counterpoint for strings, clarity and balance come from
(1) giving the main line the best register of its instrument, (2) thinning the counterpoint so the subject emerges,
(3) separating registers of subject and countersubjects, (4) making the rhythm of each counterpoint clearly different.

**Rules**
- R1 [PREFER] Make each contrapuntal element rhythmically distinctive (its own figure: running 16ths vs. accented
  quarters vs. held notes) so that it stays audible inside the homogeneous string colour. *Why:* in a one-colour
  ensemble, rhythm is the main separator (Vivaldi, Bach, Weber, Bartók examples all show this).
- R2 [PREFER] An anacrusis (upbeat) opening gives a fugue subject extra thrust to stand out against active
  counterpoint (Vivaldi).
- R3 [ALLOWED] Alternate freely between contrapuntal and homophonic passages; a homophonic climax after a contrapuntal
  stretch gives strong contrast (Bach, Weber).
- R4 [PREFER] Low-to-high fugal entry order (vc/cb → vla → vln 2 → vln 1) is the traditional Baroque layout
  [era: Baroque concerto grosso].
- R5 [ALLOWED] Very similar, rhythmically undefined lines (mostly stepwise, legato, constant eighths) produce a
  calm, contemplative haze in which entries of the subject are still heard (Bartók); use when you want tension
  without rhythmic profile.
- R6 [ALLOWED] Mutes on for a soft fugal opening, removed (senza sord.) as each section enters toward the climax
  (Weber, Bartók).
- R7 [ALLOWED] Baroque writers worked in a much narrower register than today, so highlighting individual parts within
  the homogeneous group was harder [era: Baroque]; emphasis came from rhythm and texture, not from register spread.

**Examples**
- Ex. 5-21 Vivaldi, *Concerto grosso* Op.3 No.11, 1st mvt, b.35–54 (D minor, Allegro): fugue exposition; accented,
  *marc.* subject enters in solo cello + continuo, then viola, then vln 2 (solo + tutti), then vln 1 (solo + tutti);
  each new entry is *f* against an already running counterpoint; figured continuo = harmony layer.
- Ex. 5-22 Bach, *Brandenburg Concerto No.3*, 1st mvt, b.76–92 (G major): 3 vln + 3 vla + 3 vc + cb (text says
  "six violins" [?]); dense counterpoint where rhythmic character pushes single voices forward; violas often in
  rhythmic unison; vc 1–3 + cb in unison as the bass; b.87–90 move into a rich homophonic climax (all upper parts in
  running 16ths), then b.91 drops to *p* repeated-chord eighths.
- Ex. 5-23 Weber, *Euryanthe* Overture, b.144–159 (Tempo I assai moderato, quarter = 88): Romantic string fugue;
  subject in vc + cb unison with accents; vln 2 and vla enter *pp senza sord.*, vln 1 enters *mf*; ends in *ff*
  with triplet figures — elements separated rhythmically, alternation of counterpoint and homophony.
- Ex. 5-24 Bartók, *Music for Strings, Percussion and Celesta*, 1st mvt, b.1–34 (Andante tranquillo, eighth ≈
  116–112): fugue in shifting eighth-note meters (8/8, 12/8, 7/8, 6/8, 10/8…); strings in divided pairs (Vla 1,2;
  Vln 3,4; Vc 1,2; Cb 1,2; later Vln 1, Vln 2); every entry *con sord. pp*; constant eighth motion created by
  intersecting lines; imitation (free and canonic) builds tension that does not drop until the timpani enter at b.34.

**Agent applicability**
- For a string fugato: give the subject a rhythm the countersubjects never use; start entries low and climb; keep
  the countersubject in a different register band from the subject.
- In MIDI: subject entries one dynamic level above the running counterpoint (CC1/velocity +10–15) to mimic the
  *f* entry over *p* texture (inferred).

## Escritura homófona para cuerda — chord spacing (scan p.156–157, printed p.143–144)

**Concepts**
- Distribution (distribución) of chord tones: in strings the colour is so uniform that spacing, register and
  melodic considerations decide who plays which note.
- Harmonic-series spacing (serie armónica, Ex. 5-25 shows the series on E): wide intervals at the bottom (octave,
  fifth), close intervals at the top.

**Rules**
- R8 [PREFER] Space a full string chord like the harmonic series: wider gaps between the low instruments, closer
  gaps between the high ones. *Why:* the low partials are farther apart; mimicking that sounds resonant.
- R9 [PREFER] Traditional order in a tutti chord, low to high: cb – vc – vla – vln 2 – vln 1, each section in its
  usual place (Ex. 5-26, E major: top three notes close in the violins, octave/fifth gaps in vc/cb).
- R10 [ALLOWED] Putting the cb an octave lower (Ex. 5-26, first version) and spreading the top wider makes the chord
  slightly darker; both versions work *p* or *f*; the difference is small.
- R11 [PREFER] Close (narrow) spacing gives more options: the same 4-part progression (Ex. 5-27, F major, close
  4-part chords with tenor–soprano within about an octave, approx. I – IV | V – V7 | I (inferred)) can be scored in
  at least six ways with different colours:
  1. Direct: vln 1 = S, vln 2 = A, vla = T, vc = B, cb doubles vc an octave lower. Vague but fine at *p*–*mf*;
     heavy at *f*, because vla/vc/cb are then in a much stronger register than the violins.
  2. Violas divisi on the two top parts, cellos divisi on the two lower: very "old", dark sound.
  3. As 2 plus cb on the bass an octave lower: line becomes much darker.
  4. Vln 1 on soprano, vln 2 (or violas divisi) on alto and tenor: slightly brighter but still subdued.
  5. All cellos, with cb doubling the bass line [same written pitch, i.e. sounding an octave lower]: very intense.
  6. Transpose the whole progression up one octave, then two, without cb: truly luminous.
- R12 [AVOID] In first-inversion chords, do not double the bass (the chord third) in a 4-voice texture, except when
  the root is scale degree 1, 2, 4 or 5 (VI6, VII6, II6, III6).
- R13 [MUST] For I6, IV6, V6 with more than four voices the third will be doubled somewhere: double it close to the
  bass (e.g. bass in octaves), keep the upper voices on root and fifth. *Why:* keeps the "open" sound typical of the
  inversion (Ex. 5-28, A major 6-chords: "advisable" = C# only in the bass octave; "not advisable" = C# also in two or
  three upper voices, which weakens the first-inversion effect).

**Agent applicability**
- When voicing a string pad: check bass-to-next-voice ≥ P5 (7) and top-voice gaps ≤ M3–P4 (4–5) where possible
  (inferred from R8).
- Choose the scoring of a hymn-like progression by target colour: dark = violas/cellos divisi (+ cb 8vb); bright =
  move up an octave or two and drop cb; intense = cellos + cb on the bass.
- In 6-chords count how many voices carry the third; if > 1 outside the bass octave, re-voice.

## Scoring a melody in the string section (scan p.157–158, printed p.144–145)

Ex. 5-29 is a simple G-major tune (range D4–C5, 62–72). Ways to reinforce it:

**Rules**
- R14 [ALLOWED] Vln 1 alone, unaccompanied.
- R15 [ALLOWED] Vln 2 plays it; vln 1 doubles an octave higher.
- R16 [ALLOWED] Unison doublings: vln 1+2; vlns + vlas; vlns + vcs; vlas + vcs.
- R17 [ALLOWED] Violin *sul G* (on the G string) for a darker, warmer colour.
- R18 [PREFER] For this register (D4–C5) a solo viola or cello is more intense than the violins (it lies high in
  their range, low in the violin's).
- R19 [ALLOWED] Spread over four or five octaves, played by all five string sections.
- R20 [ALLOWED] Unison of all violins, violas and cellos (strongest single-line unison).

**Agent applicability**
- For a melody in the 62–72 band: for intensity put it on viola/cello (or vln sul G); for weight, unison vln+vla+vc;
  for brilliance, transpose and double in octaves.

## Tchaikovsky, Serenade for Strings — homophonic model (scan p.158–160, printed p.145–147)

Ex. 5-30, 1st mvt opening (labelled b.1–3 but printed b.1–~33; C major, Andante non troppo, eighth = 126, 6/8,
*f sempre marcatissimo*, rehearsal letters A–D).

**Rules**
- R21 [PREFER] In rich chordal string writing, divide vln 2, vla and vc, but keep vln 1 *non divisi*. *Why:*
  dividing would weaken the leading violin line. (Double stops non divisi are possible in all sections.)
- R22 [PREFER] Always reinforce the vln 1 melody notes: either vln 1 double stops or another section doubling it
  (b.1–2 vln 2 lower notes double; b.4–5 viola doubles).
- R23 [ALLOWED] To bring the harmony forward instead of the tune (b.3 and 6), use phrasing and cross vln 2 inside
  the vln 1 notes, and reinforce vln 2 with vla and vc doublings.
- R24 [PREFER] A melody note in the same pitch is much stronger played by vln 1 than by vln 2; crossing vla and
  vln 2 around it (b.8, E4 = 64) emphasises it.
- R25 [ALLOWED] Melody restated in the low register *ff marcatissimo* (vc + cb in octaves, cb fairly high = very
  intense) works if the accompaniment is thin and in a non-brilliant register (vlns/vlas in sustained middle-register
  chords), so it cannot cover the tune (letter B).
- R26 [PREFER] A big full chord right after pale, restrained harmony sounds clamorous (b.15) — contrast of density
  is the effect.
- R27 [PREFER] Give the cb a single, well-placed note (the tonic) rather than a chord: any cb chord would need
  divisi. The low C2 (36) on the cello is sonorous enough to be the real bass of a big chord.
- R28 [PREFER] Choose chord distributions with no awkward fingering for any instrument (b.15 chord as model).
- R29 [ALLOWED] A diminuendo can be written into the orchestration itself: letter C → D, close spacing (cellos
  high) gives a tense version; from b.18 the sound becomes singing and homogeneous, like one instrument.
- R30 [PREFER] For a fading ending: put every instrument in a register easy to play softly, repeat a cadential
  formula (three times here), and drop the bass on each tonic arrival (the text names the tonic as E [?]).
- R31 [ALLOWED] Cb re-bowing a held note (no slur, bow change, b.32–33) lets it sound slightly longer, which makes
  its later disappearance more noticeable while the other strings sustain.

**Agent applicability**
- MIDI: keep vln 1 as one monophonic or double-stop track; split vln 2/vla/vc into divisi tracks for chords.
- When the tune drops to vc+cb, keep the upper-string chords at least one dynamic level lower and below ~C6.
- For a written-in diminuendo, thin out and lower the voicing besides lowering CC1.

## Grieg, Peer Gynt Suite No.1, "Åse's Death" (scan p.160–161, printed p.147–148)

Ex. 5-31, b.17–24 (B minor, Adagio, 4/4, *f con sord.*, all sections divisi, vc *divisi a 3*, b.21–24 *ff* with
accents).

**Rules**
- R32 [ALLOWED] Strict homophonic string writing where every note is doubled at the octave, like organ 8' + 4'
  stops (original version doubled an octave higher).
- R33 [PREFER] To articulate two phrases inside each four-bar unit, double differently in antecedent and
  consequent: in b.19–20 and 23–24 vln 2 doubles vln 1 an octave below/above instead of adding harmony.
- R34 [PREFER] Study tip: reduce a string passage to a simple four-part piano version and compare with the original
  to see where doublings were added.

## Penderecki, Threnody to the Victims of Hiroshima (scan p.161–165, printed p.148–152)

**Concepts**
- Prototype of mid-20th-c. string writing: exact numbers of players specified (24 vln, 10 vla, 10 vc, 8 cb),
  sections often divided so each player has his own pitch; colour is the main parameter.
- Graphic notation: thick horizontal bands = cluster (cluster) with approximate register; exact pitches of each
  divisi player given below each cluster; durations in seconds (10", 6", 30").
- Notation guide (Ex. 5-32a): quarter-tone sharp / three-quarter-tone sharp / quarter-tone flat / three-quarter
  flat; highest note of the instrument (indefinite pitch); play between bridge and tailpiece; arpeggio on the four
  strings behind the bridge; bow the tailpiece (at 90°); bow on the bridge itself; percussion effect (tap the
  violin's upper plate with the frog [?] or fingertips); several irregular bow changes; *molto vibrato*; very slow
  vibrato of 1/4-tone width by sliding the finger; very fast unmeasured tremolo; ord., s.p., s.t., c.l.,
  *legno battuto*.

**Rules**
- R35 [ALLOWED] Divide sections down to single players so each holds one pitch of a chromatic or quarter-tone
  cluster [era: 1960s Polish avant-garde / sonorism].
- R36 [ALLOWED] Shape clusters with sudden dynamic changes (*ff* → *sub. pp*), vibrato types and timbre changes
  (s.p. → ord. → s.t.) instead of harmony; the final tutti cluster: *fff* diminuendo to *pppp* over 30".
- R37 [ALLOWED] Individual cellists/bassists play irregular, unsynchronised percussive bow effects (bridge, tailpiece)
  as a pointillist layer under sustained clusters.

**Agent applicability**
- Approximate a cluster in MIDI with one note per semitone across the stated band, each on its own divisi track;
  quarter tones need pitch bend per voice (±50 cents) — most sample libraries cannot do the extended effects.
- Time-based sections: convert seconds to bars at the project tempo.

## Strings accompanying a soloist (Usos de la sección de cuerda como acompañamiento de un solista) (scan p.165–170, printed p.152–157)

**Concepts**
- The homogeneous colour, technical range and dynamic range make strings the usual accompaniment for an instrumental
  or vocal soloist, a choir, or a group of instruments.

**Rules**
- R38 [ALLOWED] Simple homophonic accompaniment on and off the beat: bass on the beat, upper strings on the
  off-beats (Mendelssohn, Ex. 5-33).
- R39 [ALLOWED] Long notes carrying the harmony (vla, vc/cb) under a very active rhythmic figure in the violins
  (repeated short notes, *p*→*pp*) (Ex. 5-34).
- R40 [ALLOWED] Long notes for the harmony (sustained viola double stops) plus a slurred violin arpeggio figure
  (vln 1 and 2 in parallel triplets, *sempre legato*) and a simple bass on beats (Ex. 5-35).
- R41 [ALLOWED] Busy, imitative string counterpoint (*pp*, staccato eighths) under rapid vocal declamation to depict
  frantic stage action (Mozart, Ex. 5-36) [era: Classical opera buffa].
- R42 [PREFER] Leave the voice unobstructed: cellos carry the characteristic dance rhythm, other strings *pizz.*
  support melody and harmony softly (Bizet Habanera, Ex. 5-37).
- R43 [ALLOWED] Low melody (cellos, doubled by trumpet, clarinet, bassoon) under upper-string tremolo chords, *ff*,
  with cb *pizz.* on beats, gives an ominous tone — now a cliché (Bizet, Ex. 5-38).
- R44 [ALLOWED] For a sombre background, only the low strings accompany the melody; violas in unison with the first
  cellos; strings often double the organ (Fauré *Requiem*; example on missing pages).

**Examples**
- Ex. 5-33 Mendelssohn, *Elijah*, "It is enough", b.1–9 (Adagio, quarter = 66, 3/4): vln 1/2 and vla *p* off-beat
  eighths, cb on-beat eighths, cello *mf* obbligato melody.
- Ex. 5-34 *Elijah*, "Hear ye, Israel", b.1–7 (Adagio, eighth = 80, 3/8): violins in running repeated short notes,
  viola and vc/cb tied dotted quarters, woodwind *sf* chords, soprano solo enters b.6.
- Ex. 5-35 *Elijah*, "He, watching over Israel", b.1–5 (Allegro moderato, quarter = 126, 4/4): vln 1+2 slurred
  triplet arpeggios, vla sustained double stops, vc/cb quarters on beats 1 and 3 *p*, timp. roll *pp*, horns/winds
  sustain, chorus.
- Ex. 5-36 Mozart, *Le nozze di Figaro*, Act II No.14, b.1–13 (Allegro assai): *pp* imitative eighths in strings,
  then repeated staccato eighths under Susanna's patter.
- Ex. 5-37 Bizet, *Carmen*, Act I Habanera, b.1–15 (Allegretto quasi Andantino, 2/4): cellos alone set the habanera
  rhythm *pp possibile*; vlns/vla enter *pizz. pp* with the voice; cb tacet.
- Ex. 5-38 Bizet, *Carmen*, Prelude, b.121–131 (Andante moderato, 3/4): vln 1/2 divisi and vla tremolo chords *ff*,
  cello melody in tenor clef, cb *pizz. f* on downbeats.

**Agent applicability**
- Accompaniment templates for a vocal/solo line: (a) bass on beats + upper strings off-beat chords; (b) sustained
  harmony + violin ostinato; (c) sustained harmony + legato violin arpeggios; (d) cello rhythm ostinato + pizz.
  chords; (e) tremolo chords over a low melody for menace.
- Keep accompaniment one or two dynamic levels below the soloist and out of the soloist's register band when
  possible (inferred from R42).

## Transcribing piano music for strings (scan p.171, printed p.160 and 162; section start missing)

**Rules**
- R45 [ALLOWED] Repeated piano chords can be given to the strings as repeated notes/chords with the same effect;
  at a fast tempo they sound consistent (Schubert, Ex. 5-41).
- R46 [PREFER] Accompaniments built on repeated notes suit the strings better than the piano (text on p.162 about
  Brahms Ex. 5-43 and Saint-Saëns Ex. 5-44).

**Examples**
- Ex. 5-41 Schubert, *Rückblick*, b.1–2 (G minor, 3/4, "Nicht zu geschwind"): piano offbeat 16th chords and a
  chromatic bass are turned into two string versions: (1) violins *pizz.* on the offbeat chords, then *arco fp* in
  b.2; viola repeated eighths; cello slurred eighth bass line; cb *pizz.* doubling; (2) all *arco* with repeated
  eighths in the inner parts, cello *p* < *f*, cb only a *pizz. sf* downbeat in b.2.
- Ex. 5-42 Brahms, *An ein Veilchen*, b.1–3 (piano version shown, described as a good example of part linkage);
  string version on the missing page.
- Ex. 5-44 Saint-Saëns, *L'Attente*, b.1–3: only the heading survives.

**Agent applicability**
- When orchestrating a piano accompaniment: split piano chords into sustained or repeated string parts, give the
  bass to vc (+ cb pizz. for punctuation), and turn broken figures into either pizz. (light) or arco (sustained).
