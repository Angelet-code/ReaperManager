## 10 Otros miembros de la familia de la trompeta — la trompeta bajo (scan p.344, printed p.339)

**Concepts**
- Bass trumpet (trompeta bajo): really a valved trombone (trombón con válvulas). Trombonists usually play it,
  with a trombone mouthpiece.
- Available in C, Bb, D and Eb. Transpositions (sounds below written):
  - in C: an octave lower (12 semitones);
  - in Bb: a major 9th lower (14 semitones);
  - in D: a minor 7th lower (10 semitones);
  - in Eb: a major 6th lower (9 semitones).
- Ranges, Ex. 10-61, written in treble clef. The written top is C6 (84) in every key. The written bottom is F#3 (54)
  for C and Bb, and F3 (53) `[?]` for Eb and D (accidental hard to read). Converted to sounding pitch:
  - C: F#2–C5 (42–72);
  - Bb: E2–Bb4 (40–70);
  - Eb: Ab2–Eb5 (44–75);
  - D: G2–D5 (43–74).

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] Write bass-trumpet parts in treble clef, transposed by the instrument's key (see the list above).
  Its MIDI notes are concert pitch: a Bb bass trumpet part sits 14 semitones below its written notes.
- R2 [PREFER] Treat its usable concert range as roughly a tenor trombone's middle and upper register
  (about E2/G2 to C5/Eb5, depending on the key). *(inferred from the ranges above)*

**Examples**
- Ex. 10-62 Wagner, *Die Walküre* Act III, mm.12–18: bass trumpet in D, Allegro 6/8, f. Accented dotted heroic
  figures (the Ride motive) on a brass bass-trumpet line.
- Ex. 10-63 Wagner, *Die Walküre* Act III, Wotan's aria "Denn Einer nur freie die Braut", mm.1–4: bass trumpet in D,
  Andante, p, lyrical legato with hairpins. Shows that it can also sing softly.

**Agent applicability**
- To evoke a Wagnerian brass sound, a trombone patch playing a heroic melody in the G2–D5 band can stand in for a
  bass trumpet. *(inferred)*

## El fiscorno (flugelhorn) (scan p.345, printed p.340)

**Concepts**
- Flugelhorn (fiscorno): a member of the bugle/cornet family (clarín y corneta). The name comes from
  *Flügelmann*, the soldier who marched at the front right of German military-band formations.
- History: the first flugelhorns were made in Austria in 1820–1830. They had a fully conical bore and a medium bell,
  taken from their predecessor, the keyed bugle (bugle con llaves). Bores have narrowed since, so today the only
  difference from the cornet is the flugelhorn's larger bell.
- The Bb flugelhorn is the most used. There are also flugelhorns in Eb, F and C. The modern instrument has 3 valves
  and a funnel-shaped mouthpiece, wider and deeper than a cornet's.
- It has the same range and transposition as the cornet: it sounds a major 2nd (2 semitones) lower than written.
- Range, Ex. 10-64: written F#3–C6 (54–84), optional D6. That sounds E3–Bb5 (52–82), optional C6 (84).
- Sound: very sweet and opulent (muy dulce y opulento), closer to the horn than even the cornet is.

**Rules**
- R3 [ALLOWED] Flugelhorns are rare in the symphony orchestra but fairly common in bands, wind ensembles and jazz
  groups. `[era: late-20th-c. practice]`
- R4 [ALLOWED] Use the flugelhorn for a horn-like, sweet solo in the trumpet register.
  `[era: early 20th c.]` It sometimes played the high horn solo in the "Quoniam" of Bach's *Mass in B minor*.

**Examples**
- Ex. 10-65 Stravinsky, *Threni* (*Trenni*), mm.89–99: flugelhorn solo, "Quite slowly", mf accompanied. A legato
  line through changing meters (3/8, 2/8, 3/4, 6/8). The book calls it the famous orchestral flugelhorn passage.

## El trombón — introduction and family (scans p.345–346, printed p.340–341)

**Concepts**
- Trombone (trombón; trombone It./Fr./Eng., Posaune Ger.).
- Origin: essentially a large trumpet. One predecessor may have been the bass trumpet, whose long tubing was either
  coiled or partly removed. Removing tubing led to the trombone: the tube is cut into two U-shaped pieces, one
  sliding inside the other. Moving the outer tube, the slide (vara), with the arm changes pitch and adjusts tuning.
- Bore: cylindrical for about two-thirds of its length, then conical to the bell. The cone is small in alto and
  tenor trombones and much wider in bass trombones, which makes the lowest harmonics easier.
- The family illustration shows the alto trombone, the tenor trombone, the tenor with F attachment (transpositor
  en FA), the bass with F attachment, and the bass with "E and F" attachments (as printed: "MI y FA").
- Today's orchestra uses three trombone types: tenor, bass and alto. All are **non-transposing**.
- Many other trombones exist, some of them transposing. Some are still used in ensembles such as British brass
  bands, which also use transposing tubas.

**Rules**
- R5 [ALLOWED] The trombone is very versatile. It can play solos, or give a warm harmonic background (fondo
  armónico cálido).
- R6 [ALLOWED] It works as a contrapuntal part, with other trombones or with instruments of other sections.
- R7 [ALLOWED] It is an effective doubler, with a sweet timbre and a wide dynamic range.
- R8 [ALLOWED] It can play every articulation effectively, including large leaps.
- R9 [PREFER] `[era: c.1900–2000 orchestral practice]` Use a standard section of 3 trombones: 2 tenors + 1 bass.
- R10 [ALLOWED] `[era: 19th c. scoring / historically informed revival]` 19th-century alto trombone parts were played on
  tenor for most of the 20th c. Lately alto trombones are returning to the parts first written for them.

**Examples** (orchestral layouts for one, two or more trombones, various articulations; printed p.346)
- Tail of an unidentified tenor-clef trombone example `[?]` (title on a missing page), mm.10–17, Bb key signature, p:
  a long slurred lyric solo line in the tenor register.
- Ex. 10-77 Beethoven, *Symphony No. 9* IV, "Seid umschlungen", mm.1–8: a single bass trombone, Andante maestoso 3/2,
  ff with sfz. It carries the male-chorus melody in unison, as a solo line of block half and whole notes.
- Ex. 10-78 Berlioz, *Rákóczy March*, mm.96–105: three trombones in unison, Allegro, ff. A fast eighth-note melodic
  line; shows unison trombones as a powerful melodic voice.
- Ex. 10-79 Brahms, *Symphony No. 1* IV, mm.47–51: three-trombone chorale, Più allegro, p dolce. Upper two parts in
  alto clef, bass trombone in bass clef. Soft sustained harmony with smooth voice leading.
- Additional study passages (three trombones), pointer only: Bach *Cantata 118*; Schumann *Symphony 3* IV;
  R. Strauss *Till Eulenspiegel*; Wagner *Tannhäuser* Overture.

**Agent applicability**
- Default trombone section: TBN 1, TBN 2 (tenor) and BTBN (bass). Write all three at concert pitch (no transposition).
- For a soft brass chorale, use p dolce, legato, in close position, with the bass trombone on the root.
  This follows the Brahms model.

## Trombón con sordina (muted trombone) (scan p.348, printed p.347)

**Concepts**
- Trombone mute types were covered in ch.9 (printed pp.307–311), which is not in this range. Like horn and trumpet
  mutes, they change the timbre and let the player play very softly.

**Rules**
- R11 [ALLOWED] Use mutes for color and for very soft trombone playing (pp).

**Examples**
- Ex. 10-80 Sessions, *Symphony No. 2* IV, mm.68–70: 3 trombones con sordini, Allegro. Accented chromatic close-voiced
  chords in eighths, p dim., then detached staccato chords down to pp.
- Ex. 10-81 Berg, *Violin Concerto* I, mm.45–51: tenor and bass trombones "m. Dpf." (mit Dämpfer = muted), Slowly.
  Triplet figures, p < poco, mp, fp. Cellos and basses col legno double the trombones; the doubling is inaudible on
  the recording.

## Glissandos (scans p.348–349, printed p.347–348)

**Concepts**
- Slide glissando (glissando de vara): the most natural trombone gliss. The slide moves quickly between two or more
  notes, like a string player sliding a finger along a string.
- Lip glissando (glissando de labio), and combinations of slide and lip glissando.

**Rules**
- R12 [NEVER] Never write a single slide glissando wider than a **tritone (6 semitones)**. That interval is what one
  harmonic covers across all the slide positions, from 1st to 7th. *(The 7-position count is general knowledge, not
  stated on these pages.)*
- R13 [ALLOWED] For a wider glissando, use harmonic-series (lip) glissandos within one slide position. This works like
  a horn gliss over the harmonic series, as in Bartók below.
- R14 [PREFER] Trombone glissandos are uncommon, so they are very effective. Save them for special moments.

**Examples**
- Ex. 10-82 Khachaturian, *Gayane* Suite No. 1, "Sabre Dance", mm.10–11: trombone, Presto, f. A short sliding
  gliss into a held note, repeated: the classic comic/brash slide.
- Ex. 10-83 Britten, *Young Person's Guide to the Orchestra*, fugue at [L]: trombone, Allegro molto 2/4. Staccato line
  where "⊕" marks notes played quasi glissando. A less obvious slide that gives a jazzy feeling.
- Ex. 10-84 Bartók, *Violin Concerto No. 2* III, mm.593–600: 2 tenor + bass trombones, dotted half = 56. Each part
  plays quintuplet arpeggio glissandos up and down a harmonic series in a fixed slide position, marked
  "I pos.", "III", "IV pos.", "V", "VI". Parts enter staggered, f, cresc. to ff, "senza sord.".

**Agent applicability**
- Render a slide gliss as a pitch-bend ramp of ≤ 6 semitones, or as a legato/portamento patch between two notes.
- Render a wide trombone sweep as a fast arpeggio up a harmonic series: fundamental, octave, 5th, octave, 3rd, 5th…
  on a fixed root. *(inferred)*

## Otros miembros de la familia del trombón (scan p.350, printed p.349)

**Concepts**
- Contrabass trombone (trombón contrabajo): in Bb, an octave below the tenor trombone. Few composers used it:
  Wagner, Strauss, Schoenberg, and Verdi, especially in *Falstaff*. In Italy the contrabass trombone was a valved
  instrument.
- Valve trombone (trombón de válvulas o pistones): valves instead of a slide. Little impact in the orchestra, some
  success in bands. It is really a large trumpet.

**Rules**
- R15 [AVOID] `[era: Adler 2002 view]` Do not write for the contrabass trombone: it is very demanding for the player.
  Parts originally written for it are played on tuba today.
- R16 [AVOID] Do not write for the valve trombone either. It gains facility and agility but loses the trombone's
  character, and very few orchestral players own one.

## La tuba — role, history, construction (scans p.350–351, printed p.349–350)

**Concepts**
- Tuba: the true bass of the brass section, ranked alongside the double bass and the contrabassoon.
- History, as given by the book:
  - The tuba as we know it entered the symphony orchestra only around 1875, when Wagner conceived it and wrote for it.
  - The Wagner tuba was horn-shaped and sounded much like a horn, but much lower, giving bass support to trumpets
    and trombones.
  - It replaced the ophicleide, which had been used until the early 19th c.
  - In the *Ring* Wagner wrote for a whole family: tenor tubas in Bb and Eb, and bass tubas in BBb ("doble Sib"), an
    octave below the Bb tenor tuba. This is as printed; it looks garbled `[?]`.
  - These were extensions of the horn family, usually played by horn players.
  - Besides Wagner, Bruckner and Richard Strauss adopted them, but few others did. Gradually the **bass tuba in F**
    became the standard orchestral tuba.
  `[era: late-19th-c. Wagnerian brass]`
- Today a tubist can choose among 5–6 tubas:
  - tenor tuba in Bb;
  - bass tubas in F, Eb and C;
  - contrabass tubas in CC and BBb.
- Unlike Wagner tubas, **no modern tuba transposes**. The key names only refer to the instrument's range, fundamental
  and pedal notes.
- CC and BBb tubas are the favorites of symphonic players: more comfortable fingering, and every note of the
  repertoire is available on them.
- Construction: a wide, fully conical body and a very large bell. Piston valves or, more often, rotary valves, in a
  system like the horn's and trumpet's. A 4th valve lowers the fundamental by a perfect 4th (5 semitones). Some
  tubas have a 5th or even 6th valve (rare).
- A very deep cup mouthpiece eases low notes and pedal notes.
- Sousaphone (footnote): popular in bands, with an odd bell orientation. Never used in the modern symphony orchestra,
  though it can play all written tuba parts.

**Rules**
- R17 [ALLOWED] The tuba works as a soloist and in combination with other instruments.
- R18 [PREFER] Combine the tuba with the 3 trombones: it gives them an excellent bass.
  The tuba with trumpets and horns is also very beautiful.
- R19 [PREFER] `[era: modern orchestra]` Use one tuba player by default.
- R20 [ALLOWED] Use 2 tuba parts, as Stravinsky, Schoenberg and Harris did, mainly to reinforce the bass of a
  larger-than-normal trumpet/horn section. This also keeps one player from being overworked.
  Give the higher tessitura to tuba 1 and the lower to tuba 2.

## Registro y características registrales (tuba) (scans p.351–352, printed p.350–351)

**Concepts**
- The modern tuba is always written in bass clef, at concert pitch.
- Range, Ex. 10-85: **D1–F4 (26–65)**.
- Register characteristics, Ex. 10-86:
  - D1–Bb1 (26–34): deep and heavy (profundo y pesado);
  - C2–E3 (36–52): very powerful (muy potente);
  - F3–F4 (53–65): increasingly weak but very intense (cada vez más débil pero muy intenso).
- Any modern symphonic tubist can play in any part of this range. Many tubists own more than one instrument and
  switch to the one that suits a particular score.

**Rules**
- R21 [MUST] Keep orchestral tuba notes within D1–F4 (MIDI 26–65).
- R22 [PREFER] In the low register, write slow-moving bass notes. Because of its size, the tuba articulates a little
  clumsily down there. Treated as slow bass notes, low notes are well controlled and effective at any dynamic from
  ff to pp.
- R23 [ALLOWED] From the middle register up, the tuba is refreshingly agile; faster lines are fine there.
- R24 [ALLOWED] The middle register is soft and round, like a horn (because of the conical body and the deep
  mouthpiece). Use it for lyrical lines and for blending with horns.
- R25 [AVOID] Near the extreme top the sound becomes much thinner and more intense, and loses its characteristic
  quality. Use that register only for a deliberately strained color.
- R26 [MUST] The tuba is the largest brass instrument and needs a lot of air. Write rests often, and do not give it
  tiring parts. Adler strongly recommends this.

**Examples**
- Ex. 10-87 Mussorgsky–Ravel, *Pictures at an Exhibition*, "Bydlo", mm.1–10: tuba solo, sempre moderato pesante 2/4,
  G# minor, pp poco a poco cresc. A tenuto/slurred melody in the middle and high register, rising to about G#4 `[?]`.
  This is above the F4 printed limit, so it is an exceptional high solo.
- Ex. 10-88 Mahler, *Symphony No. 1* III, mm.15–23: tuba, Andante, pp. A smooth slurred melody (the round theme) in
  the middle/upper register, played softly.

## Articulación y picado (tuba) (scans p.352–354, printed p.351–353)

**Concepts**
- Many articulations are possible at different dynamics.
- As on all brass, all notes under one slur are played automatically in one breath.
- Single, double and triple tonguing (picado sencillo, doble, triple) are possible.

**Rules**
- R27 [MUST] Limit each slur to what one breath can hold. On tuba that means short phrases with breath room.
- R28 [AVOID] Double and triple tonguing are not normally used in the standard orchestral tuba repertoire. Avoid
  fast repeated-note tonguing figures in a conventional orchestral style.
- R29 [ALLOWED] The tuba can play hard attacks: sffz > p < ff, or sffz > pp < ff, on sustained notes.
- R30 [ALLOWED] It can play soft, sweet, lyrical passages.
- R31 [ALLOWED] It can play surprisingly agile passages with wide leaps, given its size.
- R32 [PREFER] The tuba is a great doubler. It can reinforce any bass part, loud or soft.
- R33 [ALLOWED] Tuba plus arco double basses on the same note: the tuba adds a pizzicato-like punch to the bowed note.

**Examples**
- Ex. 10-89 "Hard attacks on the tuba": Adagio. Sustained notes with sffz>p<ff and sffz>pp<ff swells, high and low.
- Ex. 10-90 Wagner, *The Flying Dutchman* Act I, "Die Frist ist um", mm.175–181: quarter = 69, pp < > p. A soft
  legato bass line in long values; the tuba as lyric bass.
- Ex. 10-91 Prokofiev, *Symphony No. 5* I at [23]: Andante, quarter = 48, f to ff. Big leaps (two octaves and more)
  into the low register, down to about F1 `[?]`, with triplets and dotted rhythms; demonstrates agility.
- Ex. 10-92 Ravel, *La Valse*, 5 bars after [63]: Allegro in 1, ff. Waltz bass with octave-plus drops to low notes
  far below the staff, and p < ff swells. Shows quick leaps.
- Ex. 10-93 Mahler, *Symphony No. 6* IV, mm.178–180 (tuba only): Allegro moderato, "heavy marcato", f. A punchy
  staccato/accented bass figure that adds attack to the bowed basses.
- Additional study passages (tuba), pointer only: Gershwin *An American in Paris*; Shostakovich *Symphony 7* II;
  R. Strauss *Till Eulenspiegel*.

**Agent applicability**
- When doubling contrabass lines, put the tuba on accented or important notes with a shorter, harder velocity:
  "pizzicato touch". Avoid holding every bass note on tuba. *(inferred from R32–R33)*
- Use CC1/expression swells for the sffz>p<ff attack shape.

## Sordinas y efectos especiales (tuba) (scans p.354–355, printed p.353–354)

**Concepts**
- Only one kind of tuba mute exists.
- Trills (trinos) are produced by working the valves.
- Flutter-tongue (picado frullato): a special effect used by more recent composers.

**Rules**
- R34 [MUST] Putting the mute in and taking it out of the bell is awkward. Give the tubist enough rest before and
  after a muted passage.
- R35 [PREFER] Muted tuba is best in the middle and high registers, as in the book's example.
- R36 [ALLOWED] Tuba trills are rare in the symphonic repertoire, but very effective when used.
- R37 [ALLOWED] `[era: 20th c.]` Flutter-tongue is possible even very softly (ppp).

**Examples**
- Ex. 10-94 "Muted tuba": con sord., Andante, mf > p. A slurred line in the middle/upper register.
- Ex. 10-95 Wagner, *Die Meistersinger* Prelude, mm.158–165: bass tuba, Allegro. A melodic quarter/eighth line that
  ends with a trill.
- Ex. 10-96 Schoenberg, *Erwartung* m.426: tuba, ppp, flutter-tongued fast 32nd-note figures under a slur, 12/8.

## Otros miembros de la familia de la tuba (scans p.355–356, printed p.354–355)

**Concepts**
- Euphonium (bombardino) and tenor tuba (tuba tenor):
  - In some places, especially the US, the euphonium has replaced Wagner's tenor tuba and is often used to read its
    parts.
  - New Wagner tubas have appeared recently, and composers are using them, e.g. Christopher Rouse, *Symphony No. 1*.
  - Like modern tubas, the euphonium is non-transposing and written in bass clef.
  - Wagner-tuba parts are written in treble clef, so a euphonium player reading them must transpose down a
    major 9th (14 semitones).
- Euphonium range, Ex. 10-97: Bb1 `[?]`–Bb4 (34–70). The low note's ledger lines are faint. The book says the range is
  the same as the bass trombone's.
- Euphonium construction: a miniature tuba, with a conical body, a flared bell and 4 valves. Very soft and sweet
  sound, without the intonation problems that plagued Wagner tubas.
- Baritone (barítono): strictly a band instrument, closely related to the euphonium; it sometimes replaces it.
  It has 3 valves. Range, Ex. 10-99: E2–Bb4 (40–70), the same as the tenor trombone.
- Ophicleide (oficleido): looks like a metal bassoon, but with a conical tube widening to a bell about 20 cm across.
  Large tone holes are covered by padded keys like modern woodwind keys. Its cup is more pronounced than a modern
  horn mouthpiece's, and its fairly sweet sound resembles the euphonium's. Non-transposing, bass clef.
  `[era: 19th c., c.1820–1850s]`

**Rules**
- R38 [ALLOWED] Few orchestral composers wrote specifically for the euphonium. It is common in band and wind-ensemble
  repertoire, where it doubles or replaces the baritone or has its own parts.
- R39 [ALLOWED] In North American orchestras the tenor-tuba passage of Strauss's *Don Quixote* is usually played on
  euphonium.
- R40 [ALLOWED] The baritone is very agile and blends well with other band instruments. For band examples, see the
  book's Ex. 17-19a and 17-20a.
- R41 [ALLOWED] `[era: 19th c.]` The ophicleide played the bass parts of the brass section during the 19th c. Composers
  who wrote for it: Berlioz, Mendelssohn, Schumann, Meyerbeer, Verdi and many others. One of its last appearances
  was Wagner's *Rienzi*. The tuba has replaced it completely.
- R42 [PREFER] When performing or recreating an ophicleide part today, give it to the tuba, or else to the bassoon or
  the contrabassoon.

**Examples**
- Ex. 10-98 R. Strauss, *Don Quixote*, mm.140–142: euphonium (tenor tuba part). Fast slurred 16th-note figures with
  leaps; shows agility in the tenor register.
- Additional study passages, pointer only:
  - euphonium: Grainger, Harris, Kupferman, Ruders;
  - Wagner (tenor) tuba: Bruckner *Symphonies 7 and 8*, Rouse *Symphony 1*, Schoenberg *Gurrelieder*,
    Strauss *Elektra* and *Alpine Symphony*, Stravinsky *Rite of Spring*, Wagner *Ring*
    (esp. *Siegfried* III prelude and *Götterdämmerung* I.1).

**Agent applicability**
- For a 19th-c. "ophicleide" color in a Berlioz/Mendelssohn pastiche, use a tuba or euphonium patch in its middle
  register, possibly doubled with bassoon. *(inferred)*
- For Wagner-tuba chorales (Bruckner style), use a euphonium or Wagner-tuba patch in the Bb1–Bb4 band.
  If you write a part, remember that the treble-clef written notes sound a major 9th lower.

## Pasajes adicionales para el estudio — chapter-end list (scan p.357, printed p.356)

Pointer only (study list grouped by instrument). Notable items that name a technique:
- Trumpet: Debussy *La Mer* III (muted trumpet); Franck *Symphony in D minor* I (trumpet doubling English horn and
  other woodwinds).
- Horn: Dvořák *Symphony 8* IV (horn trills); Mahler *Symphony 3* (8 horns in unison, then divisi in the low register);
  R. Strauss *Symphonia Domestica* (horn at its highest); Stravinsky *Symphony in Three Movements* III (horn glissando);
  Wagner *Rheingold* prelude (8 horns); Wagner *Siegfried Idyll* (horn trills).
