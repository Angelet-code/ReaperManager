# Adler, El estudio de la orquestación — scans 448–466 (printed 449–467)

Pitch convention: C4 = middle C = MIDI 60, concert pitch. Printed pages run continuously 449–467; none
missing. Chapter 13 (keyboards) begins on the next scan (printed p.469), so chapter 12 ends in this range.
Adler orders the section: pitched (determinate) instruments first (chordophones, pitched aerophones), then
"Instrumentos de altura indeterminada" (unpitched): idiophones (metal, wood), membranophones, aerophones.
Most notation examples here are one-line percussion staves showing rhythm, rolls and dynamics only.

## Rototoms — end (scan p.448, printed p.449)

**Concepts**
- rototom (*roto-tom*): single-head drum with a plastic head on a metal ring; tuned by rotating the head
  (clockwise tightens/raises, counter-clockwise lowers).
- Older, non-precisely-tuned rototoms are now discarded and replaced in most groups by the newer tunable
  (Remo-type) drums described on the previous pages; some orchestras still use them [era: 1970s–90s
  rototom sets].

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] Older rototom sizes and approximate ranges (Ex. 12-35, read from the scan; low notes uncertain
  because ledger lines are faint): 26 cm ≈ D2[?]–D3 (MIDI 38[?]–50); 21 cm ≈ A3[?]–A4 (57[?]–69);
  16 cm ≈ D4–D5 (62–74). Each size covers roughly an octave.
- R2 [PREFER] Treat rototoms as much drier than Remo toms or timpani: very little resonance. *Why:* stated
  by Adler.
- R3 [ALLOWED] Any wooden, plastic, rubber or even cord-wound stick/mallet works on them.

**Examples**
- Ex. 12-34 (timpani + rototoms, "Libremente"): one player alternates normal-head timpani notes with x-head
  rototom notes in an accelerating feathered-beam figure, then a quintuplet across drums, ending on a
  trilled high rototom note; shows combined timp/rototom set-ups and free-tempo accelerando writing.

**Agent applicability**
- For pitched tom glissando/pitch-bend effects, rototoms are the idiomatic source; keep each drum's part
  within about an octave and keep notes short (no sustain/ring).

## Chordophones: the cimbalom (Los cordófonos; El cimbalón) (scan p.448–450, printed p.449–451)

**Concepts**
- chordophones (*cordófonos*): sound from vibrating strings; all are pitched. In the percussion section this
  means the cimbalom plus keyboard instruments used as orchestral members (piano, harpsichord, harmonium,
  organ — treated in ch.13).
- cimbalom (*cimbalón*, Cimb.): the most developed of the old dulcimers (*salterios*); trapezoid, horizontal,
  metal strings struck with leather- or wood-tipped hammers; several strings per note like a piano; often has
  a foot-operated damper pedal (*pedal de sordina*). Adopted into the symphony orchestra by Hungarians in the
  early 20th c.; a variant is common in Appalachian folk music (hammered dulcimer).

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] Cimbalom range: E2–E6 (MIDI 40–88), four chromatic octaves (Ex. 12-36; matches the text's
  "four octaves").
- R2 [PREFER] Use it for florid, fast passages (Bartók, Kodály, Stravinsky did so successfully).
- R3 [MUST] Sustained notes are played as rolls (like marimba), even when the part does not notate a roll.
- R4 [ALLOWED] Modern use in sophisticated art music, e.g. Dutilleux, *L'Arbre des songes* (and his other
  works with strings) [era: post-1980].

**Examples**
- Ex. 12-37 Kodály, *Háry János* Suite, 5th mvt, m.87–93 (♩=120): cimbalom runs continuous 16th-note
  figuration through the tutti, doubling/decorating the fl/ob/cl/Vln1 melody; at the ff climax it keeps
  the 16ths, then at the dim./p it switches to repeated rolled chord dyads (written as trilled/tremolo
  quarters) under a solo oboe; shows the cimbalom as a rhythmic-colour layer cutting through a full
  orchestra, and rolled chords for sustain.

**Agent applicability**
- A cimbalom MIDI part = fast 16th figuration (velocity-driven, no sustain pedal) or repeated-note rolls
  (re-triggered 32nds) for long notes; never a single held note longer than ~1 beat without re-attacks
  (inferred from R3).
- Clamp to MIDI 40–88.

## Pitched aerophones: whistles (Los aerófonos; Los silbatos) (scan p.450–451, printed p.451–452)

**Concepts**
- aerophones (*aerófonos*) in the percussion section: all whistles, sirens and machines such as the wind
  machine. All have a pitch, but it is not always specified (except, sometimes, whistles).
- Types of whistle to name in the score: bird whistle (*silbato de pájaro*), police whistle, slide whistle
  (*de corredera*), tin whistle (*de hojalata*), train whistle, etc.

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] Name the exact whistle type in the full score.
- R2 [ALLOWED] Write exact pitches for whistles that can play them (tin whistle).
- R3 [MUST] If no definite pitch is wanted, give at least a precise description of what the whistle must do.

**Examples**
- Ex. 12-38 Copland, *Billy the Kid*, "Street in a Frontier Town" m.1–4 (Moderato, 2/4, Eb/Ab key
  signature): tin whistle plays a fully pitched folk-tune melody in the treble staff; shows whistle as a
  melodic folk-colour instrument.
- Corigliano, *Pied Piper Fantasy* (flute concerto for James Galway): at the finale's climax the soloist leads
  children playing tin whistles onto the stage; shows whistles as a theatrical/programmatic device.
- Study pointer: Kernis, *New Era Dance* (whistles + synth); J. Williams, *Far and Away* (whistles, pipes,
  Celtic harp, two fiddles).

**Agent applicability**
- For Celtic/folk colour in film scoring, a tin-whistle melody track is idiomatic (cf. Williams); write it
  as a normal pitched melody.

**Tags:** #orchestration/percussion #instrument/whistle #style/folk #style/film

## Unpitched idiophones — metal: cymbals (Los platos o platillos) (scan p.451–453, printed p.452–454)

**Concepts**
- cymbals (*platos/platillos*; It. *piatti*, Fr. *cymbales*, Ger. *Becken*): Turkish origin; curved metal
  plates with a raised cup/bell at the centre. Three parts: edge (*borde*), bow/body (*arco*), bell/cup
  (*campana, cúpula*). Score symbols exist for pair, suspended cymbal and hi-hat.
- Standard sizes (diameter): 26–36 cm, 38–46 cm, 48–61 cm; players can usually find unusual sizes if asked.
- crash/pair cymbals (*pareja de platos*, *plato en la mano*): held by leather straps through the bells.
- suspended cymbal (*plato suspendido*): hung from a stand by a strap.
- hi-hat (*charles*, *chaston*): two facing cymbals on a vertical rod clashed by a pedal.
- sizzle cymbal (*plato claveteado*): newest member; loose metal rivets in holes drilled around the perimeter
  rattle when struck or rolled; hung by a strap or clamped to a vertical rod.
- Chinese cymbals (*platos chinos*): upturned edges, sound like high gongs (Chen Yi, Tan Dun, Bright Sheng).
- finger cymbals (*platos de dedo*; It. *cimbalini*, Fr. *cymbales digitales*, Ger. *Fingerzimbeln*): pair of
  small plates ~55 mm diameter, unpitched.

**Rules**
- R1 [PREFER] Specify which part of the cymbal is to be struck when a particular effect is wanted; otherwise
  the player strikes a suspended cymbal at the edge.
- R2 [ALLOWED] Pair cymbals, three basic methods: (1) clash and raise them overhead (let ring); if the action
  is fast, clash and damp immediately against the chest (choke); soft clashes are very effective;
  (2) slide one against the other for a hissing "swish"; (3) a roll by rubbing them with a fast rotary motion
  — possible but not very effective (AVOID as a main effect).
- R3 [ALLOWED] Suspended cymbal strikers: wooden stick for a clear articulation; brushes or yarn marimba mallets
  for softer effects; all of these can roll; a triangle beater struck or scraped gives a fully metallic
  sound; it can also be bowed (*con arco*).
- R4 [MUST] Suspended cymbal sounds differ at edge, body and bell for both strokes and rolls: name the contact
  point.
- R5 [AVOID] Hi-hat in orchestral writing unless deliberately imitating a jazz combo drum kit. Closed it gives
  a dry, non-resonant "chick"; allowed to vibrate it gives a slightly ringing, softer clash.
- R6 [ALLOWED] Sizzle cymbal uses the same strikers as suspended cymbal; produces a sizzling, hissing sound on
  strokes and rolls.
- R7 [ALLOWED] Finger cymbals two ways: (1) struck together → high metallic sound like a triangle; (2) one
  struck with a metal, wood or plastic beater → a definite but indeterminate pitch. Do not confuse with
  antique cymbals/crotales, which are calibrated and give specific pitches.
- R8 [PREFER] Finger cymbals blend easily with surrounding sounds; used as rhythmic instruments in modern
  performances of medieval music [era: early-music revival].

**Examples**
- Ex. 12-39 Pair of cymbals (Moderato 4/4; 1st time f, 2nd time p): single hand-held clashes, a "swish"
  slide notation, then repeated quarter clashes pp cresc. to ff and a held ff half/whole note; shows clash
  vs swish and let-ring durations.
- Ex. 12-40 Three suspended cymbals (Lentamente): rolls with pp<ff, pp<ff hairpins across three pitches of
  cymbal, then "rascar" (scrape), brushes (*escobillas*) triplets pp<f, bow (*arco*), and sticks with a sf
  final stroke; shows rolled swells and change of beaters mid-passage.
- Ex. 12-41 Hi-hat (3/2): steady quarters ending l.v.
- Ex. 12-42 Sizzle cymbal (3/4): sf strokes, p→f→ff repeated notes and rolls; 1st time hard sticks, 2nd time
  brushes.
- Ex. 12-43 Finger cymbals (3/4, pp): simple quarter/half pulses, then "con baqueta de metal".

**Agent applicability**
- Cymbal swell = suspended-cymbal roll with CC/velocity crescendo ending on the downbeat of the target bar;
  an orchestral crash = pair-cymbal clash; a short accent = choked clash (short note, or a "choke" articulation
  in the library).
- In orchestral (non-pop) cues, prefer suspended cymbal and pair cymbals over hi-hat; use hi-hat only for a
  jazz-kit allusion.
- GM drum-map mapping (inferred, not from Adler): crash 49/57, ride 51, ride bell 53, Chinese 52, splash 55,
  closed/pedal/open hi-hat 42/44/46.

## Triangle (El triángulo) (scan p.453–454, printed p.454–455)

**Concepts**
- triangle (*triángulo*; It. *triangolo*, Ger. *Triangel*): curved metal bar in triangle shape, probably of
  Turkish origin; one of the oldest unpitched orchestral percussion instruments; popular in early opera and a
  regular symphony-orchestra member since Haydn and Beethoven (see Beethoven Sym. 9 finale, m.14ff, Ex. 14-14).
- Main sizes 15, 20, 25 cm → high, medium, low register.

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] Played with a small metal beater; single strokes or rolls/trills, made by beating two sides
  inside one corner.
- R2 [PREFER] Soft dynamics: they are far more effective than loud ones, which tend to be monotonous and shrill.
- R3 [PREFER] Combine with strings and woodwinds, especially in their upper register; it also gives good
  contrast to low instruments.
- R4 [ALLOWED] As a solo colour, or added to a large orchestral chord to give it "luminosity".
- R5 [MUST] Resonance is long; specify exactly how long each note should ring (use *l.v.* or written durations).

**Examples**
- Ex. 12-44 Triangle (Lentamente 3/4): single strokes pp, mp, f with tied ring, then a roll mf>p and a roll
  crescendo to ff; shows dynamic range and written ring lengths.

**Agent applicability**
- Triangle hits: low velocity by default (pp–mf), placed on the downbeat of bright, high tutti chords; let
  ring (long note-off) unless choked.
- GM (inferred): open triangle 81, mute triangle 80.

**Tags:** #orchestration/percussion #instrument/triangle #orchestration/colour

## Anvil, cowbells (El yunque; Los cencerros) (scan p.454–455, printed p.455–456)

**Concepts**
- anvil (*yunque*; It. *incudine*, Fr. *enclume*, Ger. *Amboss*): large steel block struck with a metal hammer,
  imitating a smith's anvil. Much used in the late 19th c., especially Wagner, *Das Rheingold*; also Varèse,
  Bloch, Copland, Foss, Rouse. Any similar object can substitute (some percussionists use a length of
  railway track).
- cowbells (*cencerros*; It. *cencerro*, Fr. *sonnailles*, Ger. *Kuhglocken*): in Europe real (clappered)
  cowbells are used (e.g. Mahler Sym. 6/i m.198–216). The Latin-American cowbell is clapperless, roughly
  triangular, usually silver-bronze, sizes ~7–15 cm; low/mid/high sizes differ little in pitch; struck with a
  snare-drum stick for a metallic sound.
- tuned cowbells (*cencerros afinados*): requested by Messiaen, Schwantner, Crumb, Tower; now available
  [era: late 20th c.].

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] Anvil writing: hard ff single strokes and rhythmic patterns in march tempo (Ex. 12-45).
- R2 [PREFER] When using several cowbells of different sizes, place them on staff lines/spaces by relative
  pitch (Ex. 12-46, four cowbells on one staff).
- R3 [ALLOWED] Latin dance bands use cowbells at least as much as orchestras do.

**Examples**
- Ex. 12-45 Anvil (Tempo di marcia 2/4, ff): quarter/half strokes with ties.
- Ex. 12-46 Four cowbells (Bastante rápido 4/4): triplet 8th patterns spread over four staff positions,
  ending with a roll; shows multi-bell melodic-rhythmic figures.
- Study pointers: Tan Dun, *Gitimalia* (marimba, six tuned cowbells, two harps, amplified guitar); J. Tower,
  *Sequoia* opening (celesta, piano, tuned cowbells).

**Agent applicability**
- Anvil = rhythmic metal accent for heavy, industrial or "forge" music (cf. Wagner's Nibelheim); short notes,
  high velocity.
- Write multiple cowbells as distinct MIDI notes ordered low→high by size (GM cowbell 56; inferred).

**Tags:** #orchestration/percussion #instrument/anvil #instrument/cowbell #style/latin

## Tam-tam and gongs (El tam-tam y otros gongs) (scan p.455, printed p.456)

**Concepts**
- Gongs divide into high, medium and low; even good ears struggle to identify their fundamental.
- Distinguish unpitched gongs (tam-tam family, the tam-tam being the largest) from authentic Asian gongs,
  which have precise pitches.
- tam-tam: usually the largest and lowest gong; until recently the most common gong in the symphony
  orchestra; heavy metal; unlike most gongs (played with a timpani mallet or slightly thicker beater), it is
  struck with its own large gong mallet (*maza de gong*).

**Examples**
- Ex. 12-47 Three gongs + tam-tam (3/4): ff tam-tam stroke tied (let ring), an f run of gong eighths, mf
  stroke, a p<f>p triplet gong figure, then a final pp tam-tam stroke; shows tam-tam as the low sustaining
  frame around shorter gong figures.

**Agent applicability**
- Tam-tam: one stroke at structural points (climax arrival, or a pp "dark" stroke at an ending); long
  note-off; never repeat it quickly. Pitched gongs only when a library maps real pitches.

**Tags:** #orchestration/percussion #instrument/tam-tam #instrument/gong

## Wind chimes, sleigh bells, mark tree, brake drum, thunder sheet (scan p.455–456, printed p.456–457)

**Concepts**
- wind chimes (*cortinas*, Cort.): three types — bamboo, glass, metal (also wood is mentioned); suspended rods/
  pieces like a mobile. Usually struck/swept with the hand and left to ring until stopped by hand.
  Bamboo and wood: weak, hollow, brittle, whispering sound; can also be struck with a wooden stick or
  rubbed together suddenly for a dry, louder "choc". Wood chimes are tuned much higher than bamboo. Glass:
  very high, delicate, slightly jingling. Metal: somewhat more noticeable, though not loud.
- sleigh bells (*cascabeles*; It. *sonagli*, Fr. *grelots*, Ger. *Schellen*): held in one or both hands and
  shaken.
- bell tree (*árbol de campanas*): nested small bells struck with a metal rod in an upward or downward sweep
  (direction must be specified; arrows in the notation).
- brake drum (*tambor de freno*): automobile brake drums of various sizes, played with sticks or brushes like
  any drum; often substitutes for metal plates because it is more resonant. See Copland, *Connotations*
  (end) and Sym. 3; Rouse, *Phaethon* (near the end).
- thunder sheet (*plancha metálica*): large hanging metal sheet, struck with a beater or shaken by hand.

**Examples**
- Ex. 12-48 Wind chimes: bamboo with rolls and dry strokes; wood/glass/metal held notes tied over bars, ending
  on a fermata roll; shows how each type is written as a let-ring note.
- Ex. 12-49 Sleigh bells (Alegremente 6/8): shaken 8th patterns f, then rolls p<ff.
- Ex. 12-50 Bell tree (Lentamente 3/4): single sweeps with up/down arrows, f, p, pp, let ring.
- Ex. 12-51 Brake drum (2/4): repeated 8ths and 16ths ending sf.
- Ex. 12-52 Thunder sheet (4/4): one long roll p crescendo over several bars to fff.

**Agent applicability**
- Bell tree / mark-tree gesture: one sweep at a phrase start or transition, upward for "magic opening";
  wind chimes: let-ring texture, never rhythmic.
- Thunder sheet: long roll crescendo, best for storm/menace; brake drum: hard metallic ostinato.
- Sleigh bells: shaken 8ths for "sleigh/winter" or festive cues.

## Unpitched idiophones — wood (De madera) (scan p.456–460, printed p.457–461)

**Concepts**
- wood blocks / Chinese blocks (*cajas chinas*, W.Bl.; It. *blocci di legno cinese*/*cassettina*, Fr.
  *blocs de bois*, Ger. *Holzblöcke*): rectangular hard-wood pieces in graded sets of 3–5; on a table or stand.
  Though unpitched, sizes give a spectrum low (largest) → high (smallest). Incisive, penetrating, very dry.
- temple blocks (*temple-blocks*; It. *blocci di legno coreano*, Fr. *temple-blocs*, Ger. *Tempelblöcke*):
  graded set of 5 clam-shaped wooden blocks on a stand, usually red-lacquered, Asian origin; more resonant,
  sweeter and hollower than wood blocks.
- claves: Latin-American; two hard-wood cylinders ~2.5 cm diameter × 15 cm; one rests on cupped fingertips (the
  hand is the resonator) and is struck by the other. Like the highest wood block struck hard, but more
  resonant, with a "ping".
- castanets (*castañuelas*, Cast.): Mediterranean origin, two small spoon-shaped hard-wood shells. Three kinds:
  (1) hand castanets, usually two pairs — need considerable skill, uncommon in orchestra; (2) handle/paddle
  castanets (*de mango*) — easy, can play very loud; (3) concert castanets on a board, upper shell hit with a
  finger or stick — newest and the usual orchestral type. 17th–18th-c. Spanish and Italian composers wrote
  concertos for castanets and strings.
- sandpaper blocks (*lija*, Lij.): two small blocks covered with sandpaper rubbed together, like soft-shoe
  dancing.
- maracas (Mrcs.): Latin-American; gourd, wood or plastic shell filled with pebbles or seeds; usually in pairs.
  Similar instruments: chocallo, kameso, cabasa, rain stick (*palo de lluvia*).
- vibraslap (*quijada*): Latin-American, related to maracas because it rattles; descends from a donkey's
  jawbone (*quijada*), teeth and all; held in one hand, struck near the wide end with the other fist,
  giving a loose-teeth chattering buzz that lasts according to force.
- güiro (Fr. *râpe guiro*): large bottle-shaped gourd with a notched side scraped up and down with a stick;
  Brazilian relative: reco-reco.
- ratchet (*carraca*; It. *raganella*, Fr. *crécelle*, Ger. *Ratsche*): imitates a child's rattle: a cogged
  cylinder turned by a crank against a hard wooden or metal tongue.
- whip / slapstick (*látigo*; It. *frusta*, Fr. *fouet*, Ger. *Peitsche*): two thin hard-wood boards hinged to
  a handle; one very loud crack.
- log drum (*tambor de madera*, T.Mad.; Indo-Mexican) and slit drum (*tambor de hendidura*, T.Hen.; African):
  hollowed, closed wood block with a slot along one face; two tongues of different length cut into it give
  two pitches; sizes give a pitch spectrum, and precise tunings can be specified. Slit drums are struck with
  hard marimba mallets on both sides of the slot; the interval between the two tones depends on thickness:
  a third, or more often a perfect fourth or even a perfect fifth.
- hammer (*martillo*; Ger. *Holzhammer*): used by Mahler (Sym. 6) and Rouse (Sym. 1), usually to hit a piece of
  wood or a metal plate; often a large wooden box (up to ~1.5 m on a side) is built for it, giving a fortissimo
  thud.

**Rules**
- R1 [PREFER] Wood blocks: single secco strokes, rolls or fast passages. Strikers: drum sticks, marimba,
  wooden, plastic or rubber mallets all work. A single block can be hand-held and struck with a mallet.
- R2 [AVOID] Hard mallets on temple blocks (they are more fragile); avoid hard rubber, plastic mallets and
  wooden sticks; otherwise same strikers and techniques as wood blocks. Music: secco single strokes, rolls,
  fast passages.
- R3 [ALLOWED] Claves: normally play the underlying ostinato (clave pattern) in rumbas, congas and sambas; in
  orchestral music to reinforce secco chords or as an alternative to wood/temple blocks.
- R4 [ALLOWED] Castanets evoke Spain (Bizet, *Carmen*, Seguidilla); today also used to sharpen rhythms or
  reinforce strong attacks.
- R5 [ALLOWED] Sandpaper blocks: isolated short rough strokes, rhythmic passages or rolls. Ask for very coarse
  grit for a very harsh sound, medium/fine for the opposite.
- R6 [ALLOWED] Maracas: one is usually enough outside a South-American context; shake gently (most effective
  as a solo pianissimo roll) or strike one against the hand for a short note; ostinato in Latin dances.
- R7 [PREFER] Vibraslap: write only single strokes, because the buzz lasts a while after each stroke.
- R8 [ALLOWED] Güiro: single strokes and rolls; orchestral composers often prefer it to the ratchet, rattle or
  sandpaper blocks, which can be too soft in orchestral passages.
- R9 [PREFER] Ratchet: loud passages and rolls. AVOID isolated single strokes (risky; the güiro does them
  better).
- R10 [ALLOWED] Whip: normally used to emphasise a sforzando.
- R11 [ALLOWED] Log/slit drums can substitute for wood blocks or temple blocks, with a darker sound.
- R12 [MUST] For the hammer, state in the part whether the hammer is wood or metal (and the struck surface).

**Examples**
- Ex. 12-53 Three wood blocks (Tempo di valse 3/4; f then pp): waltz accompaniment pattern spread over three
  pitches, with rolls.
- Ex. 12-54 Five temple blocks (3/4; f then p): fast 16th-note melodic runs across the five blocks, ending in
  rolls.
- Ex. 12-55 Claves (cut time, f): syncopated ostinato pattern.
- Ex. 12-56 Castanets (6/8): 16th-note and triplet rolls/figures typical of Spanish dance rhythm.
- Ex. 12-57 Sandpaper blocks (4/4): steady 8ths with a trilled (rubbed) note.
- Ex. 12-58 Maracas (2/4): "agitar" (shake) and "golpear" (strike) alternation, rolls, "agitar ambas".
- Ex. 12-59 Vibraslap (2/4): sparse single quarter strokes with rests.
- Ex. 12-60 Güiro (2/4): short scrapes and longer 16th-note scrape groups with rests.
- Ex. 12-61 Ratchet (4/4, ff): short bursts and rolled long notes.
- Ex. 12-62 Whip (2/4, ff): isolated cracks.
- Ex. 12-63 Two log drums, then two slit drums (Rápido 6/8, ff): continuous two-pitch 16th patterns.

**Agent applicability**
- Melodic-rhythmic woodblock/temple-block lines: assign each block a distinct MIDI note ordered low→high;
  fast 16ths are idiomatic (GM inferred: hi/low wood block 76/77, claves 75, maracas 70, short/long güiro
  73/74, vibraslap 58, cabasa 69, castanets 85).
- Latin feel: claves carry the clave ostinato; maracas/güiro carry 8th/16th ostinatos; keep these at
  moderate velocity in an orchestral texture.
- Spanish colour: concert castanets in triplet/16th rolls on dance rhythms; whip on sforzando hits only.
- Mahler hammer = one or two ff strokes at catastrophic structural points (Mahler 6 finale).

## Membranophones: snare, tenor drum, field drum (Los membranófonos) (scan p.460–462, printed p.461–463)

**Concepts**
- snare drum (*caja clara*, C.Clar.; It. *tamburo piccolo*, Fr. *caisse claire*, Ger. *kleine Trommel*): long a
  symphony member, and earlier an opera-orchestra member. Two heads: the upper one is struck; the lower carries
  snares (gut, wire or nylon). A lever disengages the snares so it sounds like a tom-tom (notated "con
  bordones" / "sin bordones" with symbols). With snares on it is crisp and cutting, excellent for short rhythmic
  patterns.
- Basic strokes beyond single L–R: (1) flam (*apoyatura*), (2) drag/ruff (*floreo*, two grace notes),
  (3) four-stroke ruff (*floreo de cuatro golpes*), (4) roll (*redoble*). In the first three the grace notes
  precede the accented note unless otherwise indicated.
- muffled (*parche cubierto*): cloth on the batter head.
- rim shot (*aro*, *golpe en el borde*, "r. sh."): one stick laid across head and rim, struck with the other;
  very penetrating. Elliott Carter's symbol may be used but must be explained at the start of the part.
- tenor drum (*tambor tenor*, T.Ten.; It. *cassa rullante*, Fr. *caisse roulante*, Ger. *Wirbeltrommel /
  Rührtrommel*): deeper, resonant, no snares; usually two heads; in bands and orchestras.
- field drum (*tambor militar*, T.Mil.): snared drum with the tenor drum's circumference but a deeper shell, so
  lower in pitch, darker and less crisp than the snare; played with large snare sticks; rarely used in
  orchestras.

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] Snare is normally played with wooden sticks; for soft effects, especially jazz, wire brushes.
- R2 [PREFER] Notate rolls with tremolo strokes (three slashes) rather than a trill sign. *Why:* stated
  preference (Ex. 12-64 commentary).
- R3 [ALLOWED] Tenor drum shares snare technique; wooden sticks, but timpani and marimba mallets are also
  effective.
- R4 [ALLOWED] Field drum uses the same techniques as the snare [era: military/band tradition].

**Examples**
- Ex. 12-64a Snare (Allegro 4/4; ff then pp): flams, drags, short trilled rolls and a final rim shot sf.
- Ex. 12-64b Snare (4/4, f; 2nd time without snares): accented 16th runs, ruffs, ends with rim shot and
  fermata.
- Ex. 12-65 Tenor drum (Rápido 2/4, ff): ruffs and 8th/16th patterns; then a roll p<ff.
- Ex. 12-66 Field drum (Quieto 2/4): march-like ruffs and 16th patterns.
- Study pointer: Nielsen, Clarinet Concerto (snare-drum obbligato throughout).

**Agent applicability**
- Snare roll in MIDI: use the library's roll articulation, or re-triggered 32nds/64ths with a velocity ramp;
  flam = grace note ~20–40 ms before the main hit at lower velocity (inferred).
- Snare off (sin bordones) ≈ use a tom or tenor-drum sample (inferred).
- Military/march colour: field drum or snare ruffs on 2/4 marches; tenor drum for darker processional
  writing.
- GM (inferred): acoustic snare 38, side stick 37, electric snare 40; rim shot often a separate articulation.

## Bass drum (El bombo) (scan p.462, printed p.463)

**Concepts**
- bass drum (*bombo*, Bomb.; It. *gran cassa*, Fr. *grosse caisse*, Ger. *große Trommel*): two heads, both
  playable when stood upright on its side; can also lie horizontal (score symbols for each).

**Rules**
- R1 [AVOID] Overusing ff strokes or rolls: its huge power easily covers the rest of the orchestra.
- R2 [ALLOWED] Response is a bit slower than smaller drums, but it is very effective on slow repeated notes or
  fast secco single strokes.
- R3 [ALLOWED] Effective soft and loud; adds much body to the percussion section. Can begin or end a
  percussion passage with a single stroke (Copland, Sym. 3/ii opening).
- R4 [ALLOWED] pp roll imitates distant thunder or evokes impending doom; in older symphonic repertoire it often
  represented war or belligerence [era: 18th–19th c. "Turkish"/military topic].
- R5 [ALLOWED] Usual beater: a timpani-like mallet but larger and softer with more felt; wooden sticks for
  single strokes and rolls.

**Examples**
- Ex. 12-67 Bass drum strokes (Adagio 2/4; f then p): sparse quarters, a triplet 16th group, rests.
- Ex. 12-68 Bass drum strokes and rolls (Alegre 6/8, mf; 1st time ordinary mallets, 2nd time wooden sticks):
  repeated 8ths, rolls, fermata.

**Agent applicability**
- Keep orchestral bass-drum velocity moderate except at true climaxes; use a pp roll (low velocity, long) for
  thunder/dread pads; single hits mark section starts or ends.
- GM (inferred): 35/36; orchestral libraries usually have a separate "Gran Cassa" patch with rolls.

## Tom-toms, timbales, bongos, congas (scan p.462–464, printed p.463–465)

**Concepts**
- tom-toms (*tom-toms*): halfway between pitched and unpitched — can be tuned to approximate pitches. Come on two
  stands, two drums each, tuned high→low to four different voices. Single-headed toms are like smaller tenor
  drums: crisp and articulate. Double-headed toms (mainly jazz bands; must be available in a symphony orchestra
  if specified) are more resonant, darker and deeper; some say listeners cannot tell the two types apart.
- timbales / pailas (*pailas, timbales cubanos, rumberas*, Rumb.; It. *timpanetti*, Fr. *timbales cubaines*,
  Ger. *Kuba-Pauken*): Latin-American single-headed metal-shell drums in pairs on a metal stand; snare-depth
  shell gives a more metallic, penetrating sound; only two sizes, 33 cm (high) and 35 cm (low).
- bongos (*bongós*, Bong.): Latin-American single-headed drums always in joined pairs, indeterminate pitch;
  professional sets have rim tensioning and are usually tuned a perfect fourth or fifth apart. Four sizes
  exist (low, high, higher, highest) so two pairs can now safely be written. Primitive bongos have fixed
  tuning.
- congas (*congas*, Cong.; It. *tumba*): Latin-American bass drum now fairly common in the symphony orchestra;
  ~76 cm tall, single head ~28 cm; inverted-hourglass shape. Striking near the rim gives a higher note than the
  centre.

**Rules**
- R1 [PREFER] Notate four tom-toms in the four spaces of the staff to show their relative pitches.
- R2 [ALLOWED] Tom strikers: yarn or cord mallets or normal snare sticks; technique like snare drum. Much
  tom-tom music uses figures written for wood blocks and temple blocks, with which toms often dialogue in the
  orchestra.
- R3 [ALLOWED] Timbales playing methods: (1) timbale sticks (longer, lighter than snare sticks), (2) marimba
  mallets, (3) hands, (4) any stick/mallet on the shell (*cáscara*), head centre or rim.
- R4 [ALLOWED] Bongos are traditionally held between the knees and played with the hands; can be mounted like
  timbales and played with hands, snare sticks, any mallet or even brushes, very softly.
- R5 [AVOID] Expecting a virtuoso Latin hand technique from an orchestral percussionist on bongos; tom-tom
  techniques on bongos are fine.
- R6 [ALLOWED] Congas are best played with the hands, but any mallet works. If two pitches are needed (rim vs
  centre), notate them clearly on two different spaces or lines.

**Examples**
- Ex. 12-69 Four tom-toms (Rápido 3/4; ff then pp): 16th-note runs moving across the four drums, rolls, sextuplet
  figure; shows melodic tom writing.
- Ex. 12-70 Two timbales (Rápido, changing 6/8–5/8–2/8–7/8–9/8; ff then pp): mixed-meter accented 8th figures.
- Ex. 12-71 Two bongos (Rápido 2/4; hands, then sticks): continuous 16ths alternating drums with accents.
- Ex. 12-72 Conga (Rápido 4/4): 16th patterns with rests.

**Agent applicability**
- Write tom fills as 16th runs descending high→low across 3–4 distinct MIDI notes; pair them in dialogue with
  wood/temple blocks for an orchestral (not rock-kit) idiom.
- Two-pitch Latin drums: bongos tuned a P4/P5 apart (two MIDI notes), congas open/rim as two notes (GM
  inferred: hi/low bongo 60/61, mute/open hi conga 62/63, low conga 64, hi/low timbale 65/66).

## Tambourine, cuica, lion's roar (scan p.464–465, printed p.465–466)

**Concepts**
- tambourine (*pandereta*, Pand.; It. *tamburo basco*[?] (smudged in scan), Fr. *tambour de basque*, Ger. *Tamburin*): very ancient
  (mentioned in the Bible); evokes Spain but used in all styles. A very shallow drum: one skin head on a wooden
  hoop with slots holding pairs of small jingles. Sizes: large 38 cm, medium 25 cm, small 15 cm.
- cuica (*cuica*, Cuic.): Brazilian; shaped like a large deep bongo; single head with a rod fixed at its centre,
  rubbed with a wet cloth or sponge so the head vibrates; usually a big wooden tub — bigger tub, louder.
- lion's roar / string drum (*rugido de león*, *tambor de cuerda*): similar construction but a rosined cord or
  leather strip instead of a rod, often tied outside the head to a round piece of wood; turning it tightens
  and releases the cord, giving a very lion-like roar.

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] Specify tambourine size; if none is given, the player uses the 25 cm instrument.
- R2 [ALLOWED] Tambourine techniques: (1) strike with the knuckles; (2) play softly with the fingers;
  (3) shake (written as a roll), usually for loud rolls; (4) thumb roll/trill, usually for soft rolls (mark it
  in the part); (5) any sticks/mallets when it rests on a stand or chair; (6) placed on another instrument
  (timpani, snare, bass drum) and struck with a mallet.
- R3 [ALLOWED] Cuica and lion's roar are used often today, e.g. Rouse, *The Infernal Machine*; Varèse,
  *Amériques* [era: 20th c.].

**Examples**
- Ex. 12-73 Tambourine (Rápido 2/4; ff then pp): struck notes and 16ths, a "shake" roll, a "thumb" trill and a
  final shake with fermata.
- Ex. 12-74 Cuica (2/4, mf–f): sustained glide notes with wavy-line pitch inflection.
- Study pointer: D. Dratell, *Throb*.

**Agent applicability**
- Tambourine: struck hits on backbeats/accents, shake for loud sustained rolls, thumb roll for soft ones; use
  separate articulations/notes if the library has them (GM inferred: 54).
- Cuica = comic/samba pitch-gliding squeaks (GM inferred: mute/open cuica 78/79); lion's roar for
  ferocious-animal or menacing film effects.

## Unpitched aerophones: sirens, horns, wind machine (scan p.465–466, printed p.466–467)

**Concepts**
- sirens (*sirenas*): used by many 20th-c. composers — Varèse, Antheil, Hindemith — for a realistic picture of
  "modern" society [era: early-mid 20th-c. modernism].
- horns (*bocinas*, car/klaxon horns): used especially in early-20th-c. works evoking city life, e.g. Gershwin,
  *An American in Paris* [era: 1920s].
- wind machine (*máquina de viento*): large wooden cylindrical frame covered with canvas, turned by a crank; thin
  wooden slats rub the canvas, giving a howling, hissing sound that rises with the speed of rotation; a very
  realistic wind effect.

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] When using a siren, specify its volume and type (high, shrill, buzzing, etc.).
- R2 [ALLOWED] Siren notation is not standardised; write on a single line showing duration and dynamics.
- R3 [AVOID] Overusing sirens/horns and similar effects: most have become clichés; use with great care.
- R4 [ALLOWED] Wind machine as programmatic wind: R. Strauss, *Alpine Symphony* and *Don Quixote*; Morton Gould,
  "Fire Music" from *Audubon*.

**Agent applicability**
- Wind machine: long notes with a velocity/CC swell (speed = pitch and loudness) for storms and mountain
  scenes (Alpine Symphony storm); siren/horn only for explicit urban/industrial programme, once, not as a
  recurring colour.
