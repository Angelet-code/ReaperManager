# Piston, *Orchestration* — scans s183L–s192R (printed p.366–385)

Method used throughout these chapters: Piston names each texture element (elemento) with a letter (A = melody,
B = accompaniment or secondary melody, C = accompaniment) and then lists which instruments play each one
(the "distribution"). Agents can use the same tool: before writing MIDI, assign every track to one element and
check that each element is set apart by register, timbre, rhythm or dynamics.

## Melody and accompaniment, end (scan s183L–s186R, printed p.366–373)

**Concepts**
- Element (elemento): one strand of the texture heard as a unit (melody, accompaniment, bass...). It can be played by many instruments in unison or octaves.
- Subelement (subelemento): one component of a compound accompaniment. It has its own rhythm, register or timbre (e.g. sustained winds + repeated-note harmony + bass).
- Harmonic thickening (engrosamiento armónico): doubling a melody in parallel intervals (or near-parallel ones). It stays one element, not a new voice. This was set up on an earlier page (Ex. 377, outside this range).
- Tone-weight (peso sonoro): how much an instrument or doubling counts in the balance. It depends on the instrument, its register and its dynamic.

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] Hand one melodic element back and forth between instruments phrase by phrase: flute ↔ oboe in the upper octave and clarinet ↔ bassoon an octave lower (Ex. 381).
- R2 [AVOID] An entry that the ear would hear as the voice-leading continuation of another instrument's note, when that was not meant. In Ex. 381 Beethoven leaves the clarinet out of measure 4: its entry on D would have sounded like the oboe's C moving to D. Oboe and bassoon lead to B instead. *Why:* entries across instruments are heard as one line when they connect by step.
- R3 [PREFER] When a passage repeats, change the color of one octave for contrast. In Ex. 381 the violins take the lower octave the second time: their tone and their place in the orchestra contrast more with the oboe.
- R4 [ALLOWED] Change a string accompaniment from unison to thirds within a phrase for subtle variety (Ex. 381, m.2 → m.4).
- R5 [PREFER] Contrast articulation between elements: a light staccato string accompaniment under legato woodwinds (Ex. 381).
- R6 [ALLOWED] In a doubled melody, the first players (I) have the edge: better register and they are the top voice. When the two voices of an element are evenly matched, the upper one leads (Ex. 382).
- R7 [PREFER] Put most of the tone-weight of a doubled melody in the upper octave. In Ex. 382 flutes, oboes and violins are on top and the clarinets alone sit below.
- R8 [ALLOWED] Classical clarinets in the middle register often play parts that were later given to trumpets once trumpets had valves (Ex. 382). [era: Classical, natural trumpets, before about 1830]
- R9 [ALLOWED] An accompaniment can sit on one pitch (the tonic D in Ex. 382) and still drive the music, if it is split into several rhythmic patterns by group. In Fig. 112 trumpets and horns sustain and then move in quarters, timpani play their own pattern, and bassoons/violas/cellos/basses repeat half notes. Together with the melody this makes a four-part rhythmic texture.
- R10 [ALLOWED] At a cadence the first violins can leave the melody and join the accompaniment, turning the texture into part writing on the cadential chords (Ex. 382, mm.5–6).
- R11 [ALLOWED] Harmonize the melody in several parts that do not run parallel to it but still blend with it as one element (Ex. 383, horn I melody with horns and bassoons). Vary the number of voices from bar to bar.
- R12 [PREFER] When woodwinds share a melodic element in a wide spacing, balance them evenly. The top voice (flute I) is heard most because it is on top (Ex. 383, mm.6–9).
- R13 [ALLOWED] A thematically important bass turns melody-and-accompaniment toward counterpoint (two melodies) (Ex. 383).
- R14 [ALLOWED] Violins can play a decorated variation of the melody at the same time as solo winds play it plainly. This was common practice (Ex. 384: oboe and horn in octaves, violins figuring the same tune).
  - R14a [MUST] Then keep the violins softer (pp against p solos) and put rests in their figuration so the solo instruments come through.
- R15 [ALLOWED] Build the accompaniment from three subelements: sustained tones (clarinets and bassoons), animated repeated-note harmony (violas), and a bass given a light pulse by the cellos. This is an elaboration of four-part writing (Ex. 384).
- R16 [ALLOWED] A doubled flute melody in a gentle register (p, *un peu en dehors*) can be heard clearly against a large accompaniment. The condition: every accompanying instrument sits in a register where it can play as softly as needed (Ex. 385, La Mer). Piston notes that performances prove this.
- R17 [PREFER] For a very light, transparent accompaniment:
  - strings play with very little bow (*très léger*);
  - leave out the double basses except for one note;
  - double a low pp horn pedal (horns II and IV) only to steady the tone;
  - let the parts of one subelement overlap each other by an eighth note, each with a diminuendo (Ex. 385, English horn, clarinets and bassoons).
- R18 [ALLOWED] Add celesta and harps only at the end of a phrase as a touch of color (Ex. 385). The score says glockenspiel (or celesta); in practice the celesta is almost always used.
- R19 [PREFER] When a phrase repeats, re-color the melody, e.g. flutes replaced by oboe I (Ex. 385).

**Examples**
- Ex. 380 Debussy, *Prélude à l'après-midi d'un faune* (Très modéré, D♭/G♭ area): melody p *expressif et très soutenu* in flute I, oboe I, English horn and clarinets in near-parallel harmony. Under it are pp divided violins I and II and violas playing slurred repeated-note chords, pp bassoon/horn chords that answer, and a held pp bass. Its commentary is on p.365, outside this range.
- Ex. 381 Beethoven, Symphony no. 1, i (Allegro con brio): a woodwind hand-off in two octaves over staccato strings (R1–R5).
- Ex. 382 Mozart, *The Marriage of Figaro*, Overture (Presto, D): the melody is in flutes, oboes, clarinets and violins I/II in 3rds/6ths. The tonic D is spread across bassoons, horns, trumpets, timpani and the low strings in different rhythms (R6–R10).
- Ex. 383 Brahms, Symphony no. 2, i (Allegro non troppo): horns I/II and bassoons harmonize the horn melody in parts (mm.2–5). Flutes, clarinets and bassoons then answer p *dolce* in a wide, even spacing (mm.6–9). Cellos and basses carry a thematic bass throughout (R11–R13).
- Ex. 384 Schumann, Symphony no. 1, ii (Larghetto, E♭): oboe I + horn I solo in octaves (p *dolce*), and violins I/II pp *espress.* figure the same melody with rests. Violas play repeated-note harmony (*pp sempre*), clarinets and bassoons sustain, and cellos pulse under the bass (R14–R15).
- Ex. 385 Debussy, *La Mer* (Animé, E major): flutes a2 with trills carry the melody. The accompaniment has four subelements:
  - English horn, clarinets and bassoons: pp overlapping figures with diminuendo;
  - horns II and IV: a low pp pedal on sounding E2 (MIDI 40), read from a written B1 in old-notation bass clef for horn in F (inferred);
  - divided violins I: a high pp trill, with celesta and harps at the cadence;
  - violins II, violas and cellos: a pp *très léger* staccato figure, with basses on a single C♯ (R16–R19).

**Agent applicability**
- Assign tracks to elements first. A melody doubled by many instruments is still one element. Put most of its weight in the top octave.
- In an antecedent/consequent hand-off between woodwinds, check that a new entry does not sit a step from the previous instrument's last note unless that link is wanted.
- When a solo wind and violins share a melody, lower the violins about one dynamic step (CC1/CC11) and insert rests in their figuration.
- For a soft accompaniment under a quiet flute, keep each accompanying part in its easy soft register. Use short-bow or staccato string articulations, drop the basses, and double any low horn pedal for stability, not for volume.

## Ch. 21 Types of Texture — Type III, Secondary Melody (scan s187L–s190R, printed p.374–381)

**Concepts**
- Three-element texture (textura de tres elementos): primary melody + secondary melody + accompaniment.
- Secondary melody (melodía secundaria): either a fully subordinate obbligato, also called countermelody (contramelodía), or a line with thematic weight equal to the primary one. Which is primary can be a matter of interpretation and taste.

**Rules**
- R20 [AVOID] Bringing a background voice forward when the composer meant it to stay behind. Piston criticises conductors who "bring out" such voices. For agents: do not raise a countermelody's level above the main melody (inferred application).
- R21 [PREFER] Contrast the two melodic elements in timbre, melodic outline and rhythm (Ex. 386: first violins against a woodwind obbligato).
- R22 [ALLOWED] The primary melody can dominate although it is outnumbered. Its strengths: a high register, energetic bowing, and having been heard first as the main element. In Ex. 386 the first violins beat five woodwinds placed in their best registers with octave doublings.
- R23 [ALLOWED] Hold back a woodwind obbligato and bring it in only after several bars of held wind chords (Ex. 386: after 7 bars).
- R24 [ALLOWED] Second violins can play two of the three accompanying harmonic voices as the only middle voices. They balance a bass doubled in octaves by violas, cellos and basses, which is very resonant (Ex. 386).
- R25 [MUST] Check the double-bass part against the low limit of a 4-string bass, sounding E1 (MIDI 28). In Ex. 386 four-string basses must play the low E♭1 (MIDI 27) an octave higher.
- R26 [PREFER] Give the three elements different rhythms. This lets a sustained, legato secondary melody stand out even when the main melody sounds both above and below it (Ex. 387: first violins inside the wind chords).
- R27 [AVOID] Underestimating the first violins: they hold their own against full winds ff (Ex. 387).
- R28 [ALLOWED] Unison doublings outweigh octave doublings. In a ff wind tutti spread over 4 octaves, the balance can lean to the lowest octave because horns ff carry great tone-weight (Fig. 113). In Ex. 387 the melody E sits in four octaves:
  - E6: flute I (1 player);
  - E5: flute II, oboes I/II, clarinet I (4 players);
  - E4: clarinet II, bassoon I, horn I (3 players);
  - E3: bassoon II, horn II (2 players). This octave dominates because of the horn.
  The octave numbers are inferred from the figure.
- R29 [PREFER] Use clarinets as the link between upper (flute/oboe) and lower (bassoon/horn) colors in a multi-octave doubling (Ex. 387).
- R30 [ALLOWED] Move both horns to the upper octave to share a stopped note (written E♭ for horn in E, sounding G4) rather than give horn II a poor stopped note in the lower octave (written D) (Ex. 387, m.5). [era: natural horn, pre-1840]
- R31 [ALLOWED] Divide the cellos so that some double the bass and some double the violas (Ex. 387).
- R32 [ALLOWED] Trumpets and timpani punctuate tonic and dominant in the Classical way (Ex. 387). [era: Classical natural trumpets/timpani]
- R33 [ALLOWED] The secondary melody can be the same tune in canon. In Ex. 388 it is a canon at the octave one beat behind. The first entry gets slight precedence.
- R34 [PREFER] All-unison doubling adds weight. In Ex. 388 element A (violas, cellos, bassoons) is in true unison, while element B is split in octaves (violin I + flute I above, violin II + oboe + English horn below).
- R35 [PREFER] Cellos on the A string (from A3, MIDI 57, upward) are powerful for a melody; support them with bassoons and violas. Violas doubling in unison there are not in their bright register (their A string starts at A4, MIDI 69). They add warmth and body, not brilliance (Ex. 388).
- R36 [ALLOWED] A "percussive" accompaniment can be built by harmonically coloring an unpitched figure. In Ex. 388 (Fig. 114):
  - tambourine on the beat, paired with trumpet I, cornet I and horn IV in the treble and with trumpet II, cornet II and timpani in the bass;
  - triangle on the 16th afterbeats, paired with higher instruments (flute II, clarinets, cornets, horns I and III) and timpani.
  Match the register of the pitched doublings to the apparent pitch of the percussion: the triangle sounds higher than the tambourine.
- R37 [PREFER] Mark that accompaniment one level below the rest (brass and percussion p, others mf) (Ex. 388).
- R38 [PREFER] Give the basses a tenuto (sustained) line under a dry, detached accompaniment. It binds the texture and prevents dryness (Ex. 388).
- R39 [ALLOWED] A secondary melody can be a stream of parallel chords (parallel 6/4 chords in the strings) instead of a single line (Ex. 389).
- R40 [ALLOWED] Double a melody two octaves up with piccolo at a clearly lower dynamic than the main instrument (English horn pp, piccolo ppp) (Ex. 389).
- R41 [ALLOWED] Use different dynamic markings per element to create planes of orchestral sound. Different harmonic planes strengthen the effect (Ex. 389).
- R42 [PREFER] Combine rhythmic patterns among the accompaniment subelements: woodwind chords, timpani, and horns + basses (Ex. 389). This adds interest both to the accompaniment and to the overall rhythm.

**Examples**
- Ex. 386 Mozart, Symphony in E♭, K.543, ii (Andante, f): violins I carry the melody with dotted rhythms and triplets. Flute, clarinets and bassoons play a legato obbligato in octaves. Violins II divided, violas, and cellos+basses play repeated-note harmony and the bass (R21–R25).
- Ex. 387 Beethoven, Symphony no. 7, ii (Allegretto, ff): full winds and horns play the theme *ten.*/staccato in four octaves. Violins I play a sustained legato countermelody. Violins II, violas and divided cellos+basses add a triplet-pickup accompaniment, and trumpets and timpani punctuate (R26–R32).
- Ex. 388 Berlioz, *Roman Carnival* Overture (Andante sostenuto, 3/4, A major): an octave canon between low unison strings+bassoons and high violins/winds. The accompaniment is 16th-note percussion figures doubled by brass, cornets and timpani at p (R33–R38).
- Ex. 389 Milhaud, *Second Symphonic Suite* (Modéré):
  - English horn pp melody, doubled by piccolo ppp two octaves up;
  - strings p in parallel 6/4 chords;
  - flutes, oboes and clarinets pp in a repeated rhythmic pattern, with timpani pp;
  - horns and basses ppp;
  - harp and celesta for occasional color (R39–R42).

**Agent applicability**
- For a countermelody, first pick a contrasting timbre family and a contrasting rhythm (sustained against moving). Then set the countermelody's dynamic (CC1/CC11) about one step below the main melody.
- To decide balance in octave doublings, count unison players per octave and weight horns and trumpets heavily. An octave with 2 horns ff can outweigh one with 4 woodwinds.
- In bass parts, check notes below E1 (MIDI 28). Raise them an octave unless a 5-string or extension bass is assumed.
- For a festive percussive groove, double tambourine and triangle hits with short brass and woodwind chord stabs at p. Put the triangle doubles higher than the tambourine doubles, and hold a tenuto bass under it.
- Layer dynamics by element (e.g. melody pp, its piccolo double ppp, accompaniment pp/ppp) to get depth planes.

## Ch. 22 Types of Texture — Type IV, Part Writing (scan s191L–s192R, printed p.382–385)

**Concepts**
- Part writing (escritura a partes / textura de partes reales): a texture of several voices of about equal standing. The melody is just one of them. It differs from Type II, where melody and accompaniment are two distinct elements (the "Type II" reading is inferred; the scan is cut).
- Real parts (partes reales): the basic voices of the harmony, as opposed to parts that are doublings or octave reduplications of them.

**Rules**
- R43 [ALLOWED] Spread four real parts across a large wind section with unison and octave doublings. In Ex. 390 Franck uses 13 winds:
  - A (melody): flute I; an octave lower oboe I + clarinet I; another octave lower bassoon I + horn I;
  - B: flute II; an octave lower English horn + clarinet II (later oboe II);
  - C: bassoon II + horn II (later horn III);
  - D (bass): bass clarinet alone.
- R44 [ALLOWED] Instruments may change parts in mid-phrase. In Ex. 390, m.3:
  - the English horn moves from B to C, and oboe II finishes B;
  - bassoon I and horn I leave A and form a new voice ending on C♯, so the cadence has five real parts;
  - bassoon II and horn II move from C to B, and horn III finishes C.
  *Why:* some instruments can then make a better diminuendo, and the cadential harmony is richer.
- R45 [ALLOWED] Strings can add a decorative figure at a cadence and then join one of the real parts (violas in Ex. 390).
- R46 [MUST] When balancing, count only unison doublings as extra weight. Octave doublings add little to a part's tone-weight.
- R47 [ALLOWED] An uneven distribution can work. In Ex. 390 the melody is heavily doubled and the bass is a lone bass clarinet; it sounds organ-like if played with care for balance.
- R48 [PREFER] Write three or four real parts, the most common number. Two is possible (Ex. 391).
- R49 [ALLOWED] Two real parts can sound full when each is doubled in octaves (Ex. 391):
  - A: violins I + oboes I/II, with violins II an octave lower;
  - B: violas + cellos, with basses an octave lower.
- R50 [ALLOWED] Natural horns and trumpets can seem to play the real parts when they only play the notes of those parts they have. In Ex. 391 (C major) they cannot play C♯, B, A or F♯ without stopped horn notes, which were apparently not thought good enough. They add one note of their own, G, an open fifth on the dominant. That open fifth is a Classical formula for horns and trumpets. [era: natural horns/trumpets, Classical]

**Examples**
- Ex. 390 Franck, Symphony in D minor (Poco più lento, pp, B major): four-part wind choir with octave doublings. Fig. 115 gives the four real parts. Parts are swapped in the cadence bar, the violas add a pp decoration, and there is a fermata (R43–R47).
- Ex. 391 Mozart, Symphony in C, K.200, Menuetto (f): oboes a2 and violins I carry the tune with violins II an octave lower. Violas and cellos/basses play the second part in octaves. Horns and trumpets in C fill in with open notes (Fig. 116) (R48–R50).
- Ex. 392 Beethoven, Symphony no. 5, ii (Andante con moto, A♭): strings start p *dolce* (violins I and violas), then crescendo to f/sf with sf chords in the winds, then drop to p/pp. The commentary is on p.386, outside this range.

**Agent applicability**
- For chorale-like wind writing, define 3–5 real parts as abstract voices first, then map instruments to them with unison doublings where you want weight. Octave doublings add color but little weight.
- In a cadence, you may move instruments between voices or add a fifth voice. Do this so instruments that taper well carry the diminuendo.
- When the brass is "natural" in style (period pastiche), give it only the chord tones it could play, plus tonic–dominant open fifths. Let woodwinds and strings take the chromatic notes. [era: Classical]
