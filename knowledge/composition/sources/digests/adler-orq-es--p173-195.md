## 6 Introduction and construction (scan p.173–174, printed p.164–165)

**Concepts**
- Woodwind section (sección de madera): the most heterogeneous orchestral family; tuning among winds is hard and
  only the best players master all blends of their varied timbres.
- "Woodwind" is a label, not a material: flutes are metal (even gold/silver/platinum), cheap clarinets plastic,
  saxophones (19th-c. invention) always metal but grouped with the woodwinds. (Forsyth 1914 still put the sax
  under brass.)
- Sound production: the air column in a conical or cylindrical tube radiates the sound directly; no resonating
  body is needed (contrast with strings).
- Boehm system (Theobald Boehm, 1794–1881): mechanical key/lever system that made all chromatic notes easy
  to finger. Today any woodwind can play any leap, trill or tremolo (in principle; see flute exceptions below).

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] Know how each instrument produces sound (reed type, bore shape) to predict its timbre, register,
  projection, dynamic range, agility and articulation.
- R2 [ALLOWED] Write leaps, trills and tremolos for any modern woodwind; check per-instrument exceptions (flute
  trills/tremolos, scan p.194–195). [era: post-Boehm keywork, mid-19th c. onward]

## Classification of the woodwinds (scan p.174–176, printed p.165–167)

**Concepts**
- Five classifications: by family, by reed, by bore shape, by first overblown harmonic, by transposition.
- Families (familias):
  - Flute: piccolo (flautín), flute, alto flute in G (flauta en Sol), bass flute (flauta bajo). Recorders
    (flauta dulce, sopranino–bass) are not used in the modern symphony orchestra.
  - Oboe: oboe, oboe d'amore, English horn (corno inglés), heckelphone (heckélfono), bassoon (fagot),
    contrabassoon (contrafagot).
  - Clarinet: clarinets in C, D, Eb, Bb and A; alto clarinet (Eb); bass clarinet (usually Bb, sometimes A);
    contrabass clarinet (Bb or Eb); basset horn (corno di bassetto, F). The translation conflates "alto
    clarinet" and "requinto"; requinto = small Eb/D clarinet.
  - Saxophone: sopranino (normally Eb), soprano (Bb), alto (Eb), tenor (Bb), baritone (Eb), bass (Bb).
- Embouchure (embocadura): how the player blows (directly for flutes, via reed/mouthpiece for the rest); the
  lips are the main pitch control. Pulling out the mouthpiece/headjoint lengthens the tube and lowers pitch;
  pushing it in raises pitch.
- By reed: no reed = all flutes (and recorders); single reed = clarinets and saxophones; double reed = oboe,
  oboe d'amore, English horn, heckelphone, bassoon, contrabassoon.
- By bore: cylindrical = flutes (open; strictly cylindro-conical) and clarinets (closed by the mouthpiece);
  conical = oboes, English horns, bassoons, saxophones.
- By first harmonic: conical instruments and flutes overblow at the octave (12 st); clarinets overblow at the
  twelfth (19 st).

**Agent applicability**
- Clarinet registers repeat at a 12th, all others at the octave: use this when estimating register breaks.

## Transposition (scan p.176–179, printed p.167–170)

**Concepts**
- Non-transposing: flute, oboe, bassoon.
- Fixed-interval transposers: piccolo, bass flute, contrabassoon, oboe d'amore in A (like A clarinet), English
  horn in F (like horn in F), alto flute in G, alto clarinet in Eb (like alto sax), bass clarinet in Bb,
  contrabass clarinet in Eb, soprano sax Bb (like Bb clarinet), alto sax Eb, tenor sax Bb (like bass
  clarinet), baritone sax Eb (like Eb contrabass clarinet), bass sax Bb.
- Variable transposers: the clarinet and the bass clarinet (several pitches exist).
- Written vs sounding pitch (sonido escrito / altura real); concert key = "orchestral key signature"
  (armadura orquestal). Basic rule: written C sounds the note the instrument is named after.

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] Score and parts must state exactly which clarinet / bass clarinet (Bb, A, Eb, D) is used.
- R2 [MUST] Key signatures for concert C major: Bb instrument → D major; A → Eb major; Eb → A major;
  D → Bb major; F → G major.
- R3 [ALLOWED] Almost all non-octave woodwind transposers sound lower than written, except the D and Eb
  requintos and (per source) the sopranino sax "in F" [?: elsewhere the source gives sopranino in Eb].
- R4 [MUST] Instruments named tenor, baritone or bass add one octave (two for some) to their transposition when
  written in treble clef.
- R5 [PREFER] Choose clarinet by key: flat keys → Bb clarinets and Eb requinto; sharp keys → A clarinets and D
  requinto. *Why:* fewer accidentals for the player. [era: tonal common practice; much less relevant since the
  chromatic writing of the late 19th c.]
- R6 [ALLOWED] Any clarinet choice in non-tonal/chromatic music. A and D clarinets sound slightly fuller than Bb
  and Eb, but the difference is barely audible.
- R7 [ALLOWED] Switching clarinets (Bb ↔ A) within a piece is rare today [era: pre-Boehm practice used
  several pitches to avoid accidentals]; when used, leave a long rest for the change (Copland, Appalachian
  Spring alternates Bb and A).
- R8 [ALLOWED] English horn, saxophones, alto and bass flutes, piccolos and contrabassoons always transpose by
  the same interval.

**Examples**
- Ex 6-1: written vs sounding pitch for Bb and A clarinets; written key signature for A clarinet vs concert key.
- Ex 6-2/6-3/6-4: same written melody for Bb then A clarinet sounds a semitone apart; to keep the same sounding
  pitch on both, the A-clarinet part must be written a semitone higher.

**Agent applicability**
- MIDI is always concert pitch: never apply these offsets to MIDI notes. Use the table only to read printed
  examples or to produce parts (sounding = written + semitones).
- Sample libraries for "Clarinet in A" still play concert pitch; the A/Bb choice only changes timbre slightly.

## Performance techniques: dynamics and registers (scan p.179, printed p.170)

**Rules**
- R1 [AVOID] Expecting easy control of pitch, dynamics, articulation, trills and agility in extreme registers;
  every woodwind is hardest at its extremes, the top more than the bottom.
- R2 [AVOID] Asking flute or piccolo for volume in their lowest octave. *Why:* very little volume there.
- R3 [NEVER] Write pianissimo for oboe or bassoon in their lowest fifth (lowest 7 semitones).
- R4 [ALLOWED] Clarinet: full dynamic range in all registers, top to bottom (the only woodwind that has it).

**Agent applicability**
- Flute in C4–B4 (60–71): keep CC1/velocity low expectations and thin the accompaniment; oboe/bassoon in their
  lowest fifth: no pp (CC1 floor ~ mp) (inferred mapping).

## Vibrato (scan p.179–180, printed p.170–171)

**Concepts**
- Four ways to make wind vibrato: (1) lips/jaw — normal on clarinet and sax, rare on oboe and bassoon, hardest
  and can disturb the embouchure; (2) throat — sometimes on flute; (3) abdominal muscles — normal on oboe and
  bassoon; (4) throat + abdominal combined — normal on flute.

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] Leave vibrato unmarked: professional players add it naturally; its width is a matter of taste.
- R2 [MUST] To remove it, mark "senza vibrato" / "non vibrato" / "white tone" (sonido blanco, Copland); to
  restore, mark "con vibrato" or "normal".

**Examples**
- Copland, Appalachian Spring, bars 1–4 (Very slowly, q=66): solo A clarinet, p semplice, "white" tone over
  silence in the rest of the winds — vibrato-less clarinet as an open, pure colour.

**Agent applicability**
- For SWAM/modelled winds, a "white tone" passage = CC1 vibrato depth ~0; default lyrical winds keep moderate
  vibrato (inferred).

**Tags:** #orchestration/woodwinds #performance/vibrato

## Articulation, tonguing and phrasing (scan p.180–182, printed p.171–173)

**Concepts**
- Tonguing (picado): note starts as the tongue leaves the palate/reed ("ta"; sometimes "da" depending on
  instrument, register, volume); note stops by returning the tongue, touching the reed, or stopping the air.
- Unslurred notes are each tongued; a slur = one breath, only its first note tongued (like one bow stroke).
  A wind player can play many more notes per breath than a string player per bow.
- "Legato" for winds = slurred in one breath, though not every one-breath passage is legato.
- Staccato: dot above/below the head → very short note with natural separation.
- Soft tonguing (picado suave): slur over repeated notes with dots or tenuto lines; dots = slightly harder
  than lines; equivalent to string slurred staccato and louré; done in one breath.
- Double tonguing (doble picado) for very fast passages, triple tonguing (triple picado) for fast triplets;
  syllables "te" and "ke" in combinations (te-ke, te-te-ke / te-ke-te).
- Dynamic "envelope" (envolvente dinámica): sfp, or sf > p < f (or the reverse): hard attack, immediate drop,
  optional re-swell. Not exclusive to winds but works best there.

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] Slur markings define tonguing: unslurred = detached; slurred = connected in one breath.
- R2 [PREFER] More slurs = smoother, softer character; mostly tongued = livelier.
- R3 [ALLOWED] Fast repeated notes and fast figures via double/triple tonguing on all woodwinds (flute best).
- R4 [ALLOWED] fp / sf>p<f envelopes on sustained wind chords.

**Examples**
- Beethoven, Symphony 8/i, bars 1–4: flute line as written (mostly tongued) vs a legato re-slurring — shows how
  much smoother slurs sound.
- Ex 6-7: Bb clarinet staccato passage (dots).
- Ex 6-8: oboe soft tonguing — dots and lines under slurs on repeated notes.
- Ex 6-9: flute Presto, double tonguing "teke teke".
- Mendelssohn, Symphony 4/i, bars 1–6: fl, ob, A cl, bsn repeated chords in 6/8, triple tonguing, fp then f —
  winds as a fast pulsing chord engine.
- Beethoven, Symphony 1/i, bars 1–4 (Adagio molto): fl, ob, C cl, bsn chords with fp, then cresc. to f — the
  fp envelope on wind chords.
- Ex 6-12: 2 flutes + 2 C clarinets sustained chord with sf > p < f.

**Agent applicability**
- MIDI: unslurred winds = gaps between notes (or short/staccato articulation); slurred = overlap (legato).
  Repeated 16ths/triplets at fast tempo are idiomatic (double/triple tongue).
- fp chords: velocity high + CC1/CC11 drop within ~1/8 beat, then swell for sf>p<f.

## Flutter tongue, mutes (scan p.183, printed p.174)

**Concepts**
- Flutter tongue (frullato / Flatterzunge / flutter tongue): rolled tongue or guttural "r"; buzzing sound;
  analogous to unmeasured string tremolo.

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] Flutter tongue: easy on all flutes, clarinets, saxophones; harder on oboes and bassoons but
  often used in 20th-c. repertoire. Usable on long notes or whole passages, slow or fast. [era: 20th c.]
- R2 [MUST] Notate like unmeasured string tremolo (three slashes through stems or on whole notes) and/or write
  "frullato" (Frull., Flttzg.) above the passage.
- R3 [ALLOWED] Muffled wind sound: no real mutes exist; player stuffs a cloth in the bell or covers the bell
  with the hand. [NEVER] on flute (no bell).

**Examples**
- Stravinsky, Rite of Spring, Part I "Mystic Circles of the Young Girls" at [103] (accelerando, 7/4): picc, fl
  1–3, alto fl, ob 1–3, Bb cl 1–3; flutter-tongued fl 3, alto fl and clarinets in fast quintuplet/sextuplet
  chromatic figures building to f — flutter as a mass texture.

**Tags:** #orchestration/woodwinds #orchestration/extended-techniques

## Multiphonics and other special effects (scan p.184–186, printed p.175–177)

**Concepts / Rules**
- Multiphonics (multifónicos): several notes at once; not all woodwinds/models can do them; mostly solo and
  chamber repertoire, some recent orchestral/band use. [era: late 20th c.]
- Microtones (microtonos): more common in orchestral winds, but very hard; some players know special
  fingerings. [era: late 20th c.]
- R1 [ALLOWED] Glissando: best on clarinet and sax, and only upward. [AVOID] descending glissando except between
  adjacent notes.
- R2 [ALLOWED] Flutes, oboes, bassoons (and clarinets) can bend a note slightly by embouchure (a soft
  glissando). [NEVER] for intervals larger than a 2nd.
- R3 [ALLOWED] Slap tongue (picado "slap", from jazz): very effective on clarinets and saxes, possible on flute;
  fast, over-articulated, lively attack.
- R4 [ALLOWED] Key clicks (chasquidos con las llaves): percussive, rhythmic, can include faint pitches; on flute
  heard close-up like a hard left-hand pizzicato. [PREFER] use very sparingly in orchestra. *Why:* very soft.
- R5 [ALLOWED] Air sound: blowing through the instrument without tone.
- R6 [ALLOWED] Whistle tones (pitidos, flute): instrument turned slightly away, blowing across the hole while
  fingering. Used by Hodkinson (Interplay) and Erb (Concerto for Solo Percussion).
- R7 [MUST] For all these techniques, describe the intended effect in words in score and parts. *Why:* no
  universal notation (see Kurt Stone, Music Notation in the Twentieth Century).

**Examples**
- Gershwin, Rhapsody in Blue, opening: solo Bb clarinet p trill in the low register, then a 17-note rising
  scale turning into a glissando to the high register [exact written notes ?] — the upward clarinet gliss.
  It is the only sound at the start (a true "solo").
- Copland, Music for the Theatre, [5]–[6] (Subito allegro molto, q=144): Eb clarinet solo ff "slap tongue"
  with accents, over accented bassoon ostinato and piccolo interjections.
- C. Polin, The Death of Procris, mvt 2, bars 33–36: flute key clicks in fast rhythm.
- Additional study pointer: Crumb, Echoes of Time and the River, mvt 2, bars 12–13.

**Agent applicability**
- Sample-library winds rarely do real glissandi: for a clarinet/sax rise use a fast chromatic/diatonic run into
  the target (Rhapsody model), never a downward gliss (inferred).

## The woodwind section of the symphony orchestra (scan p.186, printed p.177)

**Rules** (score order = list order)
- R1 Classical orchestra up to early Beethoven: 2 fl, 2 ob, 2 cl (clarinet pitch set by the key), 2 bsn.
  [era: Classical, to early Beethoven] (Some early Haydn symphonies, e.g. No. 22, used English horn; piccolo
  appeared in pre-Beethoven stage works.)

  [era: 19th c. after Beethoven]
- R3 Large late-19th/20th-c. orchestra: picc (sometimes 2, or one doubling alto flute), 3 fl, 3 ob, Eng hn,
  cl in C/D/Eb, 2–3 cl in Bb or A, bass cl (sometimes 2, or basset horn), 3 bsn, contrabsn.
  [era: late 19th–20th c.]
- R4 [MUST] Saxophones, when used, go between clarinets and bassoons in score order: soprano, alto, tenor,
  baritone, bass.

**Agent applicability**
- Pick the wind complement from the target era; order REAPER tracks in this score order.

## Orchestrating for woodwinds — score layout (scan p.187–188, printed p.178–179)

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] Know each instrument's technical and audible limits per register and how easily it handles scales
  and leaps.
- R2 [ALLOWED] Oboe can play longer phrases without breathing than other winds. *Why:* the oboist needs less
  air. Breath control varies by instrument.
- R3 [PREFER] Write 1st and 2nd of identical instruments on one staff; use separate staves when rhythm/
  complexity differ enough to confuse.
- R4 [PREFER] With three identical instruments: 1+2 on one staff, 3 on another; if 2 and 3 are more alike than
  1 and 2, put 1 alone and 2+3 together.
- R5 [MUST] Unison of two players on one staff: mark "a 2"; three: "a 3".
- R6 [MUST] Solo for one player on a shared staff: give rests to the other, or mark "1." / "2." (1º, 2º).
- R7 [NEVER] Use "divisi" in wind or brass parts (each wind player has their own part; divisi is string-only).
- R8 [ALLOWED] Mark "solo" to show a line is the most important gesture of the texture.

**Examples**
- Ex 6-17: 2 flutes on one staff, stems split.
- Bartók, Concerto for Orchestra/i, bars 233–236 (Tempo I, dotted q=88): cl a3, ob a3, bsn a2, fl a2 enter in
  turn ff on the same figure — section unisons marked a2/a3, building by staggered entries.
- Ex 6-19: two ways to separate parts on one staff (split stems, or "Fl. 1"/"Fl. 2" alternation).

**Agent applicability**
- In MIDI, an "a 2" passage is one line doubled; in sample libraries prefer one "a2"/section patch or two
  tracks slightly detuned/humanized rather than one solo patch at double velocity (inferred).

## The flute (flauta) — range and registers (scan p.189–193, printed p.180–184)

**Concepts**
- Flute (Flauto / Flûte / Flöte / Flute): the only woodwind without a reed; the most agile and sensitive.
- Range (concert, non-transposing): C4 (60) to C7 (96); B3 (59) on flutes with a B foot (most US professional
  flutes); top extension C#7 (97), D7 (98).
- B foot: some players say it helps C4 and adds resonance/depth; European players say it reduces brilliance.

**Register characteristics** (Ex 7-2, read from the image)
| Register | Notes | MIDI | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low | B3–F4 | 59–65 | weak but seductive (débil, pero seductor) |
| Lower-middle | G4–G5 | 67–79 | sweet, little projection (dulce, poca proyección) |
| Upper-middle | A5–G6 | 81–91 | clear and brilliant (claro y brillante) |
| Top | A6–C7 | 93–96 | somewhat strident (un poco estridente) |

**Rules**
- R1 [AVOID] Notes above A6 (93) unless necessary; they are hard. [PREFER] approach them by ascending scale.
- R2 [AVOID] Chromatic writing in the top fourth (~A6–D7) and bottom fourth (~B3/C4–F4); problematic,
  especially for non-professionals.
- R3 [MUST] For a low-register flute melody, keep the accompaniment very sparse/transparent so the flute is
  heard (sustained string background, slow harmonic rhythm, soft homophonic strings + horns, pizz pp con sord.).
- R4 [ALLOWED] Upper register (A5–G6) has brilliance and authority; flute agility (fast tongued staccato,
  ornaments, trills) shines there.
- R5 [MUST] Give the flautist time to breathe, especially after hard or long passages. *Why:* the flute uses a
  lot of air.
- R6 [PREFER] For very long sustained notes, overlap two flutes so one breathes while the other holds, blended
  to sound like a single flute.

**Examples**
- Dvořák, Symphony 9/i, bars 149–156: flute solo p in the low register; strings hold a sustained harmonic
  background and don't speed up the harmonic rhythm until bar 4 — clearing space for low flute.
- Brahms, Symphony 4/iv, bars 97–104 (3/4, mp, hairpins): long flute solo sweeping across registers down to
  the low register, accompanied softly and homophonically by strings and 2 horns.
- Study pointer: Debussy, Iberia, mvt 1 (4 bars before [33]) and mvt 2 (bars 5–9 after [46]).
- Tchaikovsky, Piano Concerto 1/ii, bars 5–12 (Db major, Andante semplice, p dolcissimo): flute melody over
  strings pizzicato pp con sordino — purest flute sound needs a very light backdrop.
- Debussy, Prélude à l'après-midi d'un faune, bars 21–24 (12/8, p, légèrement et expressif): flute solo
  essentially alone.
- Rossini, William Tell Overture, bars 209–224 (G major, 3/8): flute rapid tongued staccato figuration with
  grace notes and trills in the upper register over a legato dolce English horn melody — flute agility as
  obbligato above a lyrical line.
- Bizet, Carmen, Prelude to Act III, bars 1–13 (Eb major, pp): legato flute melody in middle/upper register.
- Study pointers: Beethoven, Leonora Overture 3, bars 328–352; Hindemith, Symphonic Metamorphosis/iii,
  bars 31–48.
- Brahms, Symphony 1/iv, bars 38–46 (Più andante): two flutes alternate the long notes with <f> swells so the
  first flute can breathe (bars 2, 4, 6) — overlapping parts heard as one flute.
- Piston, The Incredible Flutist at [E] (Lento, q=44, espress.): slow expressive solo with sextuplet runs,
  trills, mp–mf — the flute's intense slow-melody capacity.
- Study pointer: Shostakovich, Symphony 5/i, 1 bar after [39] to [41].

**Agent applicability**
- Flute lead in 59–71: drop other parts to pp, avoid strings/brass in the same octave, keep harmonic rhythm
  slow; in 81–91 the flute cuts through moderate textures.
- Keep flute lines mostly below 93; reach 93–96 by stepwise ascent and only at f.
- Long flute pedals: split between FL1/FL2 tracks with overlapping crossfades (CC11) every few bars.

## Flute: trills and tremolos (scan p.194–195, printed p.185–186)

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] Trills and tremolos are very common on flute.
- R2 [AVOID] These low trills (Ex 7-13a): C4–Db4 (60–61), Db4–Eb4 (61–63), C#4–D#4 (61–63), B3–C4 (59–60),
  B3–C#4 (59–61). Some flutes have an extra C#4 trill key to ease low trills.
- R3 [AVOID] Trills above G6 (91): difficult.
- R4 [AVOID] Tremolos to or from low B3 (59): weak. Ex 7-13b shows a low tremolo to avoid [≈ C4–D#4 ?].
- R5 [NEVER] In the lowest octave (≈ B3–C5), tremolos wider than a P5 (7 st).
- R6 [ALLOWED] In upper registers, tremolos of a P4 (5 st) are no problem.

**Agent applicability**
- Validator check: flute trill/tremolo with lower note ≤ 61 → flag; trill note > 91 → flag; tremolo interval >
  7 st with lower note < 72 → flag.

## Flute: harmonics (scan p.195, printed p.186)

**Concepts**
- All open notes above C#5 (73) are in fact harmonics (overblown), some with special fingerings in the top.
- Requested harmonics: pale/white sound; notated with a small circle over the note, as for strings; sound at
  written pitch. The player fingers the note an octave lower (or another partial) and overblows.

**Examples**
- Ravel, Daphnis et Chloé, Suite 1 "Nocturne", bars 5–11 (q=72, mf): flute solo ending on a high harmonic
  (circle).
- Ravel, Daphnis et Chloé ballet at [49] (Modéré, 6/8, p): fl 1–2 and alto flute play chords in harmonics
  (circles), then a flute run ff > pp.
- Study pointer: Stravinsky, Rite of Spring, Part II Introduction, bars 38–51.

**Agent applicability**
- Harmonic flute = "white", soft colour; in sample libraries use a flautando/airy or non-vibrato patch, low CC1
  (inferred).
