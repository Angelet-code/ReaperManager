## Ch. 14 The Trombone — construction and slide technique (scan s133R–s134R, printed p.267–269)

Names: Fr. *trombone*; It. *trombone*; Ger. *Posaune* (Sp. trombón).

**Concepts**
- Tenor trombone (trombón tenor) in 9-foot Bb: the standard, typical slide trombone, essentially unchanged
  for at least five centuries. Its bore is cylindrical for about 2/3 of its length, then conical toward a
  bell about 7 in. wide. The mouthpiece is a shallow bowl cup, closer to a large trumpet mouthpiece than a
  horn's.
- Slide positions (posiciones de vara) I–VII: the closed slide is position I. Each further position lowers
  the pitch by one semitone, so VII is a tritone (6 semitones) below I. Each position has its own harmonic
  series. Position I has the same tube length as the natural horn in Bb alto, but the trombone's bore and
  mouthpiece do not reach harmonics as high as the horn's.
- Fig. 82: the harmonic series of the seven positions. Fundamentals (pedal tones, notas pedal): I Bb1 (34),
  II A1 (33), III Ab1 (32), IV G1 (31), V Gb1 (30), VI F1 (29), VII E1 (28). Pedals IV–VII are printed in
  parentheses. Upper partials run to the 10th harmonic, e.g. position I: Bb2, F3, Bb3, D4, F4, Ab4, Bb4, C5,
  D5 (partials 2–10; 10th = D5, 74); position VII: E2 (40), B2, E3, G#3, B3, D4, E4, F#4, G#4.
- Clefs: bass and tenor clef (tenor clef saves ledger lines up high). The trombone has always been
  non-transposing: notes sound as written.

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] Practical tenor trombone range (from Fig. 82): chromatic E2 (40) to D5 (74). Below E2 come only
  the pedal tones; B1–Eb2 (35–39) do not exist on the plain Bb tenor (inferred from the figure). Use a
  bass trombone (F attachment) for them.
- R2 [PREFER] The 9th and 10th partials (top notes, about C5–D5) are used mainly in positions I–II. In
  the lower-pitched series they are easier, but those pitches are better taken as lower partials in higher
  positions.
- R3 [AVOID] Notes above the symphonic range. Players reach the 12th harmonic (F5, 77, in position I), but
  that is not orchestral practice. *Why:* horns and trumpets already cover that register.
  [era: 1955 symphonic practice]
- R4 [ALLOWED] Pedal tones of positions I–III (Bb1, A1, Ab1 = 34, 33, 32) have good quality *if* the
  player has time to set the embouchure. Approach them after a rest or a slow lead-in.
- R5 [AVOID] Pedals of positions IV–VII (G1–E1, 31–28). The figure parenthesizes them as doubtful.
- R6 [AVOID] Exposing low E2 (40), the 2nd harmonic in VII. It is the poorest note in resonance.
- R7 [AVOID] Fast alternation between distant slide positions, especially VII↔I. Many notes, mainly in the
  low octave, have only one position.
- R8 [ALLOWED] Intonation: the slide has none of the tuning problems of valve combinations. Pitch can be
  adjusted to perfection, which suits pure chords and sustained harmony.

**Examples**
- Ex. 292. Bartók, *Dance Suite* (Trb II, Comodo, p con sord.): a chromatic oscillation around B2/Bb2 [?]
  that forces alternation between positions VII and I. It is cited as *very awkward* on tenor trombone.

**Agent applicability**
- Clamp tenor-trombone MIDI to 40–74. Allow 32–34 only as isolated, prepared pedal notes. Leave out 35–39
  unless the part is for bass trombone.
- In fast passages, avoid alternating B2 (47) with Bb2/C3 or similar pairs that need VII↔I jumps. Prefer
  lines whose neighbours share positions (inferred).

## Alto, bass and tenor-bass trombone (scan s134R–s135R, printed p.269–271)

**Concepts**
- Alto trombone (trombón alto) in Eb: a P4 (5 semitones) above the tenor, so its closed-position
  fundamental is Eb. It has the same technique as the tenor. Its parts were written in alto clef.
- Bass trombone in F (trombón bajo): a P4 below the tenor. England preferred G; Germany in the 19th c.
  widely used Eb. All were superseded in the 20th c. by the tenor-bass trombone, now loosely called "bass
  trombone".
- Tenor-bass trombone: a Bb tenor with an "F attachment". A loop of extra tubing, switched in by a
  left-thumb rotary valve, gives a fundamental F. The bass-trombone version has a larger bore, a bell of
  about 9.5 in., and an "E slide" on the F tubing that lowers F to E.
- Fig. 83, the low gap filler, all 2nd harmonics: tenor VII = E2 (40); on the F valve, I F2 (41), II E2
  (40), III Eb2 (39), IV D2 (38), V Db2 (37), VI C2 (36). With the E slide pulled: B1 (35). Then the tenor
  pedal Bb1 (34). On F the slide has only six positions because the distances between positions grow.

**Rules**
- R9 [era: until late 19th c.] The orchestral trombone section was alto, tenor, bass. To evoke Classical or
  early-Romantic writing (Mozart, Beethoven, Schubert, Schumann), voice trombone I as an alto, with the top
  around the alto's range (inferred).
- R10 [MUST] [era: 20th c.] Standard section: 2 tenors + 1 bass. When a score just says "three trombones",
  the third is a bass trombone.
- R11 [ALLOWED] [era: French practice] French composers favoured three tenor trombones and avoided the
  bass.
- R12 [ALLOWED] [era: Russian practice] Alto clef for trombones I–II, even when no alto trombone is
  intended. It is a habit inherited from alto-trombone parts.
- R13 [PREFER] Old alto-trombone notes too high for the tenor are usually given to a trumpet today
  (reference to an example number [?], unreadable at the page edge).
- R14 [ALLOWED] The bass (tenor-bass) trombone covers continuous chromatic notes down to B1 (35), plus the
  pedal Bb1 (34). Its F attachment gives alternative positions across the whole range: "two or three
  instruments in one", comparable in importance to the double horn.

**Agent applicability**
- Bass trombone MIDI floor: 35 chromatic, 34 as pedal. The lowest notes are slower to speak (see Tonguing).
- For a period-authentic Classical section, use an alto trombone (or tenor trombone I kept high) + tenor
  + bass. For modern film or symphonic writing, use 2 tenors + bass, usually with a tuba.

## Tonguing, flutter-tongue, legato, breath (scan s135R–s137L, printed p.271–274)

**Rules**
- R15 [ALLOWED] All tonguings (single, double, triple) work. *But:* the large mouthpiece makes long fast
  tonguing tiring.
- R16 [AVOID] Expecting crisp articulation in the low register. It is a little sluggish, and a certain
  heaviness is normal.
- R17 [ALLOWED] Flutter-tonguing (frullato, *Flatterzunge*). Modern scores have a few examples.
- R18 [MUST] A perfect slurred legato happens only between two adjacent harmonics in the same position.
  A slur that changes position needs coordinated tongue and slide. Done well, the tiny break that avoids a
  portamento is barely audible.
- R19 [PREFER] Cantabile trombone style: a soft tongue on every note of the melody, not slurs. The notes
  stay as connected as a smooth string bow change, broken only at breaths.
- R20 [MUST] The trombone needs more wind than trumpet or horn. Phrase with frequent breathing places.

**Examples**
- Ex. 293. Wagner, *Götterdämmerung* (Trb I tenor clef, II, III, contrabass trb; Sehr lebhaft 3/4, ff):
  rapid staccato triplets and 8ths. The heaviness is expected. In the score, strings and partly horns
  double the trombones.
- Ex. 294. Schoenberg, *Five Orchestral Pieces* (4 trbs + bass tuba, Sehr rasch 3/8, pp *mit Dämpfer*):
  trbs I–II play fast repeated chord figures while III–IV and tuba sustain. It ends ff with
  *Flatterzunge* chords: muted flutter-tongue in a modern texture.
- Ex. 295. Schumann, *Symphony No. 3* (alto, tenor, bass trombones; Feierlich, pp, Eb minor): a slurred,
  chorale-like contrapuntal passage. It shows trombone legato in an alto/tenor/bass section.
  [era: Romantic alto-tenor-bass section]
- Ex. 296. Hindemith, *Mathis der Maler* symphony (Trb I–III a 3, Ruhig bewegt, mp *sanft hervortreten*):
  a unison chorale melody in long dotted values. It shows the choral-type trombone melody and the breathing
  it allows.

**Agent applicability**
- Trombone legato in MIDI: with a legato-capable patch, overlap only where the line would be slurred. For
  cantabile lines, use short note gaps (about 0.02–0.05 beat) with soft attack velocity, giving tongued
  legato (inferred).
- Insert a breath gap (rest of ≥ 1/8 beat at the tempo) at least every 2–4 bars in sustained trombone
  lines (inferred from "frequent breathing places").
- Low-register fast tonguing: thin the rhythm or double with strings. Don't expect clean 16ths below about
  F2 (inferred).

## Trills and glissando (scan s137L–s137R, printed p.274–275)

**Rules**
- R21 [MUST] Trombone trills are lip trills between adjacent harmonics only, so they are limited to a
  major-second trill (2 semitones). In practice, only where adjacent partials are a whole step apart
  (upper partials; inferred).
- R22 [ALLOWED] Slide glissando (glissando de vara): intervening pitches are heard as the slide moves.
  Use it as a special effect, like the string glissando, and sparingly ("easily abused").
- R23 [NEVER] A slide glissando wider than an augmented 4th (6 semitones, the total I→VII travel).
- R24 [NEVER] A slide glissando that needs a change of harmonic number. Fig. 85: G2→C3 is *impossible*
  on the Bb tenor, because it must leave the 2nd harmonic (IV, III, II, I = G2, G#2, A2, Bb2) for the 3rd
  (VII, VI = B2, C3). The same gliss is *good* on the F attachment, where positions VI→I stay on one
  harmonic series.
- R25 [ALLOWED] Harmonic glissando: a rip up and down the overtone series of a single position, like the
  horn glissando.

**Examples**
- Ex. 297. Stravinsky, *L'Oiseau de feu* (Trbs I–II, Allegro 3/4, sfff/ff): repeated slide glissandi
  between held notes (ff → sfff), then two lip trills, both playable in position IV.
- Ex. 298. Bartók, *Violin Concerto* (Trb III/bass, dotted half ≈ 56, f): harmonic glissandi sweeping up
  and down the overtone series of position IV, then of position VI.

**Agent applicability**
- To build a trombone gliss in MIDI with a pitch-bend ramp: check that the span is ≤ 6 semitones and that
  start and end lie in the same partial of one position. Otherwise split it or use the bass trombone F
  side (inferred).
- Write trombone trills only as whole-step trills, and avoid them in the low register (inferred).

**Tags:** #orchestration/brass #instruments/trombone #glissando #trill

## Tone and registers (scan s137R–s138R, printed p.275–277)

**Rules**
- R26 [ALLOWED] The tone is homogeneous over the whole range and grows naturally in brilliance and
  penetration toward the top.
- R27 [PREFER] Low register: a very soft, subdued pp is possible. It is especially effective in chords for
  three trombones.
- R28 [ALLOWED] Low register ff: dramatic, crashing, with huge crescendo power. Uncontrolled, the metal
  vibrates and you get a *cuivré* (brassy, rattling) sound. AVOID it by default, because the exaggerated
  brassiness is usually unpleasant. ALLOWED when it fits.
- R29 [ALLOWED] Middle register: closest to the horn in timbre, but more solid, even harder.
- R30 [PREFER] Three trombones in unison, pp, middle register, give a full, round tone without loudness.
- R31 [AVOID] ff unison of three trombones unless you want domination. It is overpowering, more so higher
  up, and can easily cover the whole orchestra.
- R32 [AVOID] High register softer than mf with average orchestral players. Extreme high notes suggest
  tension and excitement.

**Examples**
- Ex. 299. Debussy, *La Mer* (Trbs I–III, Animé, p e dim. molto → pp → ppp): close soft three-part chords
  fading to a unison a 3. These are the subdued soft trombone chords.
- Ex. 300. Sibelius, *Symphony No. 1* (Trbs I–III, Allegro energico, marcato, f cresc. molto → ff → cresc.
  possibile → fff): accented chords building to fff. It shows the crashing low/middle ff and crescendo power.
- Ex. 301. Schubert, *Symphony No. 7* (old numbering; the "Great" C major, inferred) (alto-tenor + bass
  trombones, Allegro ma non troppo, pp): an unison melodic line. It shows the round pp unison.
  [era: alto-tenor-bass section]
- Ex. 302. Rimsky-Korsakov, *Scheherazade* (Trbs a 3, Allegro non troppo maestoso, ff): a unison theme.
  It shows the overpowering ff unison.
- Ex. 303. Florent Schmitt, *Antoine et Cléopâtre* (Trbs I–II tenor clef, III, Animé, f cresc. → ff):
  chromatic high-register chords and figures. It shows high-register tension.

**Agent applicability**
- Velocity/CC1 by register (inferred): low chords can sit at pp (CC1 ~25–40). For high-register notes
  (above about F4 = 65), do not ask for less than mf (CC1 ≥ ~70). A soft high trombone sounds false.
- A unison of 3 trombones at ff is a "lead over everything" device. Use it only for climactic themes, and
  lower the other sections' dynamics if the trombones are not meant to dominate.

**Tags:** #orchestration/brass #instruments/trombone #timbre #dynamics #balance

## Mutes (scan s138R, printed p.277)

**Rules**
- R33 [ALLOWED] Muted trombones (con sordina, *mit Dämpfer*) both reduce volume and give a new colour.
- R34 [ALLOWED] Muted trombone in forte is less piercing than muted trumpet.
- R35 [ALLOWED] Muted trombones in the low register sound sinister.

**Examples**
- Ex. 304. Berg, *Violin Concerto* (Trb I tenor, Trb II bass, *mit Dämpfer*, Grazioso 2/4, p/mp/fp): light
  muted lines. In the score, the muted trombone is doubled in unison by low strings *col legno*, a novel
  colour.

**Agent applicability**
- For a dark, ominous low colour, use a muted trombone articulation/patch in about 36–52 (inferred), at
  low dynamics. Doubling it with low strings col legno gives a dry, percussive menace.

## Orchestral uses (scan s138R–s140L, printed p.277–280)

**Rules**
- R36 [PREFER] Trombones are group instruments, not soloists. Most often write them in three-part harmony
  as a harmonic background, like the horns.
- R37 [PREFER] In big tutti the horns are too light to hold the harmony. Either combine trombones with the
  horns, or give the harmony to the trombones and free the horns for melody.
- R38 [PREFER] Trombones regularly go with the tuba in one of two ways:
  (a) four-part writing with the tuba as bass;
  (b) three-part trombone harmony with the bass doubled by the tuba at the octave or unison.
- R39 [ALLOWED] All three trombones may join the tuba on the bass line.
- R40 [ALLOWED] Trombone harmony sounds good in either open or close position.
- R41 [PREFER] Trombone melodies: deliberate dignity and solemnity, or choral-type tunes "sung by several
  voices". Give them to 2 or 3 trombones in unison, or in octaves with two on the upper part.
- R42 [ALLOWED] An intimate, personal melody for one solo trombone is much less common in symphonic music.
  Use it as a special colour.

**Examples**
- Ex. 305. Brahms, *Symphony No. 4* finale (Trbs I–III, Allegro energico e passionato, f): sustained
  three-part chords, some widely spaced. Woodwinds, horns and trumpets fully double them, yet the trombone
  tone dominates the phrase.
- Ex. 306. Mahler, *Symphony No. 3* (Trb I solo, Sehr getragen, *molto portamento*, p espress. … pppp
  *verklingend*; metre alternating 2/2–3/2): a long, recitative-like solo with slide portamenti and hairpins.
  It is the intimate solo type.
- Ex. 307. Hindemith, *Symphonic Metamorphosis* (Trbs I, II–III, half = 96, mp/mf with accents): the
  rhythmic, contrapuntal style, with independent accented lines. Horns, trumpets and tuba are omitted from
  the quote.
- Ex. 308. R. Strauss, *Till Eulenspiegel* (Trbs I–II, Sehr lebhaft 6/8): ff, poco rit., then p a tempo
  staccato chromatic 8ths. This is light trombone staccato.
- Ex. 309. Copland, *Piano Concerto* (Trp I in C + Trb I, both *con sord.*, half = 72, mf): an imitative
  duet between muted trumpet and muted trombone.
- Ex. 310. Debussy, *Ibéria* (Trbs I–II in parallel close chords, Trb III + tuba bass, Rubato, *très doux
  et très souple*, p): colourful parallel chords. In the score, oboes, English horn and clarinets double
  them an octave above.
- Ex. 311. Prokofiev, *Chout* (Trbs a 3, Andante gravissimo, ff *tenuto e pesante*): an unison with wide
  skips covering the whole trombone range, while the tuba plays an octave below. It ends in accented chords.

**Agent applicability**
- Default brass-chord layout (inferred from R36–R38): trombones on the three upper voices of the low
  brass chord, tuba on the root an octave below the bass trombone (or at unison). In tutti, horns take a
  countermelody or double the trombones' chord.
- Solemn or chorale melody: 2–3 trombones in unison (or octave with 2 on top), mp–f, tongued legato.
- Parallel soft trombone chords plus double reeds/clarinets an octave above give an "impressionist" colour
  (Ibéria model).

## Contrabass trombone and valve trombone (scan s140L–s140R, printed p.280–281)

**Concepts**
- Contrabass trombone (trombón contrabajo) in Bb: an octave below the tenor (fundamental Bb0 = 22, pedal
  inferred from the octave relation). Wagner used it in the *Ring* so the trombone family would not depend
  on the tuba for its low bass. Few others used it (Strauss *Elektra*, Schoenberg *Gurrelieder*).
- Italian contrabass trombone (Verdi): a *valved* instrument. Its part is usually played on the tuba.
- Valve trombone (trombón de pistones): a three-valve trombone. It is successful in bands but not used in
  the orchestra.

**Rules**
- R43 [AVOID] [era: 1955] The contrabass trombone. It is generally considered unsatisfactory because of
  its physical demands on the player. ALLOWED for a Wagnerian low trombone colour.
- R44 [PREFER] Give Verdi-type contrabass trombone parts to the tuba.
- R45 [AVOID] Valve trombone in the orchestra. The valves make fast figures easier but bring
  valve-intonation faults, and the instrument loses the slide, the trombone's defining feature.

**Examples**
- Ex. 312. Verdi, *Falstaff* (contrabass trombone, quarter = 63, ppp … p *morendo*): soft repeated and
  slurred notes in the extreme low register, around C#1–F1 [?]. It shows the pianissimo very-low bass.
- Ex. 293 (above) includes a contrabass trombone part in Wagner.

**Tags:** #orchestration/brass #instruments/contrabass-trombone #era/romantic

## Ch. 15 The Tuba — general and fourth valve (scan s141L–s141R, printed p.282–284)

Names: Fr. *tuba*; It. *tuba*; Ger. *Tuba*.

**Concepts**
- "Tuba" is an ambiguous instruction. Player and conductor choose among four or five instruments. A true
  bass tuba has existed only since about 1875, so tubists also play parts written for earlier small tubas
  or the ophicleide.
- Orchestral tube lengths: 9-ft Bb (euphonium/tenor tuba), 12-ft F, 14-ft Eb, 16-ft C, 18-ft Bb. Also
  French 8-ft C. Fundamentals (inferred from tube length): Bb1 (34), F1 (29), Eb1 (27), C1 (24), Bb0 (22).
- Construction: a widely conical bore (cylindrical only in the valves), a very wide bell and a large deep
  cup. These make the lowest notes, pedals included, easier.
- Valves: rotary or piston (player's choice). Three valves, plus usually a 4th, sometimes a 5th or even a
  6th. The first three are right-hand; the 4th is right or left; extra valves are left-hand.
- Fourth valve (cuarto pistón): lowers a P4 (5 semitones). Its main value is replacing 1+3, which
  corrects the pitch of the 1+3 and 1+2+3 combinations (worse on large instruments than on trumpet). It
  also simplifies some fingerings.
- Fifth valve: most often a P5 down, working like the 4th. Sixth valve: a semitone down, a fingering aid.
  Some players use fractional valve lengths or double tubas.
- Fig. 86, four-valve fingering on the 2nd harmonic of the small Bb tuba (euphonium): Bb2 (0), A2 (2), Ab2
  (1), G2 (1+2), Gb2 (2+3), F2 (4), E2 (2+4), Eb2 (1+4), D2 (3+4), Db2 (2+3+4), C2 (1+3+4), B1 (1+2+3+4),
  then the pedal Bb1.

**Rules**
- R46 [MUST] Tuba parts are written at sounding pitch (non-transposing).
- R47 [PREFER] Most tuba parts are simple bass parts, neither very high nor very low and without special
  technical features. That suits whatever tuba the player uses for everyday work.
- R48 [AVOID] Low notes that combine the 4th valve with others (below the 4th-valve note). They are
  hopelessly sharp unless the instrument compensates automatically. Example: B1 via 1+2+3+4 on the
  euphonium sounds about C2 uncompensated.

**Agent applicability**
- One "Tuba" track in MIDI stands for the F/Eb/C bass tuba. Use the ranges below for the type the style
  implies (Wagnerian = C tuba; general orchestral = F or C).
- Treat the notes just above the pedal on any tuba as risky (compensation-dependent). Don't write long
  exposed lines there (inferred).

**Tags:** #orchestration/brass #instruments/tuba #valves

## Tuba types and ranges (scan s142L–s142R, printed p.284–285)

**Concepts**
- Tenor tuba in 9-ft Bb (tuba tenor, euphonium, bombardino; Fr. *tuba basse, tuba ordinaire*; It. *tuba
  bassa, flicorno basso*; Ger. *Basstuba, Tenortuba*). Usually four valves, sometimes five. It has the
  same tube length as the tenor trombone and the Bb alto horn, but a much wider bore puts its natural range
  lower in the harmonic series. The euphonium is wider-bored than the baritone, a three-valve band
  instrument of the same length that suits higher parts better.
- Bass tuba in 12-ft F ("orchestral tuba in F", bombardon, bass tuba): the most general all-round
  orchestral tuba. Four or five valves.
- Bass tuba in 14-ft Eb ("Eb bass"): four or five valves. Some prefer it to the F tuba for its heavier tone
  and slight advantage in the low notes.
- Contrabass tuba in 16-ft C (CC tuba; Wagner's *Kontrabasstuba*, bass of the Wagner-tuba group): many
  players prefer it for general use.

**Rules** (ranges read from Figs. 87–90; parenthesized figure notes = optional/limit)
- R49 [ALLOWED] Tenor tuba in Bb (Fig. 87): pedals F1–Bb1 (29–34), with F1–G#1 (29–32) parenthesized as
  doubtful. Chromatic from B1 (35) to Bb4 (70, 8th partial). Harmonic landmarks: Bb2, F3, Bb3, D4, F4,
  (Ab4), Bb4. Individual players exceed the 8th partial, and the low pedal limit varies by player and
  instrument.
- R50 [ALLOWED] [era: French practice] The French tuba in 8-ft C (wide bore, six valves) can reach as low
  as the notes usually written for the larger tubas.
- R51 [ALLOWED] Bass tuba in F (Fig. 88): pedals D1, Eb1, E1, F1 (26–29), good in quality but difficult.
  With five valves, F1, E1 and Eb1 can be produced "as second harmonics", without pedal technique.
