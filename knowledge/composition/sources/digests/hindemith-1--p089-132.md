## Chapter XII — Secondary dominants (Dominantes secundarias) (scan p.089–094, printed p.82–87)

**Concepts**
- secondary / auxiliary dominant (dominante secundaria, dominante auxiliar): a major triad (less often minor) or a seventh chord — preferably of dominant-seventh type (major triad + minor 7th) — placed before a chord so that it acts as that chord's dominant. It contains notes foreign to the key (= chromatic alterations of scale notes).
- artificial leading-tone chord (VII / VII6 / VII7 "artificial"): a chord built on a note that acts as leading tone (semitone below) to the root of the following chord; gives the same "auxiliary dominant" effect without being a literal V of the target.

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] Any major or minor chord other than the tonic may be emphasized by a secondary dominant: in major II, III, IV, V, VI; in minor IV, V, VI. *Why:* the chord gets a local dominant→tonic pull.
- R2 [ALLOWED] Any major or minor chord obtained by alteration (e.g. Neapolitan II6, altered VI, altered III) may also be preceded by its own secondary dominant.
- R3 [PREFER] Use a dominant-seventh-type chord (major triad + minor 7th, 4 notes: root, M3=4 st, P5=7 st, m7=10 st above root) as the secondary dominant; a plain major triad is next; a minor triad as "dominant" is less frequent.
- R4 [ALLOWED] The key's own V7 may be preceded by its secondary dominant (V of V).
- R5 [ALLOWED] The secondary-dominant effect can also be produced by an artificial VII, VII6, VII6/4[?] or VII7 (altered or not, root position or any inversion) built on the leading tone (semitone below) of the next chord's root.
- R6 [MUST] The secondary dominant resolves to its target as a normal dominant: its 3rd (temporary leading tone) rises a semitone to the target root; its 7th (if present) falls by step (inferred from the standard V7 resolution taught in earlier chapters and the exercise realizations).

**Examples**
- Exercise 55 (drill): play V–I, V7–I, IV–I, VI–I, III–I, altered VI–I, II–I, Neapolitan II6–I in many major and minor keys and positions, each first chord preceded by its auxiliary dominant. Model in F# minor: [aux. dominant of G] → G/B (Nap. II6) → F#–A–C# (I). Bass of model approx. [?]–B2–(A#)–C#3; soprano ends on C#5 [partly illegible].
- Exercises 56–57: figured-bass melodies and melodies-with-bass in C, G, Ab/F minor, D, Bb, Eb, E minor etc. Figures that signal secondary dominants recur: "#" (raised 3rd = temporary leading tone), "#6/5", "#4/2", "b7", "♮6/5", "#5/#". They teach spotting where a chromatic bass/alto note implies a secondary dominant of the next chord.

**Agent applicability**
- To intensify arrival on any diatonic non-tonic chord X, insert V7/X (root a P5 above X's root, major 3rd, minor 7th) immediately before X.
- Alternative: insert a diminished/half-diminished chord whose root is a semitone below X's root (vii°7/X).
- In minor, valid secondary-dominant targets per Hindemith: iv, V, VI (and altered chords such as Nap6).

## Chapter XIII — Other types of alteration (Otros tipos de alteración) (scan p.095–105, printed p.88–98)

**Concepts**
- altered dominant seventh (acorde de séptima de dominante alterado): V7 (and inversions) changed by (a) enharmonic respelling of the 7th, (b) enharmonic respelling of the 5th, (c) replacing the 5th with a note a semitone above or below it. Hindemith tabulates 12 forms built on C (original: C–E–G–Bb). Readable forms:
  - No.1 original C E G Bb.
  - No.2 C E G A# = "augmented 6/5" = German sixth (sexta alemana) (7th respelled).
  - No.5 C E F# A# = "augmented 4/3" = French sixth (sexta francesa) (5th lowered to F# and 7th respelled).
  - No.9 C E G# Bb = dominant seventh with augmented (raised) 5th.
  - No.11 C E Ab Bb (inferred: raised 5th spelled as Ab) and No.12 C E Gb Bb (lowered 5th) — the flat-side spellings of 9 and 5.
  - Nos. 3, 4, 10 combine (a)/(b)/(c) with double sharps/sharps [exact spellings not reliably legible].
  - Nos. 6, 7, 8: not used as regular chords because they contain doubly augmented or doubly diminished intervals.
- Italian sixth (sexta italiana): 3-voice reduction of No.2 (C E A#, i.e. omit the 5th). Other 3-voice reductions: of No.5 (C F# A#) and of No.9 (C E G# = augmented triad with dominant function).
- Footnote fact: the augmented triad C–E–G# derived from No.9 is a dominant chord; the unaltered augmented triad of Chapter IX is III of minor — same sound, different function.

**Rules (resolving altered dominant sevenths)**
- R1 [MUST] Resolve to a major or minor chord (or its 6 chord) written in regular form (no augmented/diminished interval), reached without chromatic or enharmonic connection, by one of two methods:
- R2 [MUST] Method (a): resolve the diminished 5th inward to a major/minor 3rd (or the augmented 4th outward to a major/minor 6th); the note reached by the semitone ascent is the root of the resolution chord; complete it with the remaining two voices. Nos. 5 and 12 allow more than one solution (they contain two tritones). Examples (inferred from rule): No.2 C E G A# → B major (A#→B, E→D#); No.5 C E F# A# → B major; No.9 C E G# Bb → F major (E→F, Bb→A).
- R3 [ALLOWED] Method (b): as (a), but replace the resolution root by a note a major or minor 3rd lower (deceptive-cadence logic, "cadencia rota"), treating one of the remaining notes of the dominant as leading tone (Nos. 3, 5, 11, 12) or as subdominant (No. 2).
- R4 [MUST] If other resolutions are used, they must span more than two chords so the tonal meaning becomes clear.
- R5 [ALLOWED] In progressions containing these altered chords, the ear is fairly insensitive to consecutive fifths (e.g. German sixth → V parallel 5ths), because attention goes to the complex tonal relation.
- R6 [PREFER] Inversions of these chords are often more readable when introduced by chromatic progressions; Nos. 2, 5, 12 are also used in original form/inversions as written.
- R7 [PREFER] After No.2 (German) and No.5 (French), No.9 (augmented-5th dominant) is the most frequent; it appears almost always with the augmented 5th in the top voice (e.g. G#4/G#5 in soprano rising to A). Two of its inversions are used the same way.
- R8 [ALLOWED] 3-voice forms (Italian sixth etc.) resolve exactly like the 4-voice chords they come from.

**Diminished seventh alterations**
- R9 [ALLOWED] VII7 of minor (diminished seventh, stacked minor 3rds, 3+3+3 st) can resolve directly to four different major or minor chords (or inversions): each chord tone in turn is treated as leading tone (semitone below) of the next chord's root. E.g. G#–B–D–F → A (G#→A), → C (B→C), → Eb (D→Eb), → F#/Gb (F→F#) with respellings.
- R10 [ALLOWED] The upper 3rd above that leading tone may be replaced by a diminished 4th (e.g. leading tone B, D replaced by Eb), giving a chord like V 11/6 (i.e. a dominant with b13): example B3–F4–Ab4–Eb5 → C minor (C–Eb–G–C).
- R11 [MUST] In that form the new note (the diminished-4th note) must be in the top voice.
- R12 [NEVER] Double the note acting as leading tone — especially in forms with an omitted note or in more than four voices — despite the freedom these dominant-type sevenths give.

**General**
- R13 [ALLOWED] Any chord containing a diminished 5th / augmented 4th is especially open to alteration; there are too many to classify, so build them as needed by their structure (some, like one augmented-sixth-type chord shown resolving to a G-based chord [details illegible], have a fixed place in tonal classification).
- R14 [AVOID] Excess alteration: every note can be altered, which multiplies notations, not new sounds. Reduce complex altered chords to the familiar alterations by reading them through leading-tone technique instead of chromatic notation. *Why:* prevents chaotic dissolution of tonality.
- R15 (pedagogy fact) Figured bass becomes awkward when figures/accidentals stand for three chord notes and useless when they stand for four; then staff notation is simpler.

**Examples**
- Exercise 58: figured-bass melodies in F (6/4, "Moderado") and A minor/C ("Lento") with figures like #6/5, #6/4/3, #6/4/2, b7/5, 9/b7, #6/b5, ♮5/b4/b2, b10/b7, #7/♮5, 13/7/#, 13/#12, 10/7/x3 — drills for German/French/augmented-dominant chords and diminished-seventh respellings.
- Exercise 59: melodies with bass only, in D, A, Eb (very chromatic, double flats), C 7/8 — student supplies altered harmonies.

**Agent applicability**
- German sixth in key X minor: build Dom7 on b6 of X (bass b6, then 3, 5, #4 spelled as aug 6th) → resolve to V of X (bass falls a semitone, aug-6th voice rises a semitone). Parallel fifths here are tolerated.
- French sixth: b6–1–2–#4 over bass b6 → V.
- Augmented dominant: V7 with #5 in the soprano, #5 rises a semitone to the 3rd of I.
- Diminished seventh = pivot to 4 keys: pick which chord tone is the leading tone; target root is a semitone above it.
- Never double any voice that functions as a leading tone in these chords.

## Chapter XIV — Modulation I (Modulación I) (scan p.106–115, printed p.99–108)

**Concepts**
- modulation (modulación): moving from one key to another.
- origin key / target key (tonalidad de origen / de destino).
- cadence (cadencia): in its shortest form, three chords of a key, the last always the tonic; the simplest way to establish a key.
- plagal cadence (cadencia plagal): subdominant precedes tonic.
- half cadence / Phrygian cadence (semi-cadencia, cadencia frigia): goal is the dominant, not the tonic.
- pivot zone (zona intermedia / eje): chord or group of chords with meaning in both keys.
- "terrace" modulation (modulación tipo "terraza"): abrupt juxtaposition of keys without pivot zone.

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] A clear modulation expresses each key purely and unambiguously; do not approach the new key before the original is firmly established.
- R2 [PREFER] Establish a key with a cadence ending on I; the most decisive (and most common) cadences have V immediately before I. The chord before V may be on any scale degree.
- R3 [AVOID] Plagal cadences (IV→I) when the strongest cadential effect is required; they are less decisive.
- R4 [MUST] (principle) The more distant the relation of the penultimate chord to the tonic, the less decisive the cadence.
- R5 [ALLOWED] Half (Phrygian) cadence: in minor the V is usually preceded by the subdominant 6 chord (iv6, bass b6→5); in major by the altered (minor) subdominant 6 chord (iv6 borrowed). E.g. C minor: Ab bass (iv6) → G (V, "♮" = major).
- R6 [AVOID] Using a half cadence to define a key; its tonal meaning is only clear from a preceding or following cadence to I. Use it as a passing cadence inside a harmonic progression or as a final cadence when an open, unresolved ending is wanted.
- R7 [ALLOWED] Cadences can be expanded with extra chords and melodic ornamentation into larger tonal structures (all earlier exercises are such expansions).
- R8 [MUST] Establish the target key by the same means as the origin key (cadence).
- R9 [PREFER] Simplest modulations use the origin key's tonic (cadential) chord as pivot, reinterpreted as a common chord in both keys.
- R10 [ALLOWED] Other modulations use the origin key's dominant or subdominant chord as pivot.
- R11 [ALLOWED] The pivot can be reinterpreted in the target key as: (a) secondary dominant of the target's V or IV; (b) secondary dominant of the target's II, III or VI; (c) a simply altered chord of the target key (e.g. borrowed minor chord, Neapolitan).
- R12 [ALLOWED] Any other chord of the origin key may be the pivot if it receives one of these functions in the target key.
- R13 [ALLOWED] Direct ("terrace") modulation: establish origin key, then enter the target key directly as harmonic contrast. Use sparingly as a special device to give impulse to harmonic development. *Why:* breaks the flow of the harmonic line.
- R14 [PREFER] In direct modulations avoid too great a structural contrast between the last chord of the old key and the first of the new; some perceptible link should remain. Hindemith's examples of acceptable contrasts: augmented chord → secondary seventh chord; chord without 3rd → dominant with 6th replacing the 5th; very open position → closest position.
- R15 [AVOID] Frequent, closely spaced abrupt modulations: the effect becomes incisive, then disturbing, then incomprehensible to the listener.

**Examples**
- Ex.60 cadence basses (each key, 3 bass notes + figures), e.g. C: bass D–G–C (II–V–I) [approx.]; F# minor with "6" then "#"; Eb with "7"; D minor 6 → 7/#; G with 6/5; Bb with ♮6/4/3; D with 7; C# minor 6/5 → #6/5; A with b6/♮ → 7/#.
- Ex.61 plagal-type cadences, e.g. C; Ab minor with ♮6/4/3; E with 6/♮5; B minor 6 → #; F with b6/b → 5/♮3; C# minor 7/#5 → 7/#; Db 6 → bb; B 7/♮5 → #; D minor #5 → b.
- Ex.62 weak cadences with remote penultimate chords (e.g. G: bass with b5, A minor 6 → b6, Eb with b5 then ♮6/4/3, Eb minor 7, E #6/♮5).
- Ex.63 half cadences: C minor 6 – ♮6/4/3 – 6 – ♮ (ends on major V); D; F# minor; Ab.
- Ex.64 (pivot = origin tonic): given C-major opening (soprano E5–D5–E5–C5 | A4 … | Ab4 … G4, bass C3–F3–… with 4/3), four continuations ending in G (F#), F (Bb), F minor/Ab region (Db, Ab, Bb), E minor (D#, F#).
- Ex.65 (pivot = origin V or IV): continuations to G, E minor/B, C minor; and to F, Bb, A minor, Bb with Eb.
- Ex.66: pivot read in target as secondary dominant, as minor (borrowed) chord — marked "(menor)" — or as Neapolitan — marked "(Nap)".
- Ex.67: same, continuations through D minor, B minor, F#/C# minor, E, D, C# minor regions.
- Ex.68 (direct modulation; first chord of new key labelled): from Eb: Cb V, B minor I6/4, E minor V4/3, C# minor IV, C I6. From F: E I6/4, Ab minor I6, Gb VII6/4, Eb Neap6/4–Neap6, D I6/4.

**Agent applicability**
- Modulation recipe: cadence in old key (…–V–I) → pivot chord reinterpreted → cadence in new key (…–V(7)–I). Don't modulate before the first key has a V–I.
- For the firmest arrival use V→I; use IV→I or remote penultimate chords only when a softer close is intended.
- End a phrase on a Phrygian half cadence (iv6→V in minor) when an open ending is desired.
- Abrupt key shifts: keep a voice-leading or sonority link (shared tone, similar density) and use rarely.

## Chapter XV — Modulation II (Modulación II) (scan p.116–123, printed p.109–116)

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] Fast, surprising modulations to distant keys: make the last chord of the origin key an "extended-alteration" chord (the altered dominants of Chapter XIII, printed p.88) in the target key. *Note:* such modulations cannot be used everywhere.
- R2 [ALLOWED] The target key need not be reached directly; the modulation may pass through intermediate keys, each reached by any method described before.
- R3 [MUST] Each intermediate harmonic group must be clearly defined so it does not sound like it belongs to a neighbouring key.
- R4 [ALLOWED] From here on, strict 4-voice writing is no longer required: some passages in 3 voices, others in more than 4.
- R5 [AVOID] Heavy alteration in 3-voice writing: the meaning of the chord connections is hard to expose clearly with only 3 voices.
- R6 [PREFER] Use 5 or more real voices mainly for calm, vocal-style music; [AVOID] it in lively instrumental style, where many independent voices obstruct each other and the result is unpleasant.
- R7 [PREFER] Practise the mixed keyboard style: full many-note chords alternating with thinner passages.

**Examples**
- Ex.69: C-major opening (bass C3–F3–E3–A3/G3… with 7), continuations whose first chord is labelled in the remote target key: D VI6/4, E V6/4, F# II (altered) 6/5, Db IV; second set from A: Eb I6, C minor I6/4, A# minor IV6, G minor I, plus chord-reinterpretation spellings with double sharps/flats.
- Ex.70 modulating melodies with key/function labels (student harmonizes): (1) Bb 6/8 Andante → F: V → D minor I6/4 → C V6/5 → A minor V6/5 → F V → Bb V → Eb V7 → Bb I6/4. (2) C# minor 2/2 Movido → G# minor II(Nap) → F# V → C# minor II(Nap) → (C#) → A# minor I → F# I → B I → G I → C# minor I → F# V → E V → G# minor VII → C# minor I. (3) Ab 4/8 Moderado → Eb I6/4 → F minor → Ab IV → C I → A minor I → C IV → E I6/4 → Ab I → C# minor (=Db) I → D V → Ab I. Plus unlabelled melodies (E major 6/8 "Con moto" with D.C. al fine; C 2/4; Bb Presto 2/2).

**Agent applicability**
- Remote modulation: reinterpret the last origin chord as German/French/augmented-dominant of the new key and resolve per Chapter XIII.
- Chained modulations: give every passing key at least a short cadence-like gesture (e.g. V–I or Nap–V–I) so it is identifiable.
- Texture: 4 voices default; 3 voices only when harmony is simple; 5+ voices for slow chorale-like textures, not for busy instrumental parts.

## Chapter XVI — Supplementary exercises (Ejercicios suplementarios) (scan p.124–132, printed p.117–125)

**Concepts**
- Task: write a piano accompaniment to four solo sonatinas for flute or violin (Sonatina I: Alegre, 2/2, Bb/G minor signature; II: Moderato, 9/4, E major signature; III: Allegro assai, 6/8, D major signature; IV: Moderato, 2/4, Ab/F minor signature). Sections are marked with Roman numerals; equal numerals = corresponding (recurring) sections. Some keyboard bass lines/"continúe" cues and "unisono" passages are given.

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] Even freer than Chapter XV regarding 4-voice writing: fill out harmonies (keeping doublings in proper proportion) or reduce them to 2–3 notes, for easier playing and better sound.
- R2 [ALLOWED] Dissolve compact harmonies into melodic lines by broken-chord figuration (figuración armónica de acordes rotos).
- R3 [ALLOWED] Use unison passages, voice crossing, bass doubling, parallel motion and similar devices for life and variety — all derived from the 4-voice style.
- R4 [MUST] Procedure: (a) determine the tonal groups — some sections stay in one key (end = beginning key), others are modulatory; (b) decide the style of each section (corresponding Roman numerals = corresponding treatment); (c) write the bass from the main harmonic points first, then the connecting passages, then add sparing figures that show the harmonic plan; (d) elaborate the whole bass per the chosen style; (e) complete the harmony.
- (pedagogy fact) Completing all exercises proves correctness, not creative talent; conversely a gifted composer who cannot do them fluently is only half-trained.

**Examples**
- Sonatina II §III starts in D# ("Re#") and later §III recurs in G# ("Sol#"); markings like "continúe" indicate the given accompaniment pattern should be continued.

**Agent applicability**
- When accompanying a given solo melody: (1) segment into sections and identify key per section (static vs modulating); (2) fix cadence/structural bass notes first, then fill connecting bass; (3) choose a texture per section and reuse it for recurring sections; (4) then voice the chords (2–5 notes, arpeggiated or block).
