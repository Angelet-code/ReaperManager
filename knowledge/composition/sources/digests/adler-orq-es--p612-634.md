# Adler, El estudio de la orquestación — scans 612–634 (printed p.611–633)

## 16 The orchestra as accompaniment (la orquesta como acompañamiento) — introduction (scan p.612, printed p.611)

**Concepts**
- Accompanying orchestra (orquesta como acompañamiento): the orchestra in a supporting role under a soloist
  (concerto), a vocal soloist, or a choir (opera, cantata, song cycle, other vocal works). Historically older
  than the independent orchestra: vocal music was the main body of Western composition until about 1600.
- Concerto grosso: 17th-c. form whose rise expanded and refined the function of orchestral accompaniment.

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] The accompanying role has persisted unchanged in kind from early opera and sacred/secular vocal
  works to today; the chapter covers concerto soloists, vocal soloists and choirs.

## 16.1 The concerto (el concierto) — general (scan p.612–613, printed p.611–612)

**Concepts**
- Fusion vs. prominence: a soloist either blends into (se funde con) the orchestral texture or stands out from it.

**Rules**
- R2 [ALLOWED] `[era: pre-1900 concerto repertoire]` Before 1900 the piano was the favourite concerto
  soloist because it mixes reasonably with every orchestral instrument yet fuses with none, so it stands out
  from any orchestral combination.
- R3 [MUST] When the soloist is an orchestral instrument (violin, cello, flute, horn…), take extra care with
  the accompaniment: orchestral soloists tend to fuse into the orchestral texture far more easily than a piano.
- R4 [ALLOWED] Many treatises recommend giving concerto accompaniments mainly to strings because of their
  discreet character; the actual repertoire does not confine itself to strings (see the techniques below).
- R5 [PREFER] Use these six techniques to keep soloist and orchestra apart: (1) dialogue; (2) swapping
  foreground/background roles between solo and tutti; (3) colour contrast; (4) rhythmic independence;
  (5) thin/simple accompaniment textures; (6) spacing and register assignment (not reached in this range).

**Agent applicability**
- Before writing a concerto-like passage, classify the soloist: piano (stands out naturally) vs. orchestral
  instrument (needs active separation via colour, rhythm, density or register).

## 16.1.1 Dialogue (uso del diálogo) (scan p.613–614, printed p.612–613)

**Concepts**
- Solo–tutti dialogue (diálogo solo-tutti): derived from the Baroque concerto grosso. The soloist states
  material alone, fixing its timbre in the listener's ear; then the orchestra enters with a radical change
  of texture and colour.

**Rules**
- R6 [PREFER] Introduce the soloist alone first, then answer with the orchestra (immediate repetition of the
  melody) so the two timbres are established as separate entities.
- R7 [PREFER] Make the orchestral answer contrast in articulation as well as colour: e.g. answer a legato
  solo line with pizzicato strings.
- R8 [ALLOWED] Dialogue also clarifies the melodic material and the formal structure (e.g. the double
  exposition of a concerto first movement).

**Examples**
- Beethoven, Romance for violin and orchestra Op. 40 (G major), mm. 1–8 (Ex. 16-1): solo violin alone states
  the theme (with double stops) for ~4 bars; then fl, ob, bsn (a 2), G horns and all strings **pizz.** (vc+db
  unison pizz.) repeat it at **p**, winds carrying the tune, strings plucked to differ from the solo legato.
- Prokofiev, Violin Concerto in G minor (No. 2): cited as a famous opening of the same kind (solo alone first).
- Schumann, Piano Concerto, 2nd mvt, mm. 1–4 (Ex. 16-2; Andantino grazioso, eighth = 120, F major): strings
  at **p** trade short slurred-staccato motives with the piano; at m. 3 winds (fl, B♭ cl, bsn, F hn) and
  strings hit **fp** chords on the downbeat while the piano answers **sfp** with pedal. Cited as one of
  hundreds of such simple, effective dialogues; see also 1st mvt mm. 185–193.

**Agent applicability**
- Pattern: bars N–N+k soloist alone (or nearly); bars N+k+1… tutti repeats the phrase; switch strings to a
  pizzicato patch (or staccato) under a legato soloist's material when the orchestra takes it.
- Short-motive dialogue: alternate 1–2 beat motives between soloist track and string section; punctuate with
  short tutti **fp** chords (velocity high attack, CC1 dropping right after).

## 16.1.2 Foreground and background roles given to solo and tutti (asignación de primer plano y plano de fondo) (scan p.614–619, printed p.613–618)

**Concepts**
- Role swap: in most well-known concertos the foreground material normally given to the soloist is often
  given to the orchestra, and the background passes to the soloist.

**Rules**
- R9 [ALLOWED] Give the main theme to the orchestra and make the soloist the accompaniment.
- R10 [MUST] When the soloist accompanies orchestral foreground, use the figuration most idiomatic to the solo
  instrument. *Why:* it keeps the soloist characteristic while subordinate.
- R11 [PREFER] Piano as accompanist: an arpeggiated left hand (or massive chords) is very effective.
- R12 [PREFER] String soloist as accompanist: virtuoso scales, running arpeggios and other melodic/harmonic
  figuration in 16ths under orchestral melody.

**Examples**
- Tchaikovsky, Piano Concerto No. 1, 1st mvt, mm. 1–17 (Ex. 16-3; Allegro non troppo e molto maestoso, B♭ minor
  → D♭): after a 4-horn **ff** call and tutti **ff** chords (mm. 2–6), the big theme goes to **violins 1 +
  cellos** in octaves (**mf**) while the piano plays huge **ff** chords across the keyboard (8va/8vb) as the
  accompaniment, reinforced by soft (**p**) sustained wind chords (fl, ob, cl, bsn, hn 1–2); violas and basses
  **pizz.** on the beats.
- Saint-Saëns, Cello Concerto (No. 1), mm. 447–462 (Ex. 16-4; Allegro non troppo): the solo cello runs
  continuous 16th-note scales/arpeggios (crossing into treble clef) as accompaniment, while winds (2 ob, 2 A cl,
  2 bsn) play staccato 8th-note chords and later a legato oboe/clarinet/bassoon line; violins 1 + violas carry a
  slurred melody in thirds/sixths [?], violins 2 off-beat chords, vc/db on beats. At m. 459 violins 1–2 switch to
  **leggiero** repeated 16ths, violas arco quarter chords, vc/db **pizz.**; flutes add **p** staccato chords.
- Pointer: further study — Brahms Violin Concerto 1st mvt mm. 110–131; Mendelssohn Violin Concerto 1st mvt
  mm. 335–343.

**Agent applicability**
- For a "piano concerto" texture: theme in VLN1 + VLC (octaves), piano track with block chords on beats (wide
  voicings, both hands, octave doublings), winds sustaining soft chord pads, VLA/DB pizz on beats.
- For a string-soloist accompaniment: soloist track with continuous 16th scale/arpeggio figuration, orchestra
  carrying the tune; keep the solo dynamic below the melody (inferred).

## 16.1.3 Colour contrast to separate soloist and orchestra (aprovechamiento del contraste de color) (scan p.619–628, printed p.618–627)

**Concepts**
- Contrasting introduction colour: introduce the soloist with an orchestral combination that clearly contrasts
  with the solo instrument's timbre.

**Rules**
- R13 [PREFER] Open a movement with a colour unlike the soloist's (e.g. a woodwind section before a string
  soloist) so the soloist sounds fresh on entry, even if strings dominated the previous movement.
- R14 [ALLOWED] Intermittent doubling of the soloist by a contrasting colour (e.g. violins 1 doubling a solo
  horn) does not weaken the solo, provided the colour contrast is large.
- R15 [AVOID] Using orchestral instruments of the soloist's own kind while the soloist plays. Mozart never uses
  the orchestral horns while the solo horn plays (only in the orchestral intro and interludes), and writes no
  orchestral flutes at all in a flute concerto, so the soloist has no same-colour competitor.
- R16 [PREFER] Keep the accompaniment light (strings alone, with 2 horns and 2 oboes only at chosen points)
  under a soft-coloured soloist such as the flute.
- R17 [PREFER] Present a melody twice in two colours (e.g. first oboes, then solo flute + violins 1) to
  separate orchestral and solo statements.
- R18 [MUST] When a soloist enters in a weak register, cut the tutti drastically and drop to **piano** at the
  entry. Example: flute entering in the weakest part of its range (its low register, ~C4–G4 / MIDI 60–67,
  inferred) after a **forte** tutti.
- R19 [ALLOWED] A held pedal note in a contrasting colour (horns + cellos + basses) under a solo passage
  separates the colours and can emphasise a structural event (e.g. the dominant pedal before a return to the
  tonic).
- R20 [ALLOWED] A light accompaniment lets the soloist play scale passages freely (rubato-like freedom).
- R21 [ALLOWED] The non-soloist strings provide the harmonic background most of the time, but may come forward
  briefly for contrapuntal interest.

**Examples**
- Tchaikovsky, Violin Concerto, 2nd mvt (Canzonetta, Andante, quarter = 84, G minor), mm. 1–24 (Ex. 16-5):
  12-bar woodwind chorale (2 ob, 2 B♭ cl, 2 bsn, 2 F hn) at **p/pp**; at m. 13 the solo violin enters **con
  sordino, p molto espress.** over **pp con sordini** sustained violins 1, 2 and violas (cellos added **pp**
  later); horns keep soft accented background notes; a solo clarinet **pp** answers at ~m. 20.
- Dvořák, Cello Concerto, 2nd mvt, mm. 1–10 (Ex. 16-6; Adagio ma non troppo, eighth = 108, G major): oboe,
  A clarinet (lead) and bassoon trio opens; a D horn enters **p**; the solo cello enters ~m. 8 **p dolce** over
  only **pp** cellos and basses — woodwind intro makes the cello colour fresh.
- Mozart, Horn Concerto No. 2 K. 417, 1st mvt, mm. 6–63 (Ex. 16-7; Allegro, E♭) `[era: Classical natural horn
  concerto, 2 ob + 2 hn + strings]`: tutti intro with oboes and horns in chords; from m. 25 the solo E♭ horn
  plays with strings only (violins 2 in repeated 8th/16th patterns, **p**), violins 1 intermittently doubling or
  shadowing the horn line; oboes/horns return only in interludes (e.g. ~m. 48).
- Mozart, Flute Concerto No. 1 (G major) K. 313, 3rd mvt, mm. 54–94 (Ex. 16-8; Allegro, 3/4): orchestra =
  2 ob, 2 G hn, strings, no flutes. Tutti **f** with oboes/horns a 2; flute solo over strings **p** (light 8th
  chords); melody first in oboes (m. 65), then flute + violins 1 (m. 69); free solo scales from m. 73; tutti
  **f** at mm. 81–82 cut to **p** at m. 83 when the flute starts low; held D in horns, cellos, basses (**p/fp**)
  under it; tutti **f** returns ~m. 93 for the G tonic arrival at m. 94.

**Agent applicability**
- Orchestral intro before a solo entry: write it for a section of a different family from the soloist
  (woods before a string solo, strings before a wind solo), then switch the bed when the solo enters.
- Under a violin soloist in a slow movement: muted strings (con sordino patch) **pp** sustained chords;
  set CC1/CC11 low (pp) and keep them out of the solo's register (inferred).
- Mute (no notes) any orchestral track of the soloist's instrument type while the solo plays; use them only in
  tutti/interlude bars.
- At a solo entry after a tutti: drop orchestral dynamic by ≥ two levels (f → p) on the entry bar; if the solo
  is in its weak low register, thin to strings + one sustained pedal.

## 16.1.4 Separating solo and tutti by rhythmic independence (separación del solo y del tutti por la independencia rítmica) (scan p.628–631, printed p.627–630)

**Concepts**
- Rhythmic independence (independencia rítmica): accompaniment whose rhythmic patterns neither double nor imitate
  the soloist's. More delicate than colour contrast, and crucial when soloist and orchestra have similar colours.

**Rules**
- R22 [MUST] When the accompaniment shares the soloist's colour (e.g. string orchestra under a solo violin),
  separate them rhythmically: the accompaniment must neither double nor imitate the soloist's rhythms.
- R23 [ALLOWED] Give one section (violins 1) a canonic countermelody (contramelodía canónica) and the rest of the
  section a simple harmonic accompaniment.
- R24 [ALLOWED] Contrast a smooth, long-note solo line with a light, playful ("coquettish", coqueta) staccato
  accompaniment figuration in the strings.

**Examples**
- Tchaikovsky, Violin Concerto, 1st mvt, mm. 28–38 (Ex. 16-9; Moderato assai, quarter = 80, D major): strings
  only. Violins 2 + violas **pp** whole-note sustains, cellos **pizz. pp** arpeggiated 8ths, basses **pizz.** on
  beat 1; violins 1 enter **p** with long notes, then at m. 32 a countermelody in 8ths while vla/vc/db play
  detached 8ths on the beats (pizz → arco); at m. 35 the accompaniment drops out as the solo plays 16th/32nd
  figuration (**mf**, cresc.).
- Mendelssohn, Violin Concerto in E minor, 3rd mvt, mm. 107–117 (Ex. 16-10; Allegro molto, E major): solo violin
  in smooth half/quarter-note legato melody; strings **pp / sempre pp** with short slurred-staccato 16th–8th
  figures passed between sections (vc/db pizz then arco); 2 A clarinets and 2 bassoons hold **p** chords;
  flutes and clarinets add short **p** staccato chords at m. 117.

**Agent applicability**
- Rhythm check for an accompaniment track: onsets should not coincide with most solo onsets and should not
  reproduce the solo's rhythmic cells (inferred check: <50 % shared onsets, no copied motif).
- Default bed under a long-note solo: sustained inner strings + pizz bass on beats + one section with short
  staccato figures on off-beats.

## 16.1.5 Simple accompaniment textures (uso provechoso de las texturas de acompañamiento sencillas) (scan p.631–634, printed p.630–633; continues)

**Concepts**
- Appropriate density (densidad adecuada): a texture thin enough that the soloist's opening melodic designs are
  heard immediately.

**Rules**
- R25 [PREFER] Under a soloist's first statement, use a simple, very light texture (one repeating figure,
  sustained notes) so the melody rises freely above it.
- R26 [PREFER] A regular but pulsing, elusive background rhythm (undulating strings, quietly pulsing timpani,
  a held wind chord) contrasts well with a rhythmically complex solo melody.

**Examples**
- Sibelius, Violin Concerto, 1st mvt, mm. 1–40 (Ex. 16-11; Allegro moderato, D minor): violins 1 and 2 each
  divided in two (4 parts), **con sord. pp**, rocking two-note slurred 8th figures — an "ethereal" expectant bed;
  the soloist enters at m. 4 **mf dolce ed espressivo** with a strong, fresh sound. A solo clarinet **p espress.**
  adds a line (~m. 10); at m. 23 the solo goes **sul G**, muted violas join **mp** with sextuplets (~m. 29);
  at m. 33 the strings stop, leaving timpani roll **pp** + basses pedal **pp**, clarinets then bassoon **p ma
  marcato** under the solo.
- Bartók, Piano Concerto No. 3, 1st mvt, mm. 1–18 (Ex. 16-12; Allegretto, quarter = 88, E major): violins 2 and
  violas **div.**, **p**, undulating 16th-sextuplet oscillations; timpani **p** quietly on the beats; A clarinets
  1–2 hold a chord **p** (flutes 1–2 later); cellos/basses **pizz. p** single notes; the piano plays the lyrical
  melody in octaves (hands two octaves apart) with free, complex rhythms over this whispering bed.

**Agent applicability**
- "Ethereal" bed for a solo entry: 4-part divisi violins (or 2 VLN tracks × 2 voices), muted, pp, two-note
  slurred 8th oscillations; hold 3+ bars before the solo enters.
- "Whispering" bed (modern): divisi VLN2/VLA 16th-sextuplet oscillation p, timpani pulse on beats p, sustained
  clarinet dyad, pizz bass on downbeats; soloist free on top.
