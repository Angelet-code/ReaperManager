## 16.1.5 Simple accompaniment textures — end of Bartók example (scan p.635–636, printed p.634–635)

**Examples**
- Bartók, Piano Concerto No. 3, 1st mvt, mm. 9–19 (Ex. 16-12, cont.): the piano melody in octaves (hands an
  octave apart) gets ornamental triplet turns; flutes 1–2 and A clarinets 1–2 hold sparse sustained notes;
  horns 1–2 enter **p** on single held notes; violas/violins 2 keep the 16th oscillation bed; cellos/basses
  single **pizz.** notes (arco for the odd held note). From m. 11 the strings switch to **unis.**, short
  staccato 16th/8th chord-dots **p** on and off the beat; oboes 1–2 add soft dyads (m. 14). At m. 17
  bassoons 1–2 and horns 1–2 swell **p cresc.** to **f** under rising piano quintuplet/10-tuplet runs into
  8va, and the full strings answer with one short **f** chord stroke.

**Agent applicability**
- A long piano/solo-instrument melody can sit over (a) a 16th-oscillation string bed, (b) then a dry
  staccato string chord pattern p; mark the solo's climactic run with a wind cresc. p→f and a single tutti
  string stab.

## 16.1.6 Spacing and register to separate the solo line from the orchestra (uso de la espacialización y de la posición en el registro para diferenciar la línea solista de la orquesta) (scan p.636–639, printed p.635–638)

**Concepts**
- Blending risk: some solo instruments fuse into the orchestra when played in certain registers and with
  acoustically similar (afines) orchestral combinations. Adler shows three concerto solutions.

**Rules**
- R1 [PREFER] When the soloist would blend, place it in a register no accompanying instrument occupies
  (e.g. above the whole wind chord). *Why:* register isolation makes the solo dominate without force.
- R2 [ALLOWED] The soloist may *replace* one member of a wind combination (take the flute's top voice) and
  thereby lead the texture.
- R3 [PREFER] When a wind instrument carries the melody against the soloist's virtuoso figuration, give the
  wind its most intense, expressive register and keep the string accompaniment harmonically simple; the
  rhythmic contrast (melody slow vs. solo busy) plus the simple background sets clear foreground /
  middleground / background roles.
- R4 [ALLOWED] A unison doubling of only the *most important notes* of the solo melody by an idiomatic
  orchestral instrument (e.g. flute with trills) colours the solo without covering it.
- R5 [ALLOWED] A simple accompaniment figure to harmonize a solo melody often works very well, especially
  under woodwind soloists.
- R6 [PREFER] As far as possible keep the accompanying forces in registers distant from the soloist.
- R7 Exception to R6: a strongly contrasting rhythmic accompaniment makes register separation less
  necessary.

**Examples**
- Beethoven, Violin Concerto, 1st mvt, mm. 99–109 (Ex. 16-13; Allegro ma non troppo, D major): from m. 102
  the solo violin replaces the flute atop the wind chord (2 Ob, 2 A Cl, 2 Bsn, **p**, simple sustained
  harmony) in a high "ethereal" register, *dolce*; strings only hold one **p** chord before; the timpani
  plays the four-repeated-quarter rhythmic motive **p** that links the two melodic phrases.
- Beethoven, Violin Concerto, 3rd mvt, mm. 135–142 (Ex. 16-14; Allegro, 6/8): bassoon melody in tenor clef
  (its intense upper register) = foreground; solo violin virtuoso 32nd-note figuration = middleground;
  strings = background (violins/viola off-beat 8th pairs, cellos/basses **pizz.** on the beats).
- Liszt, Piano Concerto No. 1 in Eb, 2nd mvt (the "triangle passage"), mm. 99–104 (Ex. 16-15; Allegretto
  vivace, 3/4): piano in its high register (8va); flute **(mf) scherzando** doubles the key melody notes
  at the unison with trills; violins 1–2 and violas **pizz. p** chords on beats 1 and 2; triangle adds a
  new colour on beat 3 (one 8th stroke).

**Agent applicability**
- To make a solo cut through a pad: put the solo ≥ an octave above (or below) the accompaniment's
  register band, or let it take the top voice of a soft wind chord.
- Solo + countermelody: give the wind countermelody its expressive register and slow values; keep strings
  to pizz downbeats + off-beat 8ths.
- Pizz on beats 1–2 + one percussion colour on beat 3 = a light 3/4 bed under a sparkling solo.

## 16.2 Accompanying the vocal soloist, ensemble or chorus (acompañamiento del solista vocal, conjunto o coro) — voices (scan p.640–641, printed p.639–640)

**Concepts**
- One of the orchestra's first roles: accompanying vocal music (solo, vocal ensembles, choruses).
- The four voice types' "accepted" ranges below are the general extreme limits for most singers; some
  singers have wider ranges.

**Ranges** (sounding pitch; MIDI in brackets; parentheses = optional extension shown in parentheses by
Adler)
- Soprano: C4–C6 [60–84]. Some sopranos go above C6 (DO6) easily, and with chest resonance (resonancia
  pectoral) down to a 4th below middle C (G3 [55]).
- Alto (general): G3–G5 [55–79] [? top could be F5 [77]; small scan].
  - Mezzo-soprano: A3–A5 [57–81].
  - Contralto: (F3) G3–F5 [(53) 55–77].
- Tenor: written treble-8vb (D4–Bb5 written) → sounding (C3) D3–Bb4 [(48) 50–70]. Tenor types are not
  formally divided, but voice quality separates a heavy Wagnerian tenor from a light lyric (Mozart) tenor.
- Bass (general): F2–E4 [41–64].
  - Baritone: G2–F4 (G4) [43–65 (67)].
  - Basso profundo: (D2) E2–D4 [(38) 40–62]. Some basses reach a 4th below the lowest note shown
    (≈ B1/A1 [35/33]) (inferred from the profundo staff).

**Rules**
- R8 [AVOID] Forcing a voice to sing over a dense orchestral accompaniment for long stretches: it tires and
  strains the singer and spoils the natural beauty of the voice.
- R9 Timbre and power change across each voice's range, and, unlike orchestral instruments, also differ
  from singer to singer.
- R10 [PREFER] The lowest 5th of a range sounds weakest: accompany it lightly. The next octave is relaxed
  and fairly strong; the top 5th is the most powerful.
- R11 Exception to R10: for many basses and altos the low part of the range sounds much better than the
  top, which can sound constricted and forced.
- R12 Soprano and tenor upper registers are penetrating; their low register can only express very softly.
- R13 [MUST] Singers find pitches by ear (no strings, keys or valves); few have absolute pitch, a few
  "feel" pitches as horn players do, most rely on the accompaniment. Lead the singer to the first note
  (have the orchestra sound it or a clear reference) and keep supporting intonation from the orchestra.
- R14 [ALLOWED] This does not mean doubling the voice at the unison all the time; but give the singer
  pitch reference points as often as possible.
- R15 [MUST] R13–R14 matter more with today's expanded harmonic vocabulary, and are even more crucial for
  chorus (less trained than soloists).

**Agent applicability**
- Validate generated vocal lines against the MIDI ranges above; keep sustained writing out of the lowest
  5th unless the accompaniment thins out there (bass/alto excepted, R11).
- Before each vocal entry, sound the entry pitch (or its chord with that pitch in an audible voice) in the
  orchestra; in chromatic/atonal passages, have some instrument touch the voice's pitches regularly.
- Thin the orchestra (fewer layers, lower dynamics) wherever the voice sits in its weak register.

## 16.2.1 The recitative (el recitativo) (scan p.641–643, printed p.640–642)

**Concepts**
- Recitative: the simplest vocal piece to accompany; usually a few chords to establish and sustain the
  harmonic background.
- Two common types: (a) accompanied only by continuo (secco); (b) accompanied by orchestra or a smaller
  orchestral group (accompagnato).

**Rules**
- R16 [era: Baroque/Classical continuo practice] Continuo recitative (Bach, Handel, other Baroque
  composers) is written as a vocal line over a figured bass; the keyboard improvises the chords.
- R17 [ALLOWED] Orchestral recitative also uses simple chord blocks, but the orchestra intervenes more;
  orchestral interruptions between vocal phrases carry the drama.
- R18 (inferred from Ex. 16-17) [PREFER] In accompagnato, sustain soft chords (fp) under sung phrases and put
  the energetic orchestral figures (tremolo 16ths, f stabs) in the vocal rests.

**Examples**
- Haydn, *The Creation*, No. 9 "Und die himmlischen Heerscharen", mm. 1–14 (Ex. 16-16; 4/4): Uriel (tenor)
  declaims in 8ths/16ths; harpsichord realizes held chords (small noteheads = realization) over a held
  continuo bass; figures 6, 6#; a final cadence chord after the phrase.
- Additional passage: J. S. Bach, Cantata No. 56 "Kreuzstab", "Mein Wandel auf der Welt", mm. 1–10.
- Mendelssohn, *Elijah*, "Call him louder", mm. 1–11 (Ex. 16-17): recit opens with an A clarinet low held
  note and strings **fp** sustained chords under the bass solo; then *Allegro molto* (quarter = 160)
  violins/violas/cellos burst in **f** with repeated 16th chords and short f chord stabs placed between and
  after vocal phrases.
- Walton, *Belshazzar's Feast*, recitative "And in that same hour", mm. 1–8 (Ex. "16-16", i.e. 16-18;
  quarter = 50): baritone sings the first lines unaccompanied, *quasi recit.*, in free triplet/quintuplet
  rhythms; then the orchestra enters **ppp lugubre**: low tremolo dyads (8vb), a bass ostinato of chord
  strokes with percussion colours (cymbal, bass drum, timpani, gong) on successive beats, accented chord
  interjections between the words "Mene, mene, tekel upharsin", and a high trilled 8va figure to close.
- Additional passage: Mozart, *Le nozze di Figaro*, Act III Sc. 4, "Hai già vinta la causa", mm. 14–40.

**Agent applicability**
- Secco recit (period style): voice + harpsichord block chords changing only at harmonic changes + held
  bass (cello/bass or bassoon); chords on the downbeat or just after the singer's phrase.
- Accompagnato: fp sustained strings under text; dramatic tremolo/stab figures only in the vocal rests.
- Modern/dramatic: let the voice declaim alone first, then a ppp low tremolo + percussion-coloured ostinato.

## 16.2.2 The opera aria and the orchestral song (el aria de ópera y el "lied" orquestal) (scan p.644–653, printed p.643–652)

**Concepts**
- In an aria or orchestral song (lied, canción orquestal) the accompaniment often sets the atmosphere and
  even interprets the text — especially in opera.

**Rules**
- R19 [ALLOWED] [era: 19th-c. Italian opera] Many of the most successful Italian operas use very simple
  accompaniment figures so the singer projects words and emotion without orchestral interference.
- R20 [PREFER] Contrast the solo voice's colour/register with the accompaniment: e.g. a high soprano over
  dark, warm low instruments (cello, horns, low woodwinds).
- R21 [PREFER] Keep rhythmic separation between voice and instruments (instruments on short off-beat
  attacks or sustained values while the voice moves), so the singer is free to shape the line.
- R22 [ALLOWED] Double the voice with violins an octave higher only at the dramatic climax.
- R23 [PREFER] A colourful, busy orchestral "painting" is possible if the voice is never covered; the
  singer's notes may be present somewhere in the orchestration (doubled by changing instruments, rhythms not
  necessarily coinciding) without literal continuous doubling.

**Examples**
- Verdi, *La Traviata*, Act I Sc. 3, "Ah fors'è lui", mm. 29–44 (Ex. 16-19; Andantino, 3/8, F major): C
  clarinet arpeggiated 16th staccato obbligato; bassoon and horn sustained dotted-quarter chords; strings
  all **pizz.** (basses on beat 1, upper strings 8ths on beats 2–3); Violetta sings *con espansione* above.
- Additional passage: Verdi, *La Traviata*, Act III No. 10, "Parigi, o cara", mm. 1–35.
- Bizet, *Carmen*, Act III No. 22, Micaela "Je dis que rien ne m'épouvante", mm. 6–24 (Ex. 16-20; Andante
  molto, quarter = 44, Eb major): cellos play a slurred arpeggiated 16th figure (expressive, with grace
  notes); basses single 8th on the downbeat; flutes, English horn and 2 Bb clarinets in their low register
  plus 2 Eb and 2 Bb horns **ppp** in short tied off-beat attacks; violins 1–2 off-beat held dyads; the
  voice floats above. At the climax word "peur" (m. ~18–19) violins go **unis.** and double the voice an
  octave higher **poco sf**; trombones **ppp**, timpani **pppp** roll, oboes enter **mf dim.**; the music
  ends *colla voce*, then *a tempo* with low held notes (violins, violas *senza sordini*).
- Mahler, *Lieder eines fahrenden Gesellen*, No. 1 "Wenn mein Schatz", mm. 44–61 (Ex. 16-21; Sanft bewegt,
  6/8–3/8 changes): a pastoral "painting" — oboes accented held chords **p**, Bb clarinet slurred figures,
  horn held notes **p**, glockenspiel and harp single **p** strokes, triangle **pp**, solo violins (Vln 1
  solo trills; Vln 2–3 solo grace-note chirps) as birdsong; tutti violins 1–2 and violas **pp** move in
  smooth slurred 8ths around the voice's line; the voice is never literally doubled throughout, but its
  pitches appear in the orchestra (mm. 46–47: first violin 2, then viola). Later tempo marks *Nicht
  schleppen*, *drängend*, *Vorwärts*; bass clarinet, bassoon and timpani roll **pp** enter for depth.
- Additional orchestral songs for study (one-line pointer): Barber *Knoxville*, Berg *Altenberg Lieder*,
  Berlioz *Les Nuits d'été*, Britten *Serenade*/*Nocturne*, Chausson, Elgar *Sea Pictures*, Mahler
  *Kindertotenlieder*/*Das Lied von der Erde*, Messiaen, Ravel *Shéhérazade*, Schoenberg *Erwartung*,
  Wagner *Wesendonck Lieder*.

**Agent applicability**
- Italian-aria bed: one wind obbligato (arpeggio 16ths, staccato), sustained bassoon/horn chords, pizz
  strings oom-pah; keep the voice the only legato line in its register.
- High female voice: accompany with cello figure + low woodwinds + horns ppp; reserve violin octave
  doubling for the climax bar only.
- Lied with "nature painting": keep bird/sparkle figures (solo violins, glockenspiel, harp, triangle) in
  rests or registers away from the voice; let violin 2 / viola briefly touch the voice's pitches.

## 16.2.3 The opera vocal ensemble (el conjunto instrumental o ensamble vocal operístico) (scan p.653–656, printed p.652–655; continues)

**Rules**
- R24 [PREFER] In a vocal ensemble, double the voice parts all the time — either continuously or
  sporadically (only some of their most important notes) — and keep the orchestral texture around the
  singers extremely light.
- R25 [ALLOWED] The bass voice part may be doubled several octaves higher; the soprano part may be doubled
  by a bass instrument (lower octave(s)).
- R26 [ALLOWED] Light orchestral figuration can express the scene's mood (here: contentment).

**Examples**
- Beethoven, *Fidelio*, Act I No. 3, quartet "Mir ist so wunderbar" (canon a 4), mm. 1–51 (Ex. 16-22;
  Andante sostenuto, G major): intro for divided violas 1–2 and divided cellos 1–2 *sempre p* with basses
  **pizz.**; Marzelline enters *sotto voce* (m. 9) with C clarinet as a doubling/companion line and a
  single viola and cello below; Leonore enters (m. ~17) with flute *sempre p* over pizz. violins 2; Rocco
  (bass) enters (m. ~25) with bassoon, G horns 1–2 held notes, violin 2 repeated 8ths (arco), violin 1 pizz.;
  Jaquino (tenor) enters (m. ~32) as the texture adds triplet 8th chords in violins 2/violas and
  flute/clarinet/bassoon triplet arpeggios; cellos/basses alternate arco and pizz. Instruments double
  fragments of each canonic voice as it enters.

**Agent applicability**
- For a canon/ensemble of voices: add one wind colour per entering voice, doubling its key notes; keep
  strings to soft pizz/repeated-note chords; check every vocal line has an instrumental pitch reference.
