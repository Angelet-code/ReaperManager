# Adler, El estudio de la orquestación — scans 124–148 (ch.5 String orchestration, first part)

Pitch convention: C4 = middle C = MIDI 60. All string instruments here are non-transposing except the double bass,
which sounds an octave below written. Scan number = printed page + 13; printed pages 111–135 are continuous. This
range is about texture and role assignment, not ranges; few absolute pitches are given, so MIDI numbers below are
rare and marked when inferred.

## 5 Introduction (scan p.124, printed p.111)

**Concepts**
- String section (sección de cuerda): for roughly the last 250 years the orchestra's main lyric-harmonic element;
  it settled into five characteristic voices (vln1, vln2, vla, vc, db) and can carry the most complex ideas either
  inside the full orchestra or alone.

**Rules**
- R1 [PREFER] Treat the string section as five independent voices, each able to carry any role (melody,
  counterpoint, bass, pad), not as "violins + accompaniment".

## La individualidad dentro del conjunto — individuality within the ensemble (scan p.124–131, printed p.111–118)

**Concepts**
- Quartet legacy: the Haydn/Mozart/Beethoven string quartets shaped the five-voice orchestral string section.
  Their four results: (1) vln2, viola and cello emancipated to equal footing with vln1; (2) voice crossing (cruce
  de voces) used for special effects; (3) specific registers chosen on purpose for timbre and for form; (4) range
  extended on every instrument.
- Double pairing (doble emparejamiento): two instruments paired on the melody, the other two paired on harmony.

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] Give the theme to any of the four upper string parts in turn (vln1, vln2, vla, vc) across
  repetitions or variations; the others become accompaniment or counterpoint.
- R2 [PREFER] When a melody moves to a lower instrument, keep it in that instrument's rich upper register, even if
  it crosses above the parts normally written over it. Haydn puts the cello theme above the viola and briefly above
  vln2's lowest note, because the same line on vln2 would sit in vln2's weakest (lowest) register.
- R3 [ALLOWED] Voice crossing: viola melody placed above both violins; cello melody above viola. The parts above it
  in score order must then sit lower or thin out.
- R4 [PREFER] An accompanying violin can double a cello melody a 3rd above (parallel 3rds) as pure support.
- R5 [ALLOWED] Viola as harmonic pedal stressing tonic and dominant notes to complete and clarify the harmony while
  others carry melody and counterpoint.
- R6 [PREFER] Let the cello be a counter-melody (elaborate contrapuntal line) instead of only a bass line when the
  melody is in a middle voice.
- R7 [PREFER] Balance by register and orchestration first, dynamics second. Haydn marks no dynamic at the start of
  variations II–IV (only *sempre p* in var. I) and gets the right balance purely from register and scoring: the
  leading voice dominates, the rest are support.
- R8 [MUST] Save the highest register of a theme for the climax. Transposing the theme up an octave only in the
  final statement makes it sound fresh and climactic. *Why:* using extreme registers too early means the final
  resolution works against the needed climax.
- R9 [ALLOWED] A sustained cello pedal (e.g. dominant pedal) under the last statement gives a feeling of resolution
  and finality.
- R10 [PREFER] Build a variation set as an accumulation: introduce elements gradually in harmony and counterpoint
  so the final statement absorbs them naturally.

**Double bass in the Classical orchestra (El contrabajo en la orquesta clásica, printed p.116–118)**
- R11 [era: Classical, Haydn–Mozart–Beethoven–Schubert] The double bass doubles the cello (sounding an octave
  lower) in most passages, especially *tutti*.
- R12 [era: Classical] For a lighter string texture, the double bass is simply dropped.
- R13 [era: 19th c. onward] Independent double-bass parts became more common once the cello took over the tenor
  role of the strings.
- R14 [PREFER] When adding basses to a quartet-style texture, double the cello at the lower octave on the
  structural bass (opening phrase, cadences, the end), rest during inner/lighter bars, and join the cello on a
  long dominant pedal. Adler's hypothetical reorchestration of Haydn var. IV: bass doubles cello from the anacrusis
  through bars 1–3½, rests, re-enters for the dominant pedal (bars 8–12), doubles again at the cadence (15–16) and
  at the end.

**Examples**
- Ex. 5-1 Haydn, Quartet Op.76/3 "Emperor", ii, theme (m.1–12): vln1+vln2 paired on the melody (vln2 mostly in
  3rds/6ths below), viola+cello paired on harmony; clean voice-leading reinforces the tune. Poco adagio cantabile,
  *p*.
- Ex. 5-2 var. I: theme in vln2, vln1 above in fast 16th-note figuration (harmonic counterpoint derived from the
  theme); viola and cello tacet (a duo).
- Ex. 5-3 var. II: theme in cello in its upper register, vln2 doubling a 3rd above, viola pedal on tonic/dominant,
  vln1 syncopated counterpoint above.
- Ex. 5-4 var. III: theme in viola, above both violins; cello silent at first, then enters with an elaborate
  counter-melody.
- Ex. 5-5 var. IV: theme back in vln1, now an octave higher (climax); cello (tenor clef) sustains a long dominant
  pedal; all four parts contrapuntally active; ends *pp* with swells.
- Ex. 5-6 same var. IV with an added double-bass part illustrating R14.

**Agent applicability**
- For a theme repeated N times, rotate it through vln1 → vln2 → vc → vla (or similar) and keep the vln1 high-octave
  version for the last, climactic statement.
- When the cello has the melody, check it lies in its upper register and that vln2/vla parts below or around it are
  softer and simpler; crossings are acceptable.
- In Classical-style scoring, write db = vc − 12 semitones on tutti/structural bass and drop the db on light
  passages.
- Set balance first through register/role (velocity/CC similar across parts), then fine-tune with dynamics.

## Primer plano – plano medio – plano de fondo: foreground, middleground, background (scan p.131–139, printed p.118–126)

**Concepts**
- Foreground (primer plano): the most important voice, usually the melody meant to be heard prominently.
- Middleground (plano medio): important counterpoint voices or material.
- Background (plano de fondo): the accompaniment, either chordal or built from polyphonic/melodic figures.
- Tonal rhythm (ritmo tonal) and emotional intensity of the foreground line: what the orchestrator weighs before
  choosing an instrument and register.
- Doubling (duplicación): two instruments playing the same passage in parallel intervals (unison, octaves, 3rds…).

**Rules — treating the foreground**
- R1 [MUST] Choose the foreground instrument and register first, from the line's rhythm, intensity and the five
  instruments' registers; the foreground's writing then dictates the middleground and background.
- R2 [PREFER] A lyrical, intense mid-low melody (Brahms 3/iii: roughly the G3–G4 area [inferred from the score]) goes
  to cellos in their upper (lyric) register rather than to violins at the same pitch.
- R3 [AVOID] Violin 1 low on its range for an important lyrical melody: relatively weak, little emotional force.
- R4 [AVOID] Raising a first statement an octave to "fix" weakness: it robs the later higher restatements of their
  climax (same principle as Haydn var. IV).
- R5 [ALLOWED] Vln2 *sul G* for a melody: intense but much darker than the cello's upper strings.
- R6 [AVOID] Viola for a melody in that same middle register when intensity is wanted: sweet but bland, lacking
  intensity and richness.
- R7 [AVOID] Double bass for a lyrical melody: a caricature; its lower register lacks expressive quality.
- R8 [era: late 19th c. onward] Double-bass solos (one player or section) are used for lugubrious or comic effects.
- R9 [PREFER] Introduce a theme in the cello and restate it later in vln1 an octave higher: the cello opening is
  striking, and it leaves vln1's higher register for the intensification. Adler notes that the fuller, denser
  restatement in Brahms sounds less tense than the cello original.

**Rules — distributing the background**
- R10 [PREFER] Keep background figures out of the melody's register, especially when foreground and background
  instruments have similar timbre (common professional advice).
- R11 [ALLOWED] When background shares the melody's register, rely on register character instead: (a) the
  foreground sits in the best register of its instrument; (b) background figures sit in the less brilliant or less
  defined registers of their instruments; (c) double basses play *pizz.* to lighten the bass line's impact.
- R12 [AVOID] Writing the accompanying violins/violas an octave higher: it draws too much attention to the
  background and anticipates the high register reserved for the later melody.
- R13 [PREFER] Give the accompaniment a rhythm and expressive character radically different from the melody, e.g.
  soft, fast repeated triplet/sextuplet arpeggios under a long-note melody. The repetitive figure adds intensity to
  the melody's long notes without disturbing them.
- R14 [PREFER] Make the background arpeggios of different parts interlock (dovetail) so the texture is smooth and
  unbroken.
- R15 [ALLOWED] Soft woodwinds (flutes, bassoons) holding the harmony clarify and stabilize arpeggiated string
  harmony (topic of Adler ch.8).
- R16 [ALLOWED] A little solo rubato on the anacrusis of a cello theme is a conductor's option (performance note).

**Rules — changing texture/timbre to differentiate statements (Cambios de textura y timbre)**
- R17 [ALLOWED] State the antecedent of an idea in powerful unison/octaves, then change texture for the consequent
  to bring out its different emotional quality.
- R18 [era: Classical] A simple accompaniment in parallel 3rds (vln2 + viola) after austere octaves gives a large
  emotional contrast with only a slight change of texture and dynamics.
- R19 [ALLOWED] A sudden stop of an Alberti-type accompaniment changes the character from stable to ethereal and
  highlights the next phrase.
- R20 [PREFER] Restate a theme with a new texture: add cellos and basses, put an Alberti figure in vln2, and drop
  vln1 an octave so that woodwinds entering in the following *tutti* are heard (Haydn 103).
- R21 [ALLOWED] Solo/tutti contrast: legato, harmonically rich soloist against an austere *staccato* string unison
  (octaves), *f*, *sempre staccato*.

**Examples**
- Ex. 5-7 Brahms, Symphony 3/iii, m.1–14 (Poco allegretto, 3/8, C minor): cello melody *mezza voce, espress.*;
  vln1/vln2 *pp leggiero* triplet/sextuplet 16th arpeggios; violas *p* 16th figures; basses *pizz. p* on
  downbeats; 2 fl + 2 bsn sustain soft chords. Restatement in vln1 an octave up with denser scoring (m.12–14).
- Ex. 5-8 / 5-9 / 5-10 / 5-11 Adler's reorchestrations of the same theme in vln1, vln2 (sul G), viola and double
  bass (tenor clef, cellos then take the pizz. bass): demonstrate R3, R5, R6, R7 — each is weaker than the cello.
- Ex. 5-12 Mozart, Eine kleine Nachtmusik, i, m.56–62: *f* unison/octave statement by all strings, then *p* vln1
  melody over vln2+viola in parallel 3rds (8ths) and vc/db on downbeats (R17, R18).
- Ex. 5-13 Mozart, Eine kleine Nachtmusik, ii, m.1–12 (Andante): vln1 melody over vln2 broken-chord (Alberti-type)
  figure and quarter-note vc/db, viola silent; viola and *f* enter later; shows the texture change of R19.
- Ex. 5-14 Haydn, Symphony 103, i, m.40–48: theme first in vln1 with vln2/viola only (*p*), then restated an octave
  lower in vln1 with vln2 Alberti 16ths and vc/db added, leading to the *f* wind+brass+timp tutti (R20).
- Ex. 5-15 Beethoven, Piano Concerto 4, ii, m.1–30: *f sempre staccato* string octaves alternating with *pp molto
  cantabile* piano chords (R21).

**Agent applicability**
- Before writing parts, label every line FG/MG/BG; give FG its instrument's best register and BG a contrasting
  rhythm in a less brilliant register.
- For a tender mid-register melody (≈ C4–G4), prefer cello (upper register) over vln1 low; save vln1 an octave up
  for the second, bigger statement.
- Under a cello melody, write violins/violas as soft, quick, interlocking arpeggio figures in their middle register
  (not higher) and basses pizz. on downbeats; low velocity/CC for BG.
- To contrast phrases, alternate tutti octaves (*f*) with melody + parallel-3rds accompaniment (*p*).
- When winds are about to enter, drop the string melody an octave so the winds can be heard.

## Unisons and octave doublings for a stronger statement (Unísonos de cuerda usando duplicaciones a la octava) (scan p.142–145, printed p.129–132)

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] Build a crescendo by adding octaves to the melodic line one section at a time, each entering lower,
  up to *fff* (Beethoven *Leonore* 3).
- R2 [ALLOWED] Start a climactic build with only a few players ("due o tre violini") and add the rest of the
  section/other sections progressively (Leonore 3).
- R3 [ALLOWED] A slow, rubato string unison/octaves line swelling from *pp* to *f* builds strong tension, released
  by *ff* full-orchestra chords (Hindemith).
- R4 [PREFER] Double vln1 with vln2 in unison to give body to a melody in a less brilliant register.
- R5 [PREFER] Keep cellos and basses fairly low and give violas a contrasting rhythm (and *divisi*) so they don't
  interfere with the melody's flow.
- R6 [ALLOWED] Double vln1 in its lowest register with the cellos (unison) on a restatement: adds timbre, a stronger
  sense of resolution, and prepares a following woodwind passage.

**Examples**
- Ex. 5-16 Beethoven, *Leonore* Overture No.3, m.514–531 (Presto): running 8th figure starts in 2–3 violins *cresc.
  poco a poco*; vln2, then violas, then vc/db join in lower octaves, toward *fff*.
- Ex. 5-17 Hindemith, *Mathis der Maler* symphony, iii, m.1–6 ("Sehr langsam, frei im Zeitmaß"): violins, viola and
  cello in octaves, *pp<mf*, rubato, accented, cresc. to *f* with trills; oboes/clarinets enter *p cresc.*; then a
  *ff* accented tutti chord (picc., fl., ob., cl., bsn., 4 hn, tpt, 3 trb + bass trb, timp. roll, snare, cymbal).
- Ex. 5-18 Schumann, Symphony 2, iii, m.1–5 (Adagio espressivo): vln1+vln2 unison *p cantabile*; violas *divisi* in
  off-beat 16th figures; cellos and basses in octaves on a low cantabile bass (R4, R5). Strings alone open the slow
  movement.
- Ex. 5-19 Shostakovich, Symphony 6, iii, m.1–7 (Presto, half = 152): vln1 *p* staccato tune over short *p* chords
  (vln2 double stops, viola, vc/db on the beat); in the restatement (m.4–6) cellos double vln1 in its low register,
  then piccolo, flute, Eb/Bb/bass clarinets take a scale passage (R6).

**Agent applicability**
- For a build-up: start the line in a small group (lower CC/velocity, maybe one violin track), add vln2, viola, then
  vc/db each an octave lower, raising CC1 across the bars.
- For a slow warm string opening: vln1 = vln2 unison melody, violas divisi off-beat figures, vc/db octaves low.

## Orquestación contrapuntística para la cuerda — contrapuntal string writing (scan p.146–148, printed p.133–135; continues)

**Concepts**
- Contrapuntal texture: two or more superimposed melodies. In orchestral music there is a "poorly defined area"
  between foreground, middleground and background melodic material.

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] Decide which melody is primary (foreground), secondary (middleground) and tertiary (background), so each
  communicates its rank clearly.
- R2 [PREFER] Limit colour options instead of using every possibility, especially when a passage repeats and a
  different scoring is planned for the repeat.
- R3 [ALLOWED] Homogeneous registers can still work when (a) each layer has its own characteristic rhythm and
  articulation, and (b) the foreground and middleground themes were heard earlier without the background
  (Beethoven 7/ii).
- R4 [ALLOWED] Strings suit fugue, canon and other contrapuntal styles, since each string voice can play very
  similar gestures. Strings often state a fugue/fugato subject first, before the material is developed, altered or
  doubled by other sections. [era: from early Baroque — Italian masters, then Bach and Handel]
- R5 [MUST] In strings-only counterpoint aim for clarity and balance by:
  (1) giving the most important melody the best register of an instrument;
  (2) thinning the counterpoint so the main theme emerges;
  (3) separating registers of the theme and its counterpoints (one high, one low, or vice versa);
  (4) making the counterpoint's rhythm different enough from the theme that they don't interfere.
- R6 [ALLOWED] Different dynamics (FG *f*, BG *p*) help, but are less effective than R5's register/rhythm methods.
- R7 [era: Baroque] Traditional fugue-entry order goes from low to high: basses (+ solo cello) → violas → vln2 →
  vln1 (Vivaldi Op.3/11).
- R8 [era: Baroque] Other orders: starting with vln1 means reversing (vln1 → vln2 → vla → basses), a favourite of
  Baroque masters. Starting with vln2: vln1, vla, cb or vla, vln1, cb. Starting with viola: vla, vln2, vln1, cb or
  vla, cb, vln2, vln1.
- R9 [PREFER] A fugue subject stands out in a homogeneous string group through (a) rhythmic contrast with the
  counterpoint (middleground) and continuo/harmony (background), (b) being heard alone first, (c) easily recognised
  rhythmic figures, (d) an anacrusis (pickup) opening that gives it thrust.

**Examples**
- Ex. 5-20 Beethoven, Symphony 7, ii, m.51–66 (Andante, 2/4): vln1 simple quarter/8th melody (*p*, staccato/tenuto),
  vln2 legato long-note countermelody, viola and cello short off-beat 8th/16th figures, basses on the beat; all
  *cresc. poco a poco*. Three layers in similar registers, separated by rhythm and articulation.
- Vivaldi, Concerto Grosso Op.3 No.11 (discussion only in this range; example on later pages): fugal exposition
  starting with basses + solo cello in unison, then violas, vln2, vln1.

**Agent applicability**
- For fugato in strings: enter low to high (db/vc → vla → vln2 → vln1), each subject entry alone or over sparse
  counterpoint; give the counter-subject a different rhythmic value (e.g. subject in 8ths, counter-subject in
  quarters/16ths).
- Check every contrapuntal bar: does the FG line have its own register zone or a rhythm no other part shares? If
  not, thin or move a line.
