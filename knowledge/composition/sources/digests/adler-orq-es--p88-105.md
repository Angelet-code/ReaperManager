## Ch.3 — Viola, end of section: doublings (scan p.88, printed p.71)

**Examples**
- Berlioz, *Symphonie fantastique* I, mm.155–159 (Ex.3-39): Vln I and violas play the same line, ff with
  sf swells; violas reinforce the violins an octave below (octave inferred from clef positions).
- Beethoven, Symphony No.5 II, mm.1–10 (Ex.3-40): violas and cellos in unison, *p dolce*, Andante 3/8 in Ab —
  the classic viola+cello unison melody: warmer and darker than cello alone.
- Additional study pointer: Hindemith *Der Schwanendreher* I; Stravinsky *Rite* Part II mm.56–62;
  Tchaikovsky *Romeo and Juliet* mm.114–116.

**Agent applicability**
- For a warm, dark mid-register melody, double cello with viola at the unison (Beethoven 5 model).
- To thicken a ff violin melody, add violas an octave below (Berlioz model).

## Ch.3 — Solo viola (La viola solista) (scan p.88–89, printed p.71–72)

**Concepts**
- History: Baroque masters wrote many viola concertos; after that, little important solo viola music until
  late-19th-c. Wagner and Strauss (exceptions: Mozart *Sinfonia concertante* K.364, Berlioz *Harold en Italie*).
  In the 20th c. the viola reached near-equality with the other strings (Debussy Sonata for flute, viola and
  harp; Bartók and Walton concertos; Hindemith *Der Schwanendreher*; Vaughan Williams *Flos Campi*).
  `[era: solo viola rare c.1760–1880]`

**Examples**
- Scriabin, *Poem of Ecstasy* mm.22–25 (Ex.3-41): short solo viola phrase, *p espressivo*, slow 6/8 — viola
  as an intimate expressive voice inside a large orchestra.
- R. Strauss, *Don Quixote* Var.2, mm.1–19 (Ex.3-42): solo viola (Sancho Panza), "rather free", rapid
  slurred figuration, triplets, alto↔treble clef changes, mf/pp/f contrasts — virtuoso character writing.
- Stravinsky, *Rite of Spring*, "Mysterious Circles of the Young Girls" at [91] (Ex.3-43): **6 solo violas**
  divisi in close parallel harmony, Andante con moto q=60, *molto cantabile ma non f*, changing meters
  4/4–2/4–3/4 — a divided-viola choir as a unique soft, veiled color.
- Additional study pointer: Berg *Wozzeck* I/1; Berlioz *Harold* I mm.38–68; Wagner *Meistersinger*
  Act II sc.3 & 7, Act III sc.5.

**Agent applicability**
- A divided viola section (up to 6 parts) in close voicing, p–mf, cantabile, is a valid self-contained
  pad/chorale color (Rite model).

## Ch.3 — Viola d'amore (Viola de amor) (scan p.90–92, printed p.73–75)

**Concepts**
- viola d'amore (viola d'amore IT., viole d'amour FR., Liebesgeige DE): never a regular orchestra member;
  used for solo/chamber color and solo passages in larger works. `[era: Baroque & Romantic-opera color]`
- Sympathetic strings (cuerdas simpáticas): 7 bowed/fingered strings plus 7 steel sympathetic strings running
  under them, normally tuned in unison with the bowed strings; the characteristic ringing comes from them.
- Slightly larger than a modern violin.

**Rules**
- R1 [PREFER] Usual tuning (D major): A2 D3 A3 D4 F#4 A4 D5 (MIDI 45 50 57 62 66 69 74) (read from Ex.3-44;
  lowest note slightly uncertain [?]).
- R2 [ALLOWED] Common retuning for D minor: lower F#4 to F4 (A2 D3 A3 D4 F4 A4 D5). Other tunings let the
  sympathetic strings ring in other keys. Hindemith (Sonata Op.25/4; *Kammermusik No.6* Op.46/1) and Vivaldi
  (6-string concertos, several chordal tunings) used special tunings (Ex.3-45–3-48; exact pitches [?]).
- R3 [PREFER] Write in alto clef and treble clef. `[era: older music]` low strings were sometimes written in
  bass clef sounding an octave higher — check when reading old parts.
- R4 Register (Ex.3-49): A2 up to about A6 (MIDI 45–~93) [? top note read from 4 ledger lines above treble].
- R5 [PREFER] Chords and arpeggios are easy, especially in keys related to the tuning (D major/minor).
- R6 [ALLOWED] Natural harmonics are easy up to the 5th harmonic; artificial harmonics possible, the
  "touch the 4th" type most satisfactory. All violin fingering, bowing and color techniques are available.

**Examples**
- Repertoire named: Bach *St John Passion* ("Erwäge", mm.1–5) and cantatas; Meyerbeer *Les Huguenots*;
  Massenet *Le Jongleur de Notre-Dame*; Pfitzner *Palestrina*; Loeffler *La Mort de Tintagiles*; Puccini
  *Madama Butterfly* Act II (accompanies the offstage women's chorus).
- Meyerbeer, *Les Huguenots* I, "Ah! quel spectacle", mm.1–19 (Ex.3-51): viola d'amore obbligato introducing a
  tenor recitative (Raoul) in D major: chords, arpeggios, alto/treble clef, ends with a passage marked
  *Flageolettöne* (harmonics) in sextuplets.
- Hindemith, *Kleine Sonate* II, mm.14–37 (Ex.3-52): slow lyrical line ("Sehr langsam"), chromatic, wide range
  from alto into treble clef, p–f.

**Agent applicability**
- If a library offers viola d'amore, keep parts centered on D-major/minor harmony, use arpeggiated chords
  and harmonics; range A2–~A6.

## Ch.3 — The cello (El violonchelo) (scan p.92–100, printed p.75–83)

**Concepts**
- cello (violoncello IT., violoncelle FR., Violoncell DE): ambivalently the **tenor and the bass** of the string
  section. Held between the knees with an endpin; left hand free from supporting the instrument, so the thumb
  can be used in high positions.
- thumb position (posición de pulgar): thumb placed across the strings as a finger; sign in the score is a
  circle with a stem (♀-like). From 7th position the thumb leaves the neck to reach upper notes.

**Rules — tuning, clefs, register**
- R1 Tuning: C2 G2 D3 A3 (MIDI 36 43 50 57), strings IV–I.
- R2 [MUST] Write in bass clef, tenor clef (C on 4th line) or treble clef; change clef to avoid many ledger
  lines (Ex.3-54: bass → tenor → treble as the line rises).
- R3 [MUST] Today all cello parts in bass, tenor or treble clef **sound as written**.
  `[era: some older (19th-c.) editions]` treble-clef cello passages sounded an octave lower than written —
  beware when transcribing from old scores.
- R4 Register (Ex.3-55): all chromatic notes from C2 (36) up to about G5 (79); extreme top extends to about
  E6 (88) [?], harmonics above.

**Rules — fingering**
- R5 In 1st position the normal span from 1st to 4th finger is a (minor) third; the 2nd finger is used only for
  chromatic notes. Higher up the intervals get physically closer and all four fingers are used, like the viola.
- R6 [ALLOWED] With the thumb, octave double stops on adjacent strings are relatively easy (high register).
- R7 [AVOID] Double stops wider than a sixth in the low register (difficult).
- Fingering chart (p.77): 1st–5th positions shown by first-finger placement; half-step layout on each string.

**Rules — sound quality per string**
- R8 D string: most captivating, lyrical and warm — put lyrical cello melodies here (D3–~D4 region; inferred).
- R9 A string: most brilliant and penetrating.
- R10 G string: least volume, projects worst.
- R11 C string: rich, sonorous bass due to weight and thickness.
- R12 The cello can execute almost any technical feat possible on violin or viola (Berlioz's claim that it is
  not agile is wrong today).

**Rules — divisi, multiple stops, harmonics**
- R13 [PREFER] Cellos in divisi produce a very rich effect (Rossini model below).
- R14 [ALLOWED] Double, triple and quadruple stops: Adler gives a partial list (Ex.3-60–3-62); doubles shown
  as open string + stopped note and as fingered pairs in fifths/sixths/thirds/octaves with fingerings.
  Chords in the lists are built on adjacent strings, mostly using the open C/G/D strings in the lower notes
  (inferred from examples).
- R15 Natural harmonics work as on the other strings and are **even more secure** on cello (longer, heavier
  strings). Ex.3-63 shows the natural harmonic series on the G (III) and C (IV) strings.
- R16 [PREFER] For orchestral writing use "touch-the-4th" artificial harmonics: thumb stops the fundamental,
  3rd or 4th finger touches a perfect 4th above; sounds two octaves above the stopped note; usable in the
  whole range.

**Rules — combinations**
- R17 The cello doubles well with instruments of every section. Most used doublings: cello+double bass,
  cello+bassoon, cello+clarinet or bass clarinet, cello+horn, cello *pizzicato*+timpani.
- R18 [ALLOWED] Cello section part divided so that one half doubles the violas (Barber model).

**Examples**
- Wagner, *Tristan* Prelude mm.17–32 (Ex.3-57): cello section melody in tenor clef, 6/8 slow, p→f→p
  arches — the cello as the sustained lyrical lead.
- Harris, Symphony No.3 I, mm.1–27 (Ex.3-58): cellos alone open the work, Moderato q=84, f, long modal
  line in bass clef, divisi near the end (m.22) and moving into tenor clef.
- Additional study: Beethoven *Eroica* I mm.1–8; Brahms Sym.3 III mm.1–12; Brahms Sym.2 III mm.194–202.
- Rossini, *William Tell* Overture mm.1–10 (Ex.3-59): **5 solo cellos**, Andante q=54; cello 1 has the
  espressivo solo with an arpeggio flourish rising into treble clef, cellos 2–5 enter p with soft sustained
  chords — divided cellos as a complete chamber choir.
- Additional divisi study: Debussy *La Mer* I (throughout); Mahler *Das Lied von der Erde* V mm.1–4 at [10];
  R. Strauss *Also sprach Zarathustra* (cellos and basses divided, 18 bars from 1 bar before [10]).
- Cello concerto repertoire (one-line pointer): Boccherini, Haydn, Beethoven Triple, Schumann, Brahms Double,
  Dvořák, Tchaikovsky *Rococo Variations*, Lalo, Herbert, Milhaud, Bloch *Schelomo*, Hindemith, Barber, Walton,
  Lutosławski, Penderecki, S. Albert, C. Rouse, J. Tower. Harmonics study pointer: Corigliano *Phantasmagoria*.
- R. Strauss, *Don Quixote* mm.163–176 (Ex.3-64): orchestral solo cello (the Don), mf→f, moves through
  bass, tenor and treble clef, 12/8 lyrical figuration with harmonics at the top, *grazioso*.
- Additional solo study: Brahms Piano Concerto No.2 III mm.71–86; Haydn Sym.95 II (whole) and III trio.
- Barber, *Essay for Orchestra No.1* mm.1–10 (Ex.3-65): violas div., cellos div. (2nd cellos doubling 1st violas),
  basses div. then unis., p espressivo — a dark low-strings-only chorale.
- Additional combination study: Beethoven Sym.5 III mm.162–179; Glinka *Ruslan and Lyudmila* Overture
  mm.81–100; Schubert Sym.9 introduction (cello and viola double each other throughout); Verdi *Falstaff* I,
  2 bars after [8] for 8 bars (cello and piccolo melody **five octaves apart**).

**Agent applicability**
- Cello MIDI range for orchestral writing: 36–79 safely; 80–88 only for soloistic climaxes.
- For a lyrical cello lead, center it on the D string (D3–A3+); for cutting brilliance use the A string (A3 up).
- Low cello lines on the G string will be weaker than on C or D — boost by doubling (bassoon/bass) if needed.
- A cello choir of 4–5 divided parts in close voicing, p, is a valid intimate texture.
- Default doublings for a bass/tenor line: Vc+Cb (octave), Vc+Bsn, Vc+Cl/B.Cl, Vc+Hn; pizz Vc with timpani.

## Ch.3 — The double bass (El contrabajo) (scan p.100–103, printed p.83–85, 88)

**Concepts**
- double bass (contrabbasso IT., contrebasse FR., Kontrabass DE): the bass voice of the string section.
  Played standing or sitting on a high stool; rests on an adjustable endpin.
- Construction differences from the violin family: sloping shoulders instead of curved; geared machine
  tuning pegs for the thick strings.
- Almost all orchestral players use a full-size instrument; smaller solo models are easier but lack the true
  bass sound.

**Rules — tuning, notation, register**
- R1 [MUST] The bass is written an **octave higher than it sounds** (Ex.3-67). MIDI must be entered at
  sounding pitch (written − 12).
- R2 Tuning (4-string): sounding E1 A1 D2 G2 (MIDI 28 33 38 43); written E2 A2 D3 G3.
- R3 5-string bass adds a low string (Ex.3-66), written about C2 = sounding C1 (MIDI 24) [? pitch of the
  5th string not fully legible].
- R4 Practical orchestral range: written E2 to B4 = sounding E1–B3 (MIDI 28–59). Higher notes for solo
  passages come from natural harmonics.
- R5 Register chart (Ex.3-68): chromatic from written C2 (extension/5-string, sounding C1 = 24) / E2 up to
  about written Bb4–B4 [?], filled-note extension to about written F5 (sounding F4 = 65) [?], harmonics above.
- R6 [MUST] Write in bass, tenor or treble clef; change clef to avoid notes with three or more ledger lines.

**Rules — sound and articulation**
- R7 A single bass sounds weak and distant (Mahler Sym.1 III opening solo); a bass section can sound loud and
  powerful (Beethoven Sym.5 III trio).
- R8 Articulation is slower than any other string instrument (thick heavy strings). Remember this when
  doubling cellos with basses in fast passages.
- R9 [PREFER] In fast passages doubled by Vc+Cb, give the basses a **simplified version** (e.g. keep cellos on
  the full 16th-note figure and let basses play only the beat notes / first notes of groups with rests),
  so the bottom does not sound muddy — especially with a large modern orchestra (Beethoven 4 model).
  `[era: Beethoven wrote full doublings for smaller period orchestras; modern conductors thin the bass part]`

**Rules — fingering**
- R10 Intervals are physically far apart: in the low positions 1st→4th finger spans only a **major 2nd**.
- R11 Up to and including 5th position the 3rd finger is not used independently (it goes down with the 4th):
  positions 1–5 use fingers 1, 2, 4. In 6th position the 3rd finger is sometimes used; from 7th position the
  thumb may be needed.
- Ex.3-71 lists positions: half, 1st, 1½, 2nd, 3rd, 3½, 4th, 4½, 5th, 6th, 6½, 7th, 7½, with fingerings per
  string (Sul Mi/La/Re/Sol).
- R12 [PREFER] Approach the upper register by small intervals/steps, [AVOID] large leaps into it: the player
  must reposition the hand on very long strings. The upper register is effective in orchestral music if used
  with common sense about how the player will execute it.

**Examples**
- Mahler Sym.1 III opening: solo bass = weak, distant, eerie color.
- Beethoven Sym.5 III trio: bass section = strong, powerful fugato lead.
- Beethoven, Symphony No.4 IV, mm.319–323 (Ex.3-69): Vc+Cb in unison on fast 16th-note figuration, pp, under
  sustained violins/violas; Ex.3-70 shows the practical clarification: cellos keep the figure, basses play a
  thinned version with rests.
- Wagner, *Meistersinger* Overture mm.158–172 (Ex.3-72): characteristic marcato bass-section line,
  mf "aber sehr markiert", gradual crescendo, ending on a trill; in the opera doubled by the tuba.
- Milhaud, *La Création du monde*, 1 bar before [11] to 1 bar before [12] (Ex.3-75): bass solo, "quite fast",
  p, syncopated accented jazz line rising into tenor clef — bass as a jazz-flavored melodic voice in the
  upper register.
- Additional study pointer: Mahler Sym.2 II (around [4]); Mahler Sym.1 III mm.3–10; Persichetti *Symphony for
  Strings* I mm.16–20; Saint-Saëns *Carnival* No.5 "L'éléphant"; R. Strauss *Zarathustra* 8–22 after [9];
  Stravinsky *Rite* mm.57–66; Verdi *Falstaff* Act III opening (basses begin alone).

**Agent applicability**
- Bass MIDI (sounding) range: 28–59 for normal orchestral parts; 24–27 only if a 5-string/C-extension bass is
  assumed; 60–65 only for solo/harmonic effects.
- When copying a fast cello line to the basses, simplify the bass copy (beat notes, fewer repeated 16ths).
- For a weak/distant color use one solo bass; for power use the full section, often doubled by tuba or
  contrabassoon (inferred from Meistersinger note).
- Move bass lines into the high register stepwise, not by big leaps.

## Ch.4 — Plucked strings: introduction (scan p.104, printed p.89)

**Concepts**
- Of the plucked instruments (instrumentos de cuerda punteados) only the harp is a regular member of the
  modern symphony orchestra; the others appear increasingly in smaller ensembles. A composer must know, for
  each, the number and tuning of strings, range, playing method and notation.

## Ch.4 — The harp (El arpa): history, tuning and register (scan p.104–105, printed p.89–90)

**Concepts**
- harp (arpa IT., harpe FR., Harfe DE). The modern **double-action pedal harp** (arpa de doble movimiento)
  developed from the late 18th to early 19th century.
- `[era: before double action]` its immediate predecessor, the chromatic harp, had no pedals and one string
  per semitone: too many strings for the player, poor tone, and no diatonic or chordal glissando.
- Pedals: 7 pedals, one per pitch class, each with 3 positions (up, middle, down). Each string's top is tied
  to a tuning peg, its bottom linked to a pedal.

**Rules**
- R1 47 strings, six full octaves plus six notes. Range: Cb1 to G#7 (MIDI 23–104) (Ex.4-1; footnote names the
  lowest strings DO1/RE1 and the top SOL7). C, D and E are the extra notes at the bottom, F and G at the top.
- R2 Pedal positions: up = flat (Cb major scale); middle = natural (C D E F G A B); down = sharp
  (C# D# E# F# G# A# B#). Each lowered position raises the string by a semitone.
  [Note: the translation says the harp is "tuned in C major" with all pedals up, but its own list says Cb
  major for the top position; Cb major is the consistent reading.]
- R3 [NEVER] Write two different spellings of the same pitch class in different octaves at the same moment
  (e.g. Cb in one octave and C natural in another): one pedal controls **all** strings of that letter (6 C
  strings at once). This is a frequent orchestrator mistake.
- R4 [PREFER] Solve such conflicts enharmonically: e.g. write Cb as B natural (B string, B pedal) so that Cb and
  C natural sound on different strings controlled by different pedals.
- R5 The lowest 12 strings are wire-wound with a steel core; the rest are described as wound gut (as given in
  the translation). C strings are red, G strings blue (as stated in the translation).
- R6 Footnote: the pedal mechanism does not reach the two lowest strings (D1, C1); they must be tuned by hand.
  `[era: until c. the late 1990s]` the top string G7 was also hand-tuned; now its G pedal controls it. So the
  lowest C/D (and on older harps the top G) need manual retuning if a note change is required mid-piece; the
  harpist tunes them before the piece relative to the first pitch needed.
- R7 Tuning a harp is tedious; harpists tune long before the rest of the orchestra and must check tuning often.

**Agent applicability**
- Harp MIDI range 23–104; treat 23–26 (lowest C/D strings) as fixed per piece (no pedal changes).
- Before writing a harp part, compute one pedal setting per pitch class at each moment; reject chords/lines
  that need, e.g., C and Cb (or F and F#) simultaneously unless respelled enharmonically onto another string.
