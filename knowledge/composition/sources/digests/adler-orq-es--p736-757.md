## 17 (cont.) Ex. 17-20: Schoenberg, *Theme and Variations*, Var. 4 "Tempo di valse" (scan p.736–741, printed p.735–741)

Example 17-20 started earlier (printed p.732, CD-5 track 86 band / track 87 orchestra). Here: end of
Op. 43a (band, bars 134–148) and the complete Op. 43b (orchestra, bars 106–148). Half note = 60, 3/4.
Score marks: bracketed **P** = principal voice (Hauptstimme), bracketed **S** = secondary voice
(Nebenstimme). No prose on these pages; the observations below are read from the score.

**Concepts**
- Principal / secondary voice marks (Hauptstimme / Nebenstimme, shown as ⌈P / ⌈S ending with ⌉):
  Schoenberg's bracket that tells players and conductor which line must lead and which is the
  counter-line.

**Rules** (all read from the scores, i.e. what the example demonstrates)
- R1 [PREFER] When moving a band piece to orchestra, give the band's soft accompanying chord figure
  (muted trumpets + tambourine in Op. 43a) to divided 2nd violins *col legno battuto* p, keeping muted
  C trumpets for the short off-beat chords. *Why:* the dry, percussive string tap replaces the
  band's percussive muted-brass colour. (inferred from comparison)
- R2 [PREFER] Give a band's muted baritone secondary line (⌈S, mp) to **one solo double bass con sordino**
  (and a second solo bass later); give the band's solo clarinet/flute phrases to **solo viola con
  sordino, p dolce**, later two solo violas. Soloistic muted low strings keep the chamber transparency
  of the band's solo winds.
- R3 [ALLOWED] Use string colour changes as orchestral "extras" the band lacked: 2nd violins *arco sul
  ponticello* tremolo chords (bar 122), pizz. (bar 140), and a later "tutti senza sord." entry of violas
  and cellos at f (bar 133) to mark the growth toward the climax.
- R4 [PREFER] At the climax (orch. bar 136, ff) double the principal melody in Picc + Fl a2 + Ob a2 +
  Cl and both violin sections (violins partly 8va), while 4 horns + 2 C trumpets (open) play the
  accented staccato rhythmic figure, joined by divided violas; trombones and tuba sustain with
  < > swells; Bs Cl, bassoons, contrabassoon, cellos and basses (senza sord.) carry the bass line f.
  In the band version the same figure is horns + cornets + baritone + tuba, and the melody is the
  whole clarinet choir + Eb Cl + flugelhorns + flutes/piccolo.
- R5 [PREFER] Band-only instruments map by function: flugelhorns/cornets/clarinet choir (melody mass)
  → violins + woodwinds; saxophones (sustained harmony, swells) → trombones / strings; baritone
  (secondary voice) → solo low string or trombone; string bass → double bass. (inferred)
- R6 [PREFER] For the quiet close (orch. bars 142–148, rit.): oboe + English horn and horns 1/3 hold p
  chords; one trombone takes the ⌈P line p with a <> swell; violas, cellos and basses p dolce; timpani
  pp roll; triangle p. The band version closes with flugelhorn and euphonium p dolce, tuba p dolce,
  timpani pp and triangle/tambourine p.
- R7 [ALLOWED] Keyboard percussion carry short colour points that double the melody: glockenspiel
  in band and orchestra; xylophone in the orchestra (Perc. 1, mp, bar 132). (score observation)

**Agent applicability**
- When converting a wind-band arrangement to orchestra in MIDI, remap by function (R5), not by
  name. Use solo, muted low-string tracks (keyswitch "con sord." / solo patch) for exposed secondary
  lines, and col legno / sul pont. patches for dry chord figures.
- Tag principal / secondary lines in the arrangement spec, then check that the principal line has
  the most doublings (or the clearest solo colour) in every bar.

## 17 Transcription for diverse conventional ensembles (*Transcripción para diversas combinaciones instrumentales convencionales*) (scan p.742–757, printed p.741–756)

**Concepts**
- Transcription for a conventional, uneven ensemble: adapting an orchestral, chamber or piano work
  to whatever forces a school or community group has, with the players' skill as a constraint.
- Section labels (*tutti, mezzo tutti, soli, solo*): marks written on the piano reduction to plan
  density before choosing instruments.
- Comfortable amateur ranges: given in the book's Appendix A (not in this range).

**Rules**
- R8 [ALLOWED] Any orchestral, chamber or piano work may be adapted, even if it has a solo (e.g. an
  English-horn solo) that "would not sound right on any other instrument". The result will always
  sound different from the original. *Why:* respect for the piece and knowledge of its form and
  original orchestration are enough for a satisfying transcription.
- R9 [MUST] Account for the mixed nature of the instruments and for each player's technical limits.
  The author treats these limits as a spur to imagination.
- R10 [MUST] Step 1: reduce the orchestral work to a piano score. For a very complex work a
  four-hands (*piano a cuatro manos*) or two-piano score is even more useful. Adler suggests making
  your own reduction when the target group is particular.
- R11 [MUST] Step 2: label each passage of the reduction as tutti, mezzo tutti, soli or solo.
- R12 [MUST] Step 3: mark every solo and check whether the group has a suitable soloist. If a
  substitute is needed, choose (a) a very similar instrument, or (b) a combination of two instruments
  that gives a similar result. If neither is possible, (c) choose an instrument with the range and
  dynamic the solo needs.
- R13 [PREFER] Choose substitutes by experiment and analysis, not from a fixed substitution table.
  Weigh range, tone quality, the register the passage sits in, and the skill of the specific player.
- R14 [PREFER] Use the piano as one more orchestral instrument rather than having it play the whole
  reduction. Good uses: filling out tutti sections, arpeggiated accompaniments, harp parts, and
  occasional solo lines or doubling of solos, perhaps in the high register.
- R15 [AVOID] Writing percussion except where the piece clearly calls for it. *Why:* too much
  percussion covers the rest of the ensemble, whatever its size.
- R16 [AVOID] Extreme registers (both low and high) for amateur players. On flute, oboe, bassoon,
  horn, trumpet, tuba and their auxiliaries, the **lowest notes** are especially problematic.
  Exceeding the comfortable ranges much makes the transcription impractical however brilliant it is.
- R17 [PREFER] If you can choose the piece, choose a relatively unknown one. *Why:* (1) nobody
  compares it with the original; (2) players and audience hear unfamiliar repertoire.
- R18 [PREFER] Keep the weakest or least confident section (here: amateur brass) out of the opening
  and bring it in only at the intensification. Give exposed solos to the most skilled individual
  player, even if the original gave them to a different instrument.
- R19 [PREFER] Change the instrument colour at each new statement of a theme and of its
  accompaniment. *Why:* gives the transcription more colour, and reproduces the timbre changes of the
  original with the forces available.
- R20 [MUST] Reinforce the bass line in amateur groups (the double bass carries the bass throughout;
  euphonium or alto sax double it at key points). *Why:* in amateur groups the bass part often needs
  reinforcing.
- R21 [ALLOWED] When the ensemble lacks a section (horns, double reeds, violas), redistribute its
  function to whatever is there. One or two trombones or a tuba, if available, would be easy to add.

**Exceptions / conditions**
- Model amateur ensemble used for both examples, with each player's level: 2 flutes (fairly good);
  3 Bb clarinets (1st very good, others acceptable); 1 alto sax (fairly good); 2 Bb trumpets
  (acceptable); 1 euphonium (very good); 1 timpanist on three drums (weak at retuning); 1 percussionist
  (does not count well); 5 first violins (3 excellent up to 5th position, 2 good up to 3rd); 4 second
  violins (mediocre, 1st position only); 3 cellos (1st excellent, two mediocre); 1 double bass (not
  very competent). The author calls this typical: no double reeds, no horns, no violas.
  - Constraint for agents (inferred): 2nd violins in 1st position on the E string reach about B5
    (MIDI 83); the timpani part should avoid retuning mid-passage.

**Examples**
- Ex. 17-21, Schumann, Piano Quintet Op. 44, mvt 2, bars 1–16 (C minor, *In modo d'una marcia, un poco
  largamente*, half = 66). In the original the theme is in Vln 1 (from bar 4 per the text), later in
  Vln 2 (bar 11), over a march accompaniment of piano chords and low strings.
  The transcription (CD-5 track 88) does the following:
  - The theme gets two different colours: clarinet 1 (*sempre p ma marcato*), then 2 Bb trumpets.
    The trumpet gives the theme a new character.
  - Bass drum ppp and timpani pp add a touch of pathos.
  - The opening piano line goes to 2 flutes an octave higher, in thirds/sixths, p. It returns at bar 10.
    This widens the range and adds sheen.
  - Under the clarinet, the march chords go to strings pp (Vln 1/2, divided cellos, double bass) for
    softness. When the trumpets take the theme (bar 11), the accompaniment moves to the winds
    (clarinets 2–3, alto sax) and the strings switch to pizzicato pp. The euphonium (pp) doubles the
    bass line.
  - Notation: the Bb clarinets are written in F major (1 flat) for concert Eb. The trumpets are
    written with no key signature.
- Ex. 17-22, Suppé, *Poet and Peasant* Overture (*Poeta y Aldeano*), bars 1–35 (D major, Andante
  maestoso). Three versions: (a) orchestral original, (b) piano four-hands, (c) new transcription
  (CD-5 track 89).
  - Original (a): opens with a p chorale for horns in D, D trumpets, trombones and a bass brass part
    (labelled "Euph." in the book). The brass swells to ff at bar 7. Strings answer pp, with bass drum
    *senza piatti* pp.
  - Original, bars 13–15: tutti ff (piccolo, flute, oboes, C clarinets, bassoons in chords; strings
    in a descending triplet scale); fff with *con piatti* and a timpani roll.
  - Original, bar 16: harp arpeggios pp under a solo-cello melody (tenor clef, mf), with C clarinets
    and bassoons holding pp chords.
  - Original, bar 31: piccolo and flute play a pp triplet figure; strings repeat triplet chords pp;
    the double bass is pizz.
  - New version (c), bars 1–8: begins as a **string fanfare** (Vln 1/2; cellos *non div.* in double
    stops; double bass; timpani p). The amateur brass (2 Bb trumpets + euphonium, p) enter only at
    the intensification in bar 5.
  - Bar 9: the colour moves from strings to clarinets (+ alto sax), mirroring the original's move from
    brass to strings. Trumpets and euphonium join the crescendo to ff at bar 13. A snare drum p roll
    leads into it.
  - Bar 13 tutti: 2 flutes, clarinets and trumpets in chords ff. Alto sax, euphonium, cellos and bass
    play the descending triplet scale. A cymbal is added.
  - Bar 16: the theme goes to a **solo violin** (one of the group's best players). The clarinet takes
    the harp's arpeggios in triplet eighths. Soft sustained strings (Vln 2, cellos, bass) imitate the
    harp's bass notes and the sustained woodwinds. The author notes that this clarinet + strings
    texture is close to traditional 19th-century orchestration.
  - Bars 23–27: the arpeggios go to the violins (Vln 1 tutti, then Vln 2). Clarinets hold sustained
    chords (originally harp, clarinets and bassoons). The melody moves to the flutes (originally
    cello). The alto sax plays bass notes. Trumpet 1 is muted.
  - Bar 28: clarinets return to the arpeggios under the melody, now played by the whole first-violin
    section.
  - Bar 30: a cadenza-like solo for violin 1 (with fermata) leads to the new triplet accompaniment.
    In the transcription that accompaniment is played only by woodwinds and brass, plus timpani pp
    and double bass arco *sim.* In the original it was in the strings.
  - Bars 33–35: the second theme is in the violins (the text says "violins and violas", but the
    score has Vln 1, Vln 2 and cellos, mf, arco). The author finds this fresh compared with the
    original solo cello.
  - Notation: the Bb clarinets are written in E major (4 sharps) for concert D; the Eb alto sax in
    B major (5 sharps); the trumpets with no key signature.
- Further transcriptions for study, listed only: Bach–Casella, Bach–Klenovsky (Wood), Bach–Walton,
  Debussy–Ansermet, Debussy–Henkemans, Fauré–Rabaud, Schubert–Weiner, Shostakovich–Kelemen,
  Sweelinck–Mul.

**Agent applicability**
- When asked to arrange an existing piece for a given (possibly odd) instrument set, follow the
  workflow (steps 1–3, i.e. R10–R12):
  1. Build a 2-staff reduction (melody, bass, harmony) from the source MIDI.
  2. Tag each section tutti / mezzo tutti / soli / solo.
  3. Assign solos by similar instrument, then by a two-instrument blend, then by range and dynamic fit.
- When sample-library realism matters, treat "weak player" constraints as range constraints. Keep
  parts inside comfortable ranges and avoid the lowest notes of flute, oboe, bassoon, horn, trumpet
  and tuba (R16).
- Write piano as an ensemble member (doubling tuttis, arpeggios, harp substitute), never as a
  continuous reduction (R14).
- Use percussion sparingly; bass drum ppp or timpani pp can colour a march without covering it (R15).
- Re-orchestrate each repeat of a theme with a new melody colour and a new accompaniment colour
  (R19). Keep the bass line doubled through the whole piece (R20).
- Common substitutions shown: harp arpeggios → clarinet (triplet eighths) or violins; a solo-cello
  melody → flute, solo violin or the violin section; an opening brass chorale → strings in double
  stops + timpani.
