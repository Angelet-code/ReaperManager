# Hindemith, Armonía tradicional II (exercises for advanced courses) — scans 001–066

Nature of the volume: not a theory text. Each "exercise" (ejercicio) fixes a **chord vocabulary** (taken from vol. 1)
and then gives sketches of real pieces (songs, choirs, canons, chamber music, fugues, sonata movements) that the
student completes. The usable content is (a) the allowed chord material per stage, (b) the working instructions
attached to each piece, and (c) a full **planning method** (Sexto ejercicio) for laying out form, tonality, tension,
harmonic rhythm, texture and thickness before writing a note. The sketches themselves are not transcribed here.

## Prólogo (scan p.3–4, unnumbered)

**Concepts**
- Purpose: practice material that pushes technically capable musicians to real compositional results (pieces from
  piano solo to string orchestra, song to mixed choir, dance miniatures to sonata movements) without demanding
  "creation" from scratch. Also valuable as technical training for composers.

**Rules**
- R1 [PREFER] Treat harmony exercises as real pieces with instrumentation, tempo, dynamics and form, not as abstract
  chord-connection drills. *Why:* goes beyond the "mechanical" stage of ordinary harmony teaching.

## Primer Ejercicio — basic chord vocabulary, canons, songs, choruses (scan p.6–13, printed p.11–18)

**Concepts**
- Allowed material (scan p.6): (a) I, V, IV in major and minor; (b) all other diatonic triads (major, minor,
  diminished, augmented) — in c minor the example uses harmonic-minor forms: II = D–F–Ab (dim), III = Eb–G–B (aug),
  VI = Ab–C–Eb, VII = B–D–F (dim); (c) V7 with its 3 inversions; (d) V9 and V13/7 (the 13th replaces the 5th:
  in C G–B–F–E; in c the 9th is Ab and the 13th Eb) with inversions; (e) subdominant II6/5 (C: F–A–C–D; c: F–Ab–C–D).
  Upper-case numerals = major, lower-case = minor (footnote).
- Canon (canon): two of four voices in strict imitation; the other two are free.

**Rules — four canons for string quartet (scan p.7–8, printed 12–13)**
- R1 [MUST] Write for 2 violins, viola (alto clef), cello on four staves; one canon voice is given, add the answer.
  Answer intervals: canons 1–2 at the octave below, canon 3 at the fifth below, canon 4 at the major third above.
- R2 [MUST] In canons 1–3 the imitation is interval-exact; in canon 4 the answer uses the tones of the D scale
  (tonal/diatonic adjustment instead of exact intervals).
- R3 [MUST] Compose the two canon voices completely first; only then add the two free voices.
- R4 [MUST] Free voices must be logical, easily understood melodic lines. [AVOID] mere filler voices (voces de relleno).
- R5 [MUST] Limit the free voices' rhythmic activity so the canon voices remain clearly audible: in canons 1 and 4
  (compound meters) hardly any values shorter than a dotted quarter; in canons 2 and 3 the basic unit is the quarter.
- R6 [MUST] When the canon voices lie very close together (canon 3), the harmonic-tonal development depends mainly on
  the outer voices. *Why:* close inner canon gives little harmonic definition by itself.
- R7 [PREFER] Canon 4: watch above all the melodic fluency of the top voice.

**Rules — two songs for soprano (or tenor) and piano, Blake texts (scan p.8–10, printed 13–15)**
- R8 [MUST] Deepen the mood of the song according to the poem's content; follow the given accompaniment models but
  not exclusively.
- R9 [MUST] Counteract the "insubstantial activity" of one constantly repeated accompaniment motive by one or more
  interruptions with other motives — preferably calmer ones.
- R10 [MUST] Fit dynamics to the expression set by the voice and the accompaniment.
- R11 [MUST] The accompaniment must work for both the soprano and the tenor version (an octave apart): avoid tones or
  figures that collide with the vocal line in either register; be especially careful with non-chord tones.
- R12 [PREFER] Join the top notes of the accompaniment into a continuous line of (limited) melodic quality, so that
  in the tenor version the piano still has some top-voice predominance.

**Rules — two choruses for 3 voices S-A-T (scan p.10–11, printed 15–16)**
- R13 [MUST] Write the lowest voice first — it is the basis of harmony and tonal development.
- R14 [AVOID] Bass-type cadential leaps of 4ths and 5ths in a tenor bass (tenor lies mainly in the octave below middle
  C, ~C3–C4). Use the more melodic stepwise/third motion instead. *Why:* such cadences only work in true bass
  register sung by basses; in tenors they sound like a forced imitation.
- R15 [PREFER] Harmonize corresponding bar groups (e.g. bars 1–3 and 6–8) differently.
- R16 [AVOID] Too many eighths in the middle or lower voice (unit = dotted quarter). *Why:* they rob the top voice of
  melodic importance and create a contrapuntal web unsuited to a simple text and line.
- R17 [AVOID] In a fast tempo, values shorter than a half note or half-note triplets (do not "overload" the tempo).
- R18 [MUST] Clarify the metric structure first: group bars into units of 2 or 3, one of which feels accented; place
  the main chords of the key on those accented bars (harmonic and tonal preference).
- R19 [MUST] To get the full effect of a long crescendo (bars 13–20), renounce accentuation during it: avoid the main
  chords of the key and avoid repeating harmonies heard shortly before. *Why:* main chords and repetition create
  points of arrival that stop the build-up.
- R20 [MUST] A choral piece counts as solved only after it has been proven singable by an ensemble; play it on the
  piano afterwards as score-reading practice.

**Rules — two songs for mixed 4-voice choir (scan p.12–13, printed 17–18)**
- R21 [PREFER] Even when only "realizing the harmonies" under a given melody, intensify each piece's expression through
  the voice-leading and rhythmization of the three added voices.
- R22 [MUST] For a second stanza, adapt the melody's rhythm to the new text (strophic songs are not copied
  mechanically).

**Agent applicability**
- Canon generator: generate leader + follower first (transpose by −12, −7 or +4 semitones, or diatonic-map for the
  "tonal" answer), then add free voices with min note length ≥ the canon's beat unit.
- When a bass line is sung/played in tenor range (C3–C4), replace V–I root leaps with stepwise approaches.
- Accompaniment that serves a melody at two octaves (S/T, or a lead that may be doubled): check every piano note
  against both octave placements of the melody for clashes.
- In crescendo/build sections: avoid I and V chords and avoid any chord used in the previous ~2 bars until the arrival.
- Map accented bars (every 2nd or 3rd) to primary chords; weak bars to secondary chords.

## Segundo Ejercicio — diatonic seventh chords (scan p.13–17, printed p.18–22)

**Concepts**
- Added material: seventh chords on I, II, III, IV, VI, VII in major and minor, with inversions (C: Cmaj7, Dm7, Em7,
  Fmaj7, Am7, Bø7; c minor harmonic forms incl. Eb–G–B–D and B–D–F–Ab). Vol. 1 ch. 10.

**Rules — two songs for mezzo-soprano and piano (scan p.14–15, printed 19–20)** (sketch given as voice + figured bass)
- R1 [PREFER] Song 1: eighth-note accompaniment figure in which melodic development is suppressed in favor of broken
  chords.
- R2 [MUST] Support the dynamic swell of the vocal line by carefully graded **chord fullness** = number of notes per
  chord, increased by doublings.
- R3 [PREFER] Song 2: create variety by register: oppose chords in the low register to chords in the high register;
  the given bass register and rhythm need not be followed strictly.
- R4 [MUST] Seventh chords: if the voice sings the chord 7th, decide whether the 7th may also appear in the
  accompaniment in the same octave, or whether it is needed there at all. If the 7th appears only in the piano, choose
  the octave where it stands out best.
- R5 [MUST] Write idiomatically for piano. [AVOID] school-style 4-part chorale harmonization in a piano accompaniment.
- R6 [MUST] Insert precise dynamic markings.

**Rules — two Latin(-text) choruses for mixed 5-voice choir (scan p.15–17, printed 20–22)**
- R7 [MUST] (Piece 1) Compress the five voices into a relatively small total range — this is the main difficulty.
- R8 [MUST] (Piece 2) First 16 bars: write first a top voice of great fluency, felt as the main melody; the bass is
  only the lower frame.
- R9 [MUST] A refrain must differ from the first part by phrasing, expression and dynamics; on its repetition give the
  lead to another voice (here the tenor).
- R10 [ALLOWED] The fifth voice added to SATB = second soprano or second tenor (baritone).
- Style fact: Hindemith regards pre-1500 music as an inexhaustible source of inspiration, comparable to 18th–19th c.

**Agent applicability**
- Velocity/density coupling: for a crescendo, increase chord voice count (add doublings) rather than only velocity.
- If the lead melody holds the 7th of a chord, do not double it in the same octave in the pad/piano; if the 7th is only
  in the accompaniment, put it in an exposed octave (top of the voicing).
- Avoid block 4-voice chorale voicings in piano parts; use broken-chord or register-contrasting figures.
- On a repeated refrain, move the melody to another instrument/voice.

## Tercer Ejercicio — simple alteration (scan p.17–24, printed p.22–29)

**Concepts**
- Simple alteration (alteración simple) — added material:
  - (a) chords from the variable 6th and 7th of melodic minor (shown on C/c): II = D–F–A, III = Eb–G–Bb, IV = F–A–C,
    V = G–Bb–D (minor dominant), VI = A–C–Eb (dim), VII = Bb–D–F.
  - (b) secondary seventh chords produced the same way (in c).
  - (c) in **major**, chords borrowed from minor: minor iv (F–Ab–C), bVI (Ab–C–Eb), Neapolitan (Db–F–Ab, "nap."),
    II7 with Ab (D–F–Ab–C), diminished VII7 (B–D–F–Ab).
  - (d) in **minor**: Neapolitan (Db–F–Ab) and a chord with a raised tone [? likely major IV F–A–C].
  (Vol. 1 ch. 11.)

**Rules — four Rubaiyat choruses, SATB (scan p.18–20, printed 23–25)**
- R1 [MUST] Complete the fragmentary melody first by **singing** it (full voice, with text) — never with piano help,
  never by calculating the missing tones. Fix its final form only after vocal completion. *Why:* only singing teaches
  the organic growth of melodic lines.
- R2 [MUST] Order of work: melody → bass → remaining voices.
- R3 [PREFER] Raise the songs to intense expression through: (a) rate of chord succession, (b) exaltation or
  restraint of melodies/harmonies, (c) calm vs agitated progression, by step or by leap.
- R4 [MUST] Match means to the text arc: a jubilant opening needs strong harmonic and rhythmic impulse; a gradual
  decline of optimism needs a parallel reduction of *all* compositional means.
- R5 [PREFER] Resigned expression: mainly stepwise motion plus repeated calm but expressive motives.
- R6 [PREFER] Heavy, striking expression: heavy harmonies mostly in whole-note/half-note values; a whole bar (and part of
  the next) may be harmonized entirely by the Neapolitan sixth to lead, with the melody, to a "piano" ending.
- R7 [MUST] Make anguished halts (fermata-like stops in the sketch) stand out both melodically and harmonically.
- R8 [NEVER] Modulate in exercises 3, 4 and 5 (no new tonics). *Why:* the target tonal style of each stage only exists
  if modulation is avoided.
- R9 [NEVER] Exceed the chord material of the stage. [NEVER] use cadences other than diatonic full cadences and
  half cadences.
- R10 [MUST] Because altered chords easily cause tonal disorder, keep the listener oriented by frequent return of the
  tonic and careful placement of the tonic and other main harmonies.

**Rules — theme and variations for string trio (vn, va, vc) (scan p.21–22, printed 26–27)**
- R11 [MUST] Theme and each variation contain three distinct thematic groups, clearly differentiated by type of
  voice-leading and harmonic conception; corresponding bar groups stay similar (here bars 1–2/7–8/13–14,
  3–4/9–10, 5–6/11–12).
- R12 [PREFER] In a variation, one group may be a "solo" (here viola) accompanied by the others; in the in-between bars
  bring out the motivic alteration clearly.
- R13 [AVOID] An obvious, annoying correspondence between the same group in different variations — each variation's
  treatment of a group must be its own.

**Rules — three pieces for clarinet (A), English horn, bassoon (scan p.23, printed 28)**
- R14 [AVOID] Setting a truly independent upper melody against a bass "melody" that merely arpeggiates the main
  functions (tonic, both dominants, leading-tone chord, Neapolitan). *Why:* it forces the ear to follow two equally
  important factors.
- R15 [PREFER] Instead write the upper voice mostly in halves and quarters, alive enough to feel melodic, but leaving
  rhythmic and melodic freedom to the active bass; the middle voice then stays in the shadow.
- R16 [PREFER] In a contrasting middle section after independent outer voices, switch to homophony: all three voices in
  (nearly) the same rhythm, or two similar voices paired against one calmer voice in half notes.
- R17 [MUST] Make formal structure audible; example plan for a 20-bar piece: 1–4 main material, 5–10 secondary part +
  transition, 10–13 main material, 14–17 coda, 18–20 final affirmation.

**Agent applicability**
- Generation order for vocal-style pieces: lead melody → bass → inner voices.
- When the bass is the active melodic line, the top line should be slower (halves/quarters) — one active line at a time.
- Contrast section = change texture type (polyphonic → homorhythmic) rather than only key.
- Without modulation, re-state the tonic chord at least every phrase when using borrowed/altered chords.
- Neapolitan (bII6) as a prolonged whole-bar harmony is a valid way to darken toward a soft ending.

## Cuarto Ejercicio — auxiliary (secondary) dominants (scan p.24–28, printed p.29–33)

**Concepts**
- Auxiliary dominant (dominante auxiliar) = secondary dominant. Any diatonic major or minor chord except the tonic
  (major: II, III, IV, V, VI; minor: IV, V, VI), and any major/minor chord produced by alteration, can be reinforced by
  a preceding chord (major, less often minor) or seventh chord (usually with dominant-7th interval structure) that
  stands in dominant relation to it.
- Auxiliary dominants contain tones foreign to the main key (they are alterations).

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] The key's V7 may itself be preceded by its own dominant (V/V).
- R2 [ALLOWED] The tonic may act as an auxiliary dominant and thus precede the subdominant (I(7) → IV).
- R3 [ALLOWED] Chords in VII, VII6, VII6/4, VII7 relation (plus inversions, altered or not) to the following chord
  produce nearly the same effect as auxiliary dominants (secondary leading-tone chords).
- R4 [MUST] Working method for harmonizing a given melody (viola + piano): (1) mark every place where an auxiliary
  dominant is possible; (2) determine under which melody tones the main tonal harmonies go; (3) compare and use only
  few members of series 1 to support series 2.
- R5 [AVOID] Many auxiliary dominants. *Why:* they gain supremacy and devalue the main harmonies; even few cause abrupt
  tonal jolts; rapid tonal oscillation without change of main direction is like a vehicle zigzagging instead of
  going straight or in well-drawn curves. Overused, they produce the tonal manner of 2nd/3rd-rate 19th-c. music and of
  sentimental entertainment music (essentially chains of secondary dominants).
- R6 [PREFER] Use auxiliary dominants where repetition of them reinforces the construction (aids comprehension); stop
  where they become mere ornamental flourish.
- R7 [PREFER] Differentiate formal sections by different piano textures; a return may be modified; mark exact
  dynamics. A da capo can be varied by giving the piano the melody of the middle part transposed to the tonic.
- R8 [PREFER] (Trombone scherzi) Place the auxiliary dominant → auxiliary tonic progressions mainly on the
  accompaniment's two-eighth figures (i.e. on short metric units, not on main structural points).
- Example (scan p.27): in Bb, a "B major" chord is really Cb = the Neapolitan — read enharmonics by function.
- R9 [MUST] Final judgment only on performance; if the instrument is unavailable, play the part on another.

**Agent applicability**
- Secondary-dominant budget: insert V/x (or vii°/x) only before chords that are structural targets; suggested cap
  (inferred): ≤ 1 secondary dominant per phrase-level target, never chained more than 2 in a row unless the style is
  deliberately sentimental/late-Romantic.
- Generation algorithm: first place primary chords under the melody, then test which can be preceded by V/x whose
  root-position upper voices fit the melody note; keep only a few.
- Put secondary dominants on weak beats/short durations of the accompaniment.

## Quinto Ejercicio — extended (chromatic) alteration (scan p.29–36, printed p.34–41)

**Concepts**
- Extended/chromatic alteration (alteración ampliada o cromática): any tone of a key may be chromatically altered and
  the new tones may be members of chords considered to belong to the key.
- Constraint: every such chord must reduce to a close position of stacked thirds (of any size), i.e. to one of the
  basic types: triads (4 types), dominant 7th, secondary 7ths, 9th chords. Applying the standard operations to these
  altered chords — inversion, replacing a chord tone by its neighbor tone, use as auxiliary dominant — yields the whole
  arsenal of late traditional harmony.
- Key claim: almost all chords so obtained are derivatives/variants of the dominant 7th chord (cf. vol. 1 ch. XIII).
- Table (scan p.29): altered chords on each chromatic degree of C major (augmented-sixth-type, altered-fifth 7ths,
  altered 9ths…; not reliably transcribable — [?] individual chords).
- Other constructible chords are only notational variants of known ones. Many chords lie outside this method; traditional
  harmony gives no satisfactory rule for them and they are excluded from the treatise.

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] Every altered chord must be explainable as stacked thirds reducible to a basic type (otherwise it is out
  of the traditional style).
- R2 [NEVER] Modulate yet (no new, definitely autonomous tonics) — miniature dances for piano (waltz in C, march in F,
  gigue in c#, polka in F).
- R3 [MUST] (Dances) Write a fluid bass first, then fill in the remaining chord tones.

**Harmonium pieces (scan p.32–36, printed 37–41)**
- Concept: on harmonium every tone sustains at constant, unaccentable volume, so harmonies are heard "naked": an
  unpleasant chord sounds unmodified until the next; a pleasant one stays pleasant. Other instruments change a chord's
  structure during its sounding (piano decay; voice-leading-driven balance), and timbre, dynamics and vibrato make
  harshness plausible and season bland chords — so a "simple" harmonic effect is really a complex mix of factors.
- R4 [MUST] On a non-accenting instrument, express rhythmic/melodic accent by **quantity**: an accented chord contains
  more tones than its unaccented neighbors. Consequently the composer (not the performer) is responsible for formal
  clarity. *Why:* volume accent is unavailable (expression knee is negligible).
- Claim: modern listeners depend so much on regular metric accent that they hardly grasp melodies without meter
  indications, and add harmonies unconsciously to any line; harmonium writing trains making meter and rhythm "servants"
  again.
- R5 [PREFER] Treat harmonium (like choral arranging) as a constant exercise in composition teaching.
- R6 [MUST] For each piece, keep the target affect in mind and adapt the whole technical disposition of the material to
  it (three pieces: deep melancholy; amusement/scherzando; sad resignation). No recipe is given; experiment or use models.
- R7 [AVOID] (as a default) Continuous use of altered chords with dominant character. *Why:* it always yields the
  post-"Tristan" expressive idiom; once felt as modern, it is a technical means of very limited effect. Practice it once
  by exaggeration to know what to avoid and to foresee the effect of such progressions.
- Texture note (sketch, scan p.35–36): pieces alternate "a 2 voces" and "a 3 o más voces" (number of voices as an
  expressive variable); Resignación has the melody in a "voz media" (inner voice) at q = 40.

**Agent applicability**
- Any chromatic chord the agent generates should be checked: can it be spelled as stacked 3rds (triad / 7th / 9th),
  possibly with one neighbor-tone substitution? If not, reject in "traditional" styles.
- For organ/pad/harmonium/sustained synth patches (no velocity dynamics): realize accents by adding chord tones on
  strong beats and thinning on weak beats (e.g. 5–6 notes on downbeats, 3 on offbeats).
- Use stacked dominant-function altered chords sparingly; a continuous chain = Wagnerian/late-Romantic cliché.

## Sexto Ejercicio — full material incl. modulation; the planning method (scan p.36–66, printed p.41–71)

Material: entire traditional harmony including modulation (vol. 1 ch. 14–15).

### Suite for string orchestra (scan p.36–51, printed 41–56): the seven planning factors

Hindemith describes in full detail the procedure for the first movement (Presto, C#, 2/2, half = 112, 77 bars) and
requires the same process for every later piece. Defends the method: technique made unconscious through practice cannot
harm inspiration; it only reduces sources of error.

**Concepts**
1. Form (Forma): five formal elements, each different in character, expression, formal function and technical treatment.
   Plan (bars): A 1–5 | D 5–16 | B 17–25 | E 25–33 | C 33–40 | F 41–47 | A 48–52 | B 52–68 | E 68–70 | A 71–77.
   - A = main theme; appears 3× (1–5, 48–52, 71–77); always keeps all its characteristic traits despite dynamic changes.
   - B = second theme, nearly as important; first a triple repetition of one phrase (17–25); second appearance (52–68)
     expanded to 16 half notes and presented twice.
   - C = climax, closely related to A; quoted 3 times, each time with a different tonal meaning.
   - D = fills the space between first and second theme with motives from A — familiarizes the listener with the main
     material while moving away from it.
   - E = transition link, no melodic character; its linear impulse comes from continuous repetition of one two-note motive.
   - F = least independent; a brief rest between two more significant groups.
2. Tonality (Tonalidad): the sum of harmonies the ear relates to a central harmony (tonic). For a self-contained tonal
   group, the tonic must dominate by cadences, favorable position, repeated reappearance, and support from related
   harmonies. Total tonality of a piece = tonal groups joined by modulations (via harmonic groups belonging to both keys,
   reducible to a single chord; occasionally an apparent lack of connection is allowed).
   Stable stretches alternate with "indeterminate" stretches (tonics change too fast to be related).
   Tonal plan: 1–5 C# | 6–14 oscillating E / G# | 15–16 indet. | 17–25 F | 25–32 indet. | 33–35 C | 36–37 A |
   38–40 F# | 41–47 indet. | 48–67 C# | 68–70 indet. | 71–75 F# (as IV of C#) | 76–77 C#.
3. Amplitude of tonal oscillation (amplitud de oscilación tonal): degree of tonal tension, within one consolidated group,
   between the tonic and each chord related to it. Small: I–V–I, I–VI–IV–I. Widest (with this material): auxiliary
   dominants before altered chords.
4. Harmonic gradient (declive armónico): tension difference between individual chords. Lowest tension = major triad;
   highest = deformations of V7 (V9 especially in minor, V13/7, chords of extended alteration). Independent of
   oscillation: a tonic chord with a complicated structure (in c: a tonic triad with chromatic added tones, e.g.
   Bb/B [?]) = small oscillation, high gradient; very simple triads remote from the key (in c: sharp-side major triads
   such as C#/D# [?]) = wide oscillation, low gradient. Plotted per bar as a zig-zag line above a baseline of tension-free chords.
5. Harmonic density (densidad armónica): duration of each harmony (harmonic rhythm), distinct from surface rhythm.
   Example: bars 1–2 have surface quarters/eighths but harmonies whole (c#), half (A), half (B).
6. Style (Estilo): position between homophonic (chordal) and contrapuntal (melodically independent) disposition.
7. Stratification (estratificación): vertical thickness of the texture = number of tones per harmony (incl. doublings)
   plus the rate of growth/decrease of sound mass from chord to chord. With real voices it ranges from 1–2 voices up to
   7–8+ tones (double stops). In string music it depends mainly on the number of autonomous voices; in piano music
   (broken chords) on the plain number of chord tones.

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] Before writing, plan form, tonal plan, oscillation, gradient, density, style and stratification for the
  whole movement (bar by bar).
- R2 [MUST] The main key is the one with the largest share of bars and that holds first and last place; support it with
  related keys. The second key (here F, bars 17–25) must also be well affirmed.
- R3 [ALLOWED] In indeterminate stretches no clear key is needed (tonics follow each other too fast to relate to a
  higher common tonal denominator). [AVOID] a piece made only of such material — like an architect building walls
  and beams out of a gelatinous mass.
- R3b [PREFER] Do not fight the main key's natural preeminence; reinforce it with related keys and make isolated
  main-tonic (C#) harmonies appear even inside the "indeterminate" stretches.
- R4 [MUST] A short late excursion to the subdominant key (F# at 71–75) must not sound autonomous: color it as IV of the

**Agent applicability (Sexto Ejercicio — directly implementable as a pre-generation plan)**
- Build a per-bar plan table before generating MIDI: `section`, `key|indet`, `oscillation(0–3)`, `gradient(0–3)`,
  `harmonic_rhythm(beats per chord)`, `style(homophonic..contrapuntal)`, `voices(1–5+)`.
- Tension scoring for `gradient`: 0 = major triad; 1 = minor/diminished triads and diatonic 7ths; 2 = V7;
  3 = V9 (esp. minor), V13/7, chromatically altered dominants (inferred numeric scale from the source's ranking).
- Assign tension by function: tonic & subdominant chords → gradient 0; dominant / leading-tone chords → may carry 2–3.
- Never let gradient jump from a run of 3s straight to 0 unless a deliberate shock is wanted.
- Themes: 1 chord per bar or per half bar; transitions/climaxes: 1 chord per beat; let the melody's shorter values be
  non-chord tones relative to the plan.
- Transitions: add a syncopated inner voice against half-note harmony; climax: lots of notes, but on chord tones.
- Use the "reduce the others" rule as a global constraint: in bars where one parameter peaks (e.g. tension), hold the
  others (density, voices, tonal oscillation) moderate.
- Final return of the main theme: thickest texture (5+ voices); section entries: thin (1–3 voices).
- For a late subdominant-key passage before the final cadence, inject tonic-key colour tones (tonic & dominant
  scale-degree notes) so it reads as IV, not a new key.
- Fugal writing: countersubject less melodic than the subject; 2-voice vocal lines mostly in 3rds/6ths/4ths/5ths/8ves;
  keep connecting bars between key areas tonally neutral; fix one harmonic-rhythm value per fugue.
- Accompaniment for an existing 2-voice texture: support, never dominate (lower velocity, no new melodic material in
  the top register).
- Remember transposition when writing MIDI for transposing-instrument libraries only if the library expects written pitch
  (most sample libraries expect sounding pitch — inferred).
