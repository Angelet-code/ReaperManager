# Piston, *Orchestration* — scans s193L–s202R (printed p.386–405)

Notation used in Piston's "Distribution" tables, kept below: lines are labelled A, B, C… (one per melodic line or
real part). A bracket marked `8` between two instrument groups means the lower group plays the same line an octave
below the upper group. "½ va." = half the violas (divisi). Double-bass (d-b.) in these tables is listed at its own
octave (it sounds an octave below written) (inferred from standard practice).

## Type IV, Part Writing — end (scan s193L–s193R, printed p.386–387)

**Concepts**
- Part-writing texture (textura de escritura a partes / voces reales): a texture of real parts (voces reales), each
  with its own voice leading, with or without doublings.
- Harmonic thickening (engrosamiento armónico): a single melodic line given parallel intervals or chords, so several
  staves sound as one line.

**Rules**
- R1 [ALLOWED] A part-writing texture may change its number of real parts within a phrase, e.g. from 3 parts in bar 1
  to 4 parts in bar 3 (Fig. 117 / Ex. 392).
- R2 [PREFER] Vary the distribution of each line among instruments from bar to bar to raise the expressive power of a
  phrase (Ex. 392: line A goes vn I + ½ va + bn I → fl I / vn I + cl I / bn I in three octaves → vn I alone).
- R3 [ALLOWED] Give a double appoggiatura to the violas while the bassoons already sound the notes of resolution. The
  simultaneous clash is a characteristically orchestral effect (Ex. 392, bar 2).
- R4 [ALLOWED] Pair oboe I with flute I (instead of flute II) to play the second part under flute I. Piston calls this a
  favourite procedure of Beethoven. `[era: Classical/early Romantic, Beethoven]`
- R5 [ALLOWED] A score that looks like 7 parts may be only 2 lines: each line thickened, the upper in parallel
  root-position triads, the lower in parallel 6/4 chords, the two moving in contrary motion (Ex. 393).
- R6 [PREFER] Use real-part writing (a) in the accompaniment element of Type II textures (melody + accompaniment),
  (b) sometimes in the primary element itself, and (c) in chord connections such as cadences, where voice leading of
  the parts must be heard.

**Examples**
- Ex. 392 (composer not shown in range; Beethoven inferred from R4), 3 flats, 5 bars. Line A: vn I with ½ va + bn I
  an octave below (bar 2) → fl I / vn I + cl I / bn I (bar 3) → vn I alone (bars 4–5). Line B mirrors it with vn II, ob I,
  cl II, bn II. Line C appears only in bar 3 (ob II / ½ va / ½ va in octaves). Line D: vc with d-b. an octave below
  throughout.
- Ex. 393 Holst, *The Planets* (Vivace, 6/8; "Mercury" inferred): oboes, A clarinets and 3 bassoons play staccato
  parallel triads, p. Strings (vn II, vc, then div. vn I and va with accents) play the lower line. Piccolos and flutes
  enter in bar 5 with a key change. It shows 2 thickened lines in contrary motion, not 7 parts.
- Ex. 383 and Ex. 384 (Schumann, Symphony no. 1) are cross-referenced as a primary element built in part writing and
  as an elaboration of 4-part writing (outside range).

**Agent applicability**
- When a generator emits parallel triads in woodwinds, treat the stack as ONE line for density and balance checks:
  count lines, not staves.
- For a cadence inside a thin texture, switch to 4 real parts with proper voice leading for the last 2 bars.

## Ch. 23 Types of Texture — Type V, Contrapuntal Texture (scan s194L–s197R, printed p.388–395)

**Concepts**
- Contrapuntal texture (textura contrapuntística): made entirely of melodic elements. Lines may be in imitative
  counterpoint or independent melodies. It may be fugal, or a combination of thematic melodies already heard singly.
- Line (línea): one melodic voice, which may be carried by several instruments in unison and/or octaves.

**Rules**
- R7 [ALLOWED] A 2-line texture that starts as if in canon and then continues in mostly consonant intervals without
  close imitation is very common in orchestral music. It is close to 2-voice part writing (Ex. 394).
- R8 [PREFER] Close a 2-line contrapuntal phrase with a cadence in 4-voice part writing (Ex. 394, bars 7–8).
- R9 [PREFER] For a strong contrapuntal effect, contrast the rhythmic patterns of the lines and allow harmonic
  dissonances between them (Ex. 395).
- R10 [ALLOWED] Halfway through a phrase, change a woodwind doubling from unison to octaves. This strengthens the top
  part and is brighter than a continued unison (Ex. 395, bar 3: oboes and clarinets move from unison to octaves).
- R11 [PREFER] Bring in horns where a line carried by bassoons gets weak. The horns need not finish the melody: they
  may repeat a held tone, e.g. the dominant, as extra notes (Ex. 395).
- R12 [ALLOWED] Bassoons need not double cellos and basses. Here they double the upper line instead (Ex. 395).
- R13 [PREFER] Watch the vertical (pitch) location of each line, which is set by its doublings, not only by its
  written register. A bass line doubled by flute and strengthened by violas + oboes + clarinets, with the
  double-basses placed fairly high, sounds close in pitch to the upper line (Ex. 395).
- R14 [ALLOWED] Add less-important melodic voices that enter during a crescendo mainly to add sonority. They keep
  the texture fully contrapuntal because they are melodic, not chordal (Ex. 396).
- R15 [ALLOWED] At a phrase climax, let the strings stop and finish the phrase on winds alone in diminuendo. The
  added wind voices then continue the lines the strings dropped (Ex. 396, bars 4–5).
- R16 [ALLOWED] A nearly full tutti can be distributed among just 4 melodic lines, each a unison/octave block of mixed
  winds and strings (Ex. 397).
- R17 [PREFER] When a line rises above an instrument group's practical top, hand those notes to an instrument that
  can play them, e.g. add piccolo for an F# too high for the violins (Ex. 397).
- R18 [ALLOWED] Make forced octave changes in bass doublings, and drop notes (e.g. one unplayable note in the
  contrabassoon) rather than break the line's playability (Ex. 397).
- R19 [ALLOWED] In a 5-part counterpoint, a dominant violin line may make the texture read as melody + 4-voice
  contrapuntal accompaniment. Imitation between the 4 subsidiary lines sharpens that reading (Ex. 398).
- R20 [PREFER] Drop a doubling instrument an octave for a few notes when a pitch sounds bad on it. Bartók's English horn
  takes 4 notes an octave lower to avoid its high Bb (octave and written/sounding status not stated [?]) (Ex. 398, bar 5).
- R21 [PREFER] When basses go deep (below the G string), move a doubling bassoon up an octave to keep the bass line
  clear (Ex. 398, bar 5).
- R22 [AVOID] A doubling note that also sounds in another line at the same moment gives that pitch too much weight.
  Omit it in the doubling (Ex. 398: bassoon skips a Db already present in line B).

**Examples**
- Ex. 394 Haydn, Symphony in D "Clock" (no. 101; *Glocken* in Piston), Menuetto, f: 2 lines. A = vn I + fl I–II + ob I,
  with vn II + ob II an octave below (ob II and cl I/II take over at bar 4). B = va + vc + bn I–II, with d-b. an octave
  below. The cadence (bars 7–8) is in 4 parts.
- Ex. 395 Mozart, Symphony in G minor K. 550, Menuetto, f: line A (the bass) in 4 octaves (fl; va + ob I–II + cl I–II;
  vc; d-b.). Line B in 3 octaves (vn I; vn II + hn I; bn I–II + hn II). Horns in G enter at bar 4 repeating D.
  `[era: natural horn in G, Classical]`
- Ex. 396 Wagner, Prelude to *Tristan und Isolde* (Langsam und schmachtend, p, cresc. to f, dim.): 3 main lines (A ob I
  + cl I over cl II + hn I; B vn II over va; C bass cl/hn II over d-b. + bn III) and 3 entering lines (D bn I–II; E
  hn III/IV; F Eng hn). Line A dominates; B and C are low. Strings drop out mid-bar 4; bar 5 is winds only.
- Ex. 397 Milhaud, Symphony no. 2 (quarter = 144, ff): 4-part fugal stretto, 2 parts in augmentation, tonal answer (4th
  answered by 5th). A ½ vn I + fl I–II over ½ vn I + ob I–II + trp I; B vn II + cl I over va + cl II + hn I–II; C ½ vc + trb
  I over ½ vc + bass cl + bn I–II + trb II; D tuba over d-b. + contrabn. Brass to the fore; bass deep and ponderous.
- Ex. 398 Bartók, *Concerto for Orchestra* (Andante, non troppo, f espr.): 5 lines, unison doublings: A vn I + vn II
  (sul G, marked IV, sempre f); B vc + bass cl; C hn I + II; D va + Eng hn; E d-b. + bn I–II.

**Agent applicability**
- Build contrapuntal tutti as a few lines (2–5), each assigned a block of instruments in unison/octaves, instead of
  one part per instrument. Keep a distribution table per section (line → instruments → octave).
- Before writing a doubling, check each instrument's range note by note. Re-octave or drop single notes that fall
  outside it or sound poor; do not abandon the doubling.
- Give each line its own rhythm profile (long vs. running notes) so lines stay separable.
- To make a mid-phrase lift, change a woodwind unison into octaves rather than adding new instruments.

## Ch. 24 Types of Texture — Type VI, Chords (scan s198L–s201R, printed p.396–403)

**Concepts**
- Isolated chord (acorde aislado): a chord little or not at all shaped by voice leading. It is judged by tone colour,
  vertical order of instruments, spacing, balance and equality of registers, always in relation to its dynamic and
  musical intent.
- Tutti chord (acorde de tutti): a chord for full orchestra. Common.
- Pure colour (color puro) vs. mixed timbre (timbre mixto): a chord without unison doublings in one timbre vs. a chord
  mixing instruments on the same notes.
- Overlapping (solapamiento): the lower note of one instrument pair is in unison with the upper note of a second pair
  (Fig. 118).
- Superposition (superposición): one pair placed wholly above the other, with no contact (Fig. 119).
- Interlocking (entrelazamiento / cruzamiento): the lower note of the upper pair lies below the upper note of the lower
  pair (Fig. 120).
- Enclosing (encierro / inclusión): both notes of one pair lie between the two notes of the other pair (Fig. 121).
- Spacing (disposición): open or close position, and whether gaps were left where chord tones could have been placed.
- Balance (equilibrio): relative tone-weight or carrying power of each note. The usual intent is a flat vertical plane
  of sound in which no tone protrudes toward the listener.

**Rules**
- R23 [MUST] Choose a chord's colour from both the instruments and how they are combined: woodwinds, brass or strings
  alone, or mixtures; pure colours or mixed timbres; low or high register; double-reed or muted emphasis.
- R24 [PREFER] Remember that the chord's interval structure shapes its colour, apart from the instruments.
- R25 [PREFER] Aim for an even vertical plane with no audible break where one tone colour meets another. Use one of the
  4 pair relationships (overlapping, superposition, interlocking, enclosing) in addition to unison and octave
  doubling.
- R26 [MUST] Judge balance mainly by (a) how many instruments play each note and (b) each instrument's dynamic power in
  the register it is in.
- R27 [MUST] For winds, always account for weak and strong registers. Players often correct imbalances in music they
  know well; do not rely on it.
- R28 [ALLOWED] Deliberately overweight one part of a chord's range to stress its location as low, medium or high.
- R29 [PREFER] The root-position major triad is the most resonant chord, because its tones match overtones of its
  lowest note.
- R30 [PREFER] Space chords like the harmonic series: widest intervals at the bottom, closer intervals higher up. Chords
  sound clearer that way.
- R31 [AVOID] A narrow interval low in a loud tutti: in Ex. 399 the E in trombone II (a low 3rd) is the least ideally
  placed note.
- R32 [PREFER] In a loud tutti with brass, put the woodwinds high, above the brass. The brass are much stronger, so the
  woodwinds mainly reinforce upper partials (Ex. 399).
- R33 [ALLOWED] Undoubled notes in a tutti are acceptable when they are chord tones reinforced acoustically by the
  fundamental (root/octaves below). A bassoon on an inner note benefits from the strong difference tone of octave C's
  below it (Ex. 399).
- R34 [PREFER] Tremolo strings placed high are very brilliant and can even out a loud tutti chord (Ex. 399).
- R35 [ALLOWED] Leave open spaces in soft chords. They let the overtones of the lower instruments sound and help the ear
  separate two colour layers (Ex. 400).
- R36 [AVOID] Open spaces in loud chords: there the overtones are too weak to compete with nearby instruments.
- R37 [PREFER] Double-basses in unison with the cellos' open C (C2, MIDI 36) give a tone rich in upper partials in the
  horn/clarinet register (Ex. 400).
- R38 [PREFER] In a medium-low loud chord, guard against the strong major-3rd overtone by doubling (even tripling) the
  upper roots (Ex. 401). (Piston says "upper C's" in a C chord.)
- R39 [MUST] In a crescendo on a held tutti chord, expect trombones and timpani to outgrow the other winds and most
  string sections. Horns can crescendo better than woodwinds, above all when the woodwinds are in their weakest
  registers. Either limit the crescendo to what the woodwinds can do, or accept losing balance for drama (Ex. 401).
- R40 [ALLOWED] A complete large-orchestra tutti at ppp gives a rich, sumptuous sound no smaller group can produce.
  ppp then means "as soft as possible", relative to the forces (Ex. 402).
- R41 [ALLOWED] In a chord spanning a very wide range, some instruments will necessarily sit outside their weak (soft)
  registers. Accept it (Ex. 402).
- R42 [PREFER] Voice dominant 9th chords along the harmonic series: root only in the lowest octave, all spaces filled
  with chord tones above the bass, and the 9th left undoubled in each of its upper appearances (Ex. 402).
- R43 [ALLOWED] A very soft high timpani roll on the root, with soft sticks, may sit above the bass in a ppp chord
  (Ex. 402).
- R44 [ALLOWED] Stopped horns with muted trumpets, trombones and tuba blend on fairly equal terms with woodwinds in a
  soft chord (Ex. 403).
- R45 [PREFER] In a polychord of two tonal components (tonic + dominant, or two triads), prefer more open than close
  intervals so the partials of both roots sound. Keep close dissonance to a single spot, e.g. one minor 2nd
  (1 semitone) between one instrument and a mixed-timbre unison (Ex. 403).
- R46 [PREFER] For a 2-layer polychord heard as 2 layers, place one triad low and the other high, and leave out a
  shared or bridging tone between the layers. In Ex. 404 there is no G between horns and trumpets (Ex. 404).
- R47 [PREFER] To make the upper layer of a polychord forceful and brilliant, mass the upper woodwinds in
  superposition above high trumpets. Give strings and lower winds less intense positions (Ex. 404).
- R48 [MUST] When brass dominate a ff/fff tutti chord, trumpets and trombones must play under the marked dynamic for
  balance (Ex. 404). In MIDI: lower brass velocity/CC1 relative to the marking (inferred).

**Examples** (Piston gives them in condensed score at sounding pitch)
- Fig. 118–121: each relationship shown with pairs 2 fl/2 ob, 2 trp/2 hn, 2 fl/2 cl/2 ob, 3 ob/3 fl/3 cl. Illustration
  (inferred, not transcribed): overlapping fl C6–A5 over ob A5–F5; superposition fl C6–A5 over ob F5–C5; interlocking
  fl C6–F5 over ob A5–C5; enclosing fl C6–C5 around ob A5–F5.
- Ex. 399 Weber, Overture to *Der Freischütz*, ff, strings tremolo: a brilliant C major tutti in root position.
  Superposition fl over ob and trp over hn; interlocking ob/cl; overlapping ob/trp and trb/bn. Woodwinds high above
  the brass; bassoons in the lowest octave. Undoubled: ob I, hn I, hn IV, trb II, bn II. 4 horns, 3 trombones, timp
  roll. `[era: early Romantic orchestra, natural horns/trumpets]`
- Ex. 400 Brahms, Symphony no. 3 (slow movement, closing chord), p: high violins (div.) and flutes give a luminous
  layer. The dark minor 3rd on the violas' C string (div., about E3–G3, MIDI 52–55 [?]) is doubled by close-spaced
  trombones. The G just above the horns is left out to open a gap. Vc and d-b. are in unison on C2 (MIDI 36).
- Ex. 401 Berlioz, *Roméo et Juliette*, p < ff > p, strings and 2 timpani tremolo: medium-low chord. Fl/ob/cl
  overlap; violins in interlocking double-stops; upper C's doubled or tripled. Only fl I, timp I and basses undoubled.
  No open spaces except the bottom octave.
- Ex. 402 Strauss, *Don Juan*, ppp dolce: full tutti on a dominant major 9th (F#–A#–C#–E–G#, inferred from the text's
  F# root and G# 9th) with 3 fl, 2 ob, Eng hn, 2 cl, 4 hn, 2 trp, 3 trb, tuba, 2 bn, contrabn, timp roll, strings div.
  and tremolo. The 2 high flutes are undoubled. From the C# in trb III and cellos upward, every space is filled. Harp
  arpeggio and glockenspiel tremolo omitted from the reduction.
- Ex. 403 Stravinsky, *Symphonies of Wind Instruments*, p: a soft wind chord combining C and G harmonies. 3 fl and
  2 ob with high clarinet brighten trumpet I. 4 stopped horns; 3 trp, 3 trb and tuba all muted (sord.); 2 bn,
  contrabn.
- Ex. 404 W. Schuman, Symphony no. 3, ff to (fff): C major triad low (low brass, low reeds, strings, timp) plus Eb major
  high (2 picc, 2 fl, Eb cl, 3 cl, 3 ob over 3 trp), 4 hn in between. Brass dominate.

**Agent applicability**
- For a sustained chord, first decide its dynamic and intent, then voice it: wide intervals at the bottom (octave/5th
  below about C3, MIDI 48), thirds and seconds only above (inferred threshold, consistent with R30).
- Check balance per pitch: count instruments on each note, and weight brass > horns > woodwinds at equal marked
  dynamics. Give undoubled woodwind notes in a brass chord to the top of the voicing, or double them.
- Soft chord: open gaps allowed, single colours, low brass muted or stopped to blend with winds.
  Loud chord: fill the gaps, double the upper roots, keep the 3rd thin in the low/middle.
- On a held-chord crescendo in MIDI, cap the brass and timpani CC1/velocity curve so the woodwinds stay audible, or
  accept brass dominance on purpose.
- For polychords, give each triad its own register band and leave a gap between the bands.
- Use the 4 pair relationships to decide how a woodwind pair sits against another pair. Default: interlocking or
  overlapping for blend, superposition for separate layers (inferred).

## Ch. 25 Types of Texture — Type VII, Complex Texture (start) (scan s202L–s202R, printed p.404–405)

**Concepts**
- Complex texture (textura compleja): a combination of two or more of texture types I–VI. It can be complex in
  varying degrees.
- Recap of subdivisible elements: in Type I the melodic line may be thickened by parallel intervals and finally fully
  harmonized. In Type II the accompaniment may be made of several separate details. In Type III several secondary
  melodies may approach a contrapuntal texture. Types IV and V can add more real parts.

**Rules**
- R49 [ALLOWED] Layer a complex texture as separate elements, each with its own texture type and its own instrument
  family: e.g. a chorale melody in part writing, a contrasting-rhythm secondary melody, a scale-based third line,
  and a pedal (Ex. 405).
- R50 [PREFER] Make the primary element a theme already established in the piece. Give secondary elements contrasting
  rhythm so they stay distinct (Ex. 405).

**Examples**
- Ex. 405 Hindemith, Symphony *Mathis der Maler* (Breit, ff): trumpet I plays a chorale-like melody as the top of
  brass part writing (hn, trp, trb I–II), supported by clarinets. Piccolo, flutes and oboes play a thematic secondary
  melody in contrasting rhythm with trills. The strings play a descending scale, with violins varying it in running
  8ths while violas and cellos move in quarters. A held pedal bass (bn, trb III + tuba, d-b., timp roll, cymbal) forms
  the fourth element.

**Agent applicability**
- For a climactic tutti, plan 3–4 elements before writing notes: brass chorale (melody + harmony), woodwind
  counter-melody in faster values, string scale/figuration line, and a sustained pedal bass. Give each element its
  own rhythmic level.
