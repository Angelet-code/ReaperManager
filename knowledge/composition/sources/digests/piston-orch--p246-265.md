## Ch. 13 The Trumpet: the classical (natural) trumpet (scan s123L–s125R, printed p.246–251)

**Concepts**
- trumpet (Fr. trompette, It. tromba, Ger. Trompete).
- Classical trumpet: a narrow, mostly cylindrical brass tube (conical only in the last ~1.5 ft before the
  bell), folded twice, cup mouthpiece, bell about 4 in. wide. It had no valves and played only the
  harmonic series of its tube length.
- crook (tono, Bogen): an extra length of tube inserted to change the key. The C trumpet was an 8-ft tube.
  Its written series is the same as the horn in C alto and an octave lower than the modern 4-ft C trumpet.
- Around 1800 the F trumpet (about 6 ft, with a tuning slide) became the standard. Crooks put it in E, Eb, D
  or C, and combined crooks gave B, Bb, A or Ab.
- clarino playing: the early-18th-c. specialist art of playing very high partials, up to the 21st (a P4
  above the 16th). It needed long specialisation and a shallow mouthpiece, and the lower pitch of the time
  helped. The art died out after about 1750, when the linear style gave way to the symphonic style.

**Rules**
- R1 [MUST] [era: natural trumpet, Classical] Transposition by crook (Fig. 74; written C5 = 8th harmonic):
  in F it sounds +5 semitones, E +4, Eb +3, D +2, C 0 (as written), B −1, Bb −2, A −3. Shorter crooks sound
  higher than written and longer crooks lower.
- R2 [PREFER] [era: Classical] Crooks higher than F were rarely called for. In keys such as G major,
  composers used C trumpets or left the trumpets out. Beethoven 7 (A major) uses D trumpets rather than the
  heavily crooked A trumpets.
- R3 [MUST] [era: natural trumpet, c.1750–1850] Usable notes are harmonics 3 to 12 only (Fig. 75). Written
  pitches: G3, C4, E4, G4, Bb4, C5, D5, E5, F#5, G5 (written MIDI 55, 60, 64, 67, 70, 72, 74, 76, 78, 79).
  Concert pitch = written + the crook offset in R1.
  - C trumpets, concert: 55, 60, 64, 67, (70), 72, 74, 76, (78), 79.
  - D trumpets, concert: 57, 62, 66, 69, (72), 74, 76, 78, (80), 81.
  - Values in brackets are the doubtful 7th and 11th harmonics (R5, R6).
- R4 [NEVER] [era: Classical] Do not go above the 12th partial (written G5). Higher partials existed on the
  low crooks, but Classical composers almost never wrote them. The pedal (1st partial) was playable only on
  the F trumpet, the 2nd partial was unreliable on most crooks, and the 3rd was poor on the low crooks.
- R5 [AVOID] [era: natural trumpet] The 7th harmonic (written Bb4) is too flat. It was still written now
  and then: Ex. 266 repeats it.
- R6 [AVOID] [era: natural trumpet] The 11th harmonic (written F#5) lies between F and F# and needs lip
  correction. Trumpets cannot hand-stop the way horns do.
- R7 [ALLOWED] [era: Classical] Bass clef in a trumpet part followed the old horn convention: the notes
  are written an octave too low. A low note in bass clef is the 2nd harmonic, not a pedal (Ex. 267).
- R8 [PREFER] [era: Classical] Signature use: the tutti flourish of trumpets and timpani, usually with the
  horns doubling (Ex. 268).
- R9 [PREFER] [era: Classical] For loud tutti chords and accents, give both trumpets whatever chord tone
  is available, even if both sit on one unison note (link R11). Composers accepted a badly balanced chord
  rather than lose the weight of the trumpet tone. In D minor, C trumpets can only play D, so both double D
  and the voice leading becomes unorthodox (Ex. 269).
- R10 [PREFER] [era: Classical] Typical trumpet material:
  - long held notes in octaves;
  - rhythmic punctuation;
  - one- or two-note patterns;
  - occasional short solos.
  Themes were often built on the harmonic series so that the trumpets could join part of the melody in the
  tutti (Ex. 270).
- R11 [ALLOWED] [era: Classical] Trumpets are not only for loud music. A soft entry of trumpets with
  timpani (pp) is a delicate colour (Ex. 271).
- R12 [PREFER] (inferred) The long Classical trumpet was probably less piercing than the modern one, and
  the unison doublings in R9 were probably played below full ff. When you imitate these doublings with
  modern samples, reduce the level.

**Examples**
- Ex. 266. Beethoven, Symphony 9, i (trumpets in D, a2): repeated written Bb4, the flat 7th harmonic.
- Ex. 267. Mozart, *Don Giovanni* Overture (trumpets in D): the 2nd trumpet is notated low in bass clef.
  Read with the horn convention, it is the 2nd harmonic (concert D3, unreliable on the D crook) and not a
  pedal.
- Ex. 268. Mozart, Symphony 41 "Jupiter", finale: horns and trumpets in C play the same repeated triadic
  C–E–G rhythm, and the timpani alternate C and G. This is the tutti flourish.
- Ex. 269. Beethoven, Symphony 6, v: over the chords F, Dm, G, the two C trumpets both land on D for the
  D-minor chord.
- Ex. 270. Beethoven, Symphony 5, ii: trumpets in C, ff, play a theme built on C–E–G with sf accents.
- Ex. 271. Beethoven, Symphony 1, ii: 2 trumpets in C hold a pp octave G (written G3/G4) under a dotted
  pp timpani rhythm, while flutes and strings carry the figuration.

**Agent applicability**
- For a period (Haydn–Beethoven) pastiche, choose a crook that matches the key: C for C major, D for D
  major, Eb for Eb major, and so on. Restrict trumpet MIDI to that crook's concert list (R3 and R1). Skip
  the 7th and 11th harmonics, and use nothing above harmonic 12 or below harmonic 3.
- In minor or remote chords, keep the trumpets on whatever chord tone they have, doubled if needed, or
  rest them. Do not give them non-harmonic notes.
- Typical period part: tonic–dominant fanfare rhythms doubled by the horns and locked to the timpani;
  sustained octaves on the tonic or the dominant.

## Ch. 13: the valve trumpet in F (scan s125R–s126R, printed p.251–253)

**Concepts**
- valve trumpet (trompeta de pistones): introduced mid-19th c. It was the same 6-ft F trumpet, and its
  three valves extend the tube to about 8.5 ft.

**Rules**
- R13 [MUST] [era: valve F trumpet, c.1850–1900] The written chromatic range runs from the 3rd to the
  12th partial: written C#3–G5 (Fig. 76), sounding a P4 higher, concert F#3–C6 (MIDI 54–84).
- R14 [AVOID] [era: valve F trumpet] Its lowest tones were probably of useless quality. Low notes written
  for the F trumpet do not prove they were playable, because the part may have been played on a different
  instrument.
- R15 [PREFER] [era: late 19th c.] Strauss's advice in his revision of Berlioz: write trumpet parts in
  whichever key makes the written part look as close to C major as possible. The player transposes onto
  the instrument of his choice. Since about the 1850s, a part marked "trumpet in F" may never have been
  played on an F trumpet, and the composer may not have expected it to be.
- R16 [ALLOWED] [era: late 19th c.] After valves arrived, composers kept writing trumpet parts "in" E, Eb,
  D, C and Bb. These parts were transposed by the players.
- Contemporary reports call the F trumpet tone noble, heavy, dominating and superior to the modern
  trumpet. Piston doubts that all these reports come from actually hearing the instrument.

**Examples**
- Ex. 272. Bruckner, Symphony 7, i: 3 trumpets in F, ff, with dense marcato accents in chromatic valve
  writing and some low notes. Piston invites the reader to imagine it on the original F trumpets.

**Agent applicability**
- Romantic brass (Wagner, Bruckner): write chromatically at concert pitch. Concert F#3–C6 is the period
  ceiling and floor, so keep the lowest fifth for weight only, not melody.

## Ch. 13: the modern trumpet (scan s126R–s130R, printed p.253–261)

**Concepts**
- In the 20th c. the standard trumpet is the C or the Bb, at half the tube length of the Classical trumpet
  in the same key (C = 4 ft; Bb = 4 ft 6.5 in). The cause was the trend toward the high register, brilliance
  and agility.
- A trumpet: a Bb trumpet with a pull-out slide at the first bend and adjusted valve slides.
- Valves: piston valves are the universal standard on the trumpet, unlike the horn's rotary valves. Rotary
  trumpets exist.
- The written harmonic series moves up an octave (Fig. 78): 1 = C3 (in brackets), then C4, G4, C5, E5,
  G5, Bb5, C6 and D6 for partials 2–9 (written MIDI 48, 60, 67, 72, 76, 79, 82, 84, 86).
- Fingering: there are 7 valve positions (0, 2, 1, 1+2 or 3, 2+3, 1+3, 1+2+3). Each lowers the open series
  by 0 to 6 semitones (Fig. 79).
- mute (sordina; Fr. sourdine; Ger. Dämpfer, abbreviated "Dpf"; mit/ohne Dpf. means with/without mute).

**Rules: transposition and range**
- R17 [MUST] The C trumpet sounds as written. The Bb trumpet sounds a M2 (2 semitones) lower than
  written.
- R18 [NEVER] Do not use the fundamental (written C3). Its quality is poor and it is not part of the range.
- R19 [MUST] The normal bottom is written F#3:
  - C trumpet: concert F#3 (MIDI 54);
  - Bb trumpet: concert E3 (MIDI 52).
  Lower notes are only possible by drastic means, pulling the valve slides and the tuning slide all the
  way out (Ex. 273 goes to about written F3).
- R20 [MUST] The normal top is written C6:
  - C trumpet: concert 84;
  - Bb trumpet: concert Bb5 (82).
  Written D6 (the 9th harmonic) is extreme (R26). Partials above the 8th come more easily on the Bb than
  on the C trumpet.
- R21 [AVOID] Avoid the flat 7th harmonic as an open note (written Bb5). It is fingered with valves
  instead.
- R22 [PREFER] Comfortable melodic range: written C4 to the G a twelfth above (G5).
  - C trumpet: concert MIDI 60–79.
  - Bb trumpet: concert Bb3–F5 (58–77).
  The high and low points of a melodic curve may go past this on purpose.

**Rules: intonation and fingering**
- R23 [AVOID] The third valve and valve combinations have intonation problems:
  - The Ab series (valves 2+3) can be corrected with the lips.
  - The 1+2+3 series cannot. Written low F#3 and G3, and their fifths C#4 and D4, can only be fingered
    1+2+3 or 1+3 and are bound to be out of tune.
  - Players fix this with a thumb trigger on valve 1 and a ring on valve 3, which can be moved while the
    note sounds.
  - High written Ab5 also needs care.
  For MIDI with sample libraries this does not matter, but in writing for real players do not make these
  notes long, exposed and soft.

**Rules: tone by register**
- R24 [PREFER] Tone:
  - Bb trumpet: keeps more of the fullness of the older trumpets.
  - C trumpet: more intensity and also more delicacy. Its use grew between about 1930 and 1955.
  - Players often swap Bb and C parts between the two instruments.
  - Bore (small, medium or large) and mouthpiece also shape the tone; these are the player's choice.
- R25 [PREFER] The trumpet carries over the full orchestra with authority.
  - Low register: full and solemn (Ex. 274).
  - High register, written G5–C6: the most penetrating. It sounds beautiful at piano but is hard to play
    softly.
  - Avoid long, exposed pp passages above written G5 (inferred).

**Rules: extreme high notes**
- R26 [AVOID] Avoid written high D6 on the C trumpet. It is common for players who take C parts on the Bb
  trumpet, because there it is their written C6. A part that keeps hitting the top (Ex. 277) would
  probably be played on the D trumpet.
- R27 [AVOID] Do not lean on the extreme top in orchestral writing.
  - Dance-band trumpeters often reach written G6 (the 12th harmonic) and beyond, but they lose quality,
    especially in the middle and low registers.
  - The orchestral trumpeter is expected to be at his best in the middle range, as opposed to the
    18th-c. clarino specialist.

**Rules: mutes**
- R28 [ALLOWED] Muted trumpet has two opposite uses:
  - forte to fff, the most piercing sound in the orchestra (Ex. 278);
  - soft, as an echo or a sound from far away (Ex. 279).
- R29 [era: early 20th c.] At one time early in the 20th c. muted trumpets were so fashionable that they
  were heard almost as often as open ones.

**Rules: tonguing and trills**
- R30 [PREFER] Separately tongued notes on the trumpet are more incisive than on the horn, and especially
  forceful when accented (Ex. 280). The trumpet responds faster than the horn to every kind of tonguing.
  Its staccato is very crisp and pointed, and its double and triple tonguing are lighter and neater than
  on any other wind instrument.
- R31 [ALLOWED] Fast repeated-note figures with double or triple tonguing work even at pp with mute
  (Ex. 281).
- R32 [PREFER] Write trills as valve trills; most are very good. Only a few lip trills are possible, all in
  the high register.
- R33 [AVOID] Avoid trills whose two notes need awkward valve changes, where several valves move at once.
  Fig. 80 gives these bad cases (written pitches):
  - Bb3–Ab3 (valve 1 ↔ 2+3);
  - C4–Db4 (0 ↔ 1+2+3);
  - B3–C#4 [?] (2 ↔ 1+2+3).
- R34 [ALLOWED] Fast running figures are quite playable, but they are not the best trumpet style. One
  dazzling use is fast trumpet runs in a loud tutti that thins out at once to pizz. strings and timpani
  marking the beats (Ex. 282).
- R35 [PREFER] Fanfare and "pronouncement" material belongs naturally to the trumpet (Ex. 283).

**Rules: doublings and sections**
- R36 [AVOID] Avoid unisons of the open trumpet with anything other than brass. The trumpet is too
  assertive and covers the other instrument.
- R37 [ALLOWED] Exception to R36: a muted trumpet blends especially well in unison with the English horn
  (Ex. 284).
- R38 [PREFER] With 3 or 4 trumpets, parallel chords (planing triads in close position) are a natural and
  widely used effect (Ex. 285, also Ex. 282).

**Examples**
- Ex. 273. Ravel, *Rapsodie espagnole*: muted solo C trumpet, p. Low repeated notes with accents and
  hairpins around written G3, dipping to about F3/F#3 [?]. The muted low register sits at the bottom limit.
- Ex. 274. Shostakovich, Symphony 5, i: 2 Bb trumpets a2, f. A low-register melody from about written C#4
  down to G#3, then mp. Shows the full, solemn low register.
- Ex. 275. Hindemith, *Concerto for Orchestra*: 2 C trumpets in linear counterpoint. Trumpet I has a
  chromatic, staccato-detailed line in the upper middle; trumpet II has sparse low punctuations.
- Ex. 276. Stravinsky, *Symphony in C*: 2 C trumpets "marc. in p". A diatonic G-major line climbing to the
  high register (about written C6), then imitated by trumpet II. Shows a high line held at piano.
- Ex. 277. Bartók, *Concerto for Orchestra*, finale (Presto): C trumpet, f. A fast flat-side line that
  keeps returning to the top of the range (written high Db6 or D6 [?]). This kind of part is likely taken
  on D trumpet.
- Ex. 278. Stravinsky, *Petrushka*: 2 Bb trumpets a2, con sord., fff, Furioso. Piercing muted triplet
  figures.
- Ex. 279. Berg, Violin Concerto: trumpet II open, mp "pastorale", then trumpet I muted, p, answers the
  same figure. Shows the distant echo effect.
- Ex. 280. Stravinsky, *Firebird* (Infernal dance, Allegro feroce): Bb trumpet, mf, every eighth note
  accented. Shows incisive separate tonguing.
- Ex. 281. Debussy, *La Mer* (movement [?]): muted F trumpet, pp. Repeated tongued triplet and eighth figures with
  small hairpins in the low-middle register.
- Ex. 282. Ravel, *Daphnis et Chloé*: 4 C trumpets, ff. Repeated eighth-note chords, then fast chromatic
  chord streams in parallel motion falling to pp.
- Ex. 283. Copland, *Music for the Theater*: 2 C trumpets, f, marked "sharp, fast, clear, nervous". Fanfare
  pickups in 32nd notes, accented double-tongued figures, a sffz overlap between trumpets I and II.
- Ex. 284. Debussy, *La Mer*, i: muted F trumpet, p expressif, in unison with the English horn.
- Ex. 285. Casella, *Partita* for piano and orchestra: 3 C trumpets, f non troppo, in parallel triads. The
  accompaniment is pizz. chords on each eighth, timpani on each quarter, and arpeggios in 2 clarinets plus
  bass clarinet.

**Agent applicability**
- Hard limits for a modern Bb trumpet track: concert MIDI 52–82, with 84 as a rare extreme.
- Melodies for a Bb trumpet sit at concert 58–77, peaking at about 79–82 only for climaxes.
- Above concert ~77 (Bb), give the trumpet loud dynamics (high CC1/velocity). Do not write long soft held
  notes there.
- For low, solemn melodies use concert 52–62 at f or mf. The register sounds full, not weak.
- Do not double an open trumpet melody in unison with woodwinds or strings. Double it with brass, or with
  woodwinds or strings at the octave. A muted trumpet plus English horn in unison is a good blend.
- For muted trumpet, choose the mute articulation or patch. Use it at fff for a snarling, piercing line, or
  at p for echoes or answers to an open trumpet.
- Separate tonguing and accents are idiomatic. Fast repeated notes (double/triple tonguing) are fine; use
  short, crisp note lengths of about 50–70% of the grid (inferred).
- With 3 trumpets, close-position parallel triads with the melody on top is an idiomatic section texture.

## Ch. 13: auxiliary trumpets (scan s131L–s132L, printed p.262–264)

**Concepts**
- small (high) trumpets in D and Eb (trompeta piccolo/aguda en Re, Mib). The modern D trumpet is 3 ft 5 in,
  not to be confused with the older ~7.5-ft D trumpet. D and Eb exist as separate instruments, or as one
  instrument with a device to switch between D and Eb.
- bass trumpet (trompeta baja).
- tromba contralta: Rimsky-Korsakov's alto trumpet, in modern Russian scores.
- "Bach trumpets" in high F and G: Piston calls the name a misnomer. They do not play the original 8th–16th
  partials. Being half the length of the old trumpets, their 4th–8th partials sound just as high, and the
  valves fill the gaps.
- Bb sopranino trumpet: the highest trumpet (main tube 2 ft 3.25 in), used in some military bands.

**Rules**
- R39 [MUST] The small D and Eb trumpets have the same technique and the same written range as the C
  trumpet.
  - D trumpet: sounds a M2 (+2) higher than written; concert about G#3–D6 (56–86) (inferred from R19 and
    R20).
  - Eb trumpet: sounds a m3 (+3) higher than written; concert about A3–Eb6 (57–87) (inferred).
- R40 [AVOID] The small trumpets tend to sound shrill, especially in the high register, which is where
  they are mostly used.
- R41 [PREFER] The small trumpets and the high F/G trumpets are the modern way to perform Bach and Handel
  clarino parts. [era: Baroque clarino]
- R42 [MUST] Bass trumpet in 8-ft C or in the Bb below (as Wagner and Strauss wrote it): it is effectively
  a valve trombone. Trombonists play it with a trombone mouthpiece.
- R43 [MUST] Bass trumpet in Eb:
  - It keeps a trumpet quality, with a large trumpet mouthpiece.
  - It sounds a M6 (9 semitones) lower than written.
  - With its 4th valve (changing it to D), the lowest written note is F3, sounding Ab2 (MIDI 44).
  - Without the 4th valve the bottom would be written F#3, sounding A2 (45) (inferred).
- R44 [PREFER] Use the Eb bass trumpet:
  - in the register where the ordinary trumpet is weak;
  - for low trumpet melodies when you want a more resonant tone;
  - for parts written for older, longer trumpets that go below the modern trumpet's range (Ex. 289).
- R45 [era: Rimsky-Korsakov, late 19th c.] The tromba contralta has the same fundamental as the 19th-c.
  F valve trumpet, but its bore and mouthpiece give it partials 2–9 instead of 3–12. With F trumpet
  partials this is about concert F3–G5 (53–79) (inferred, not stated).
- R46 [era: 18th c.] When comparing old and new instruments, remember that 18th-c. pitch varied and was
  generally about a semitone below 20th-c. pitch.

**Examples**
- Ex. 286. Britten, *Four Sea Interludes* (*Peter Grimes*): D trumpet, ff "brillante". Fast 16th-note
  scale runs up to the top of the range and back down.
- Ex. 287. d'Indy, Symphony in Bb (*Symphonie no. 2*): Eb trumpet, Lent, mf. A slow chromatic line rising
  from about written B3 to an ff/sffz climax around written A5–B5 (concert about D6).
- Ex. 288. Stravinsky, *The Rite of Spring*: Eb bass trumpet, con sord., mf, Lento. A slurred low figure
  around written F3–Bb3, sounding about Ab2–Db3 [?].
- Ex. 289. Strauss, *Ein Heldenleben*: 2 Eb trumpets a2, mässig langsam. A rising natural-harmonic
  arpeggio from written C3 (2nd partial: G3, C4, E4, G4, C5), p → ff → p molto dim. It reaches below the
  modern trumpet's range, so Piston suggests the bass trumpet for parts like it.

**Agent applicability**
- High D/Eb trumpet ("piccolo"-style brilliance): bright fanfares and runs at concert 74–86. Expect a
  shrill tone, so keep the dynamics in check or double with the C/Bb trumpets an octave lower.
- When a trumpet line must go below concert E3 (52), give it to a bass trumpet: concert floor Ab2 (44) on
  the Eb bass trumpet with 4th valve. Otherwise give it to the trombone.
- For a Wagner or Strauss bass trumpet in C or Bb, use trombone-like phrasing and a trombone sample if no
  bass-trumpet patch exists.

## Ch. 13: the cornet (scan s132L–s132R, printed p.264–265; continues)

**Concepts**
- cornet (corneta; Fr. piston, cornet à pistons; It. cornetta; Ger. Kornett): it originated in France
  around 1825. It descends from the post horn, not the trumpet, so it is closer kin to the horn. The
  standard modern cornet is in Bb, with a slide change to A. It has the same tube length and range as the
  Bb trumpet, a bore two-thirds conical and one-third cylindrical, and a cup mouthpiece.

**Rules**
- R47 [MUST] Cornet in Bb: range and transposition are the same as the Bb trumpet. Concert E3–Bb5
  (52–82), sounding a M2 lower than written (inferred from "same range as the Bb trumpet"). Cornet in A
  sounds a m3 lower than written.
- R48 [PREFER] The cornet tone lies between trumpet and horn. It lacks the trumpet's heroic quality but has
  a warm, mellow smoothness otherwise associated with the horn. Piston notes it is rarely played well.
- R49 [PREFER] The cornet is the most agile of the brass: give it fluent, virtuoso passages.
- R50 [ALLOWED] [era: 19th-c. French and band/theatre orchestras] Cornets were brought into the symphony
  orchestra to supplement the trumpets and fill out the brass harmony. French scores kept doing this well
  after the valve trumpet was established. Cornets combine more sympathetically with other instruments than
  trumpets do, and suit lyrical, popular-style melodies (Ex. 290).
- R51 [AVOID] [era: mid-20th c.] Cornets are not regular members of the symphony orchestra. Use them only
  for special occasions, partly because modern cornets and trumpets have grown so alike that the difference
  is barely audible.

**Examples**
- Ex. 290. Tchaikovsky, *Capriccio italien*: 2 cornets in A, mf. A folk-like tune harmonised in parallel
  thirds and sixths, then f/dim. with triplets. This is the "sympathetic" melodic cornet style.
- The text announces a further "typical cornet style" example, which falls past p.265 (next range).

**Agent applicability**
- For a 19th-c. French, band or Italian-festive colour, use cornets (or a softer trumpet patch at moderate
  CC1) for tunes in parallel thirds or sixths. Use trumpets for heroic or fanfare material.
