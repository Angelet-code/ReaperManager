## Ch. 16 Percussion: snare drum, strokes and notation (scan s153L–s153R, printed p.306–307)

**Concepts**
- roll (redoble): alternating right/left double strokes. Rolls are named by stroke count including the final
  single stroke: Fig. 96 (previous page) is a 17-stroke roll. Short rolls: 3-stroke (written as two grace
  notes; played l-l-r) and 5-stroke (played r-r-l-l-r).
- flam (flam): a principal note preceded by one grace note, played almost together, with the accent on the
  principal note (closed flam). If the grace note falls on the beat it is an open flam, which is rarely used.
- drag / ruff (arrastre / ruff): heavier than the flam. Two grace notes before the beat (double stroke) or
  more grace notes played as single strokes.
- paradiddle (paradiddle): not a stroke but a sticking pattern of single strokes (r l r r l r l l) that
  makes accents fall alternately on right and left hands.
- rim shot (golpe de aro): left stick tip on the head, middle resting on the rim, struck sharply by the right
  stick. Sounds like a pistol shot.
- on the rim: playing on the rim itself. Effective on a wooden drum; a metal snare-drum rim has little tone.
- coperto (It.): snare head muffled with a cloth. Wire brushes give a rustling, shuffling sound.

**Rules**
- R1 [PREFER] Write the drum part exactly as you want it rhythmically. Drummers add flams and drags even
  when not written, so be explicit (and write "no flams" style intentions in words if needed (inferred)).
- R2 [PREFER] Use the snare drum to sharpen the rhythmic outline and accents of a tutti.
- R3 [PREFER] Use a snare roll to support a crescendo. *Why:* it is a strong crescendo driver.
- R4 [AVOID] Long sustained snare rolls. *Why:* the effect goes dull quite soon.
- R5 [ALLOWED] Soft snare drum. Piston calls it a common mistake to think drums only suit loud passages;
  the snare is outstanding for crisp rhythmic patterns at soft dynamics.
- R6 [ALLOWED] All snare-drum strokes (roll, flam, drag) apply to other side drums and to any percussion
  played with snare sticks.

**Examples**
- Ex. 336. Prokofiev, *Symphony no. 5*: snare (tambour militaire), Allegro marcato 3/4, mp then mf. A short
  repeated rhythmic cell of sixteenths with flams, separated by rests: a soft, crisp ostinato figure.
- Ex. 337. Ravel, *Rapsodie espagnole*: snare, Assez animé 6/8. Continuous even notes at pp, then a sudden
  ff with tremolo (roll) figures: soft-texture snare and contrast with a loud roll.

**Agent applicability**
- MIDI flam: grace note ~15–30 ms before the main hit at lower velocity (inferred). Drag: 2 grace notes.
  Short rolls: 3- or 5-stroke as fast repeated hits ending on an accented single stroke.
- For crescendos into a tutti, use a snare roll (or roll articulation) with rising velocity/CC; keep it
  shorter than a few bars.
- Do not reserve snare for ff: a pp–mp snare ostinato is idiomatic (Prokofiev 5, Ravel).

## Ch. 16 The bass drum (scan s154L–s154R, printed p.308–309)

**Concepts**
- bass drum (gran cassa; Fr. grosse caisse, It. cassa / gran cassa, Ger. grosse Trommel): 24–36 in.
  (up to 40 in. for band); the orchestral size is ~30 in. head, ~16 in. shell. No snares; two tuned heads;
  played upright, usually with the right hand, on either head.
- bass drumstick (mazza; Fr. mailloche, Ger. Klöppel): wooden handle, large soft felt knob, glancing stroke.
- secco: short note damped by the left hand and by keeping the stick against the head.
- Ruthe (Ger.): bundle of birch rods used to beat the drum. Snare sticks and wire brushes are other effects.

**Rules**
- R7 [MUST] Treat the bass drum as the most powerful instrument for carrying power. Its soft notes are felt
  more than heard (like deep organ pipes).
- R8 [ALLOWED] Use it under a chord: although unpitched, it often sounds like the fundamental of the harmony
  the higher instruments play.
- R9 [MUST] Write note lengths as wanted, because the player damps the drum. Mark short notes secco.
- R10 [AVOID] Fast repeated notes and complex rhythms on the bass drum. *Why:* it speaks with a slight lag
  (the player anticipates the beat), so busy figures blur.
- R11 [ALLOWED] Roll (written as trill or tremolo): with a double-headed stick, two sticks on the two heads,
  or two timpani sticks on one head. The timpani-stick roll sounds like an extremely deep kettledrum roll.
- R12 [PREFER] Use the bass drum sparingly. Its effect is inversely proportional to how often it appears;
  bass drum parts have few notes.
- R13 [ALLOWED] Muffling: slacken the heads (a thumbscrew damper also exists). Notation: bass clef staff or
  single line.

**Agent applicability**
- Bass-drum MIDI: few notes, on structural downbeats and climaxes. Place hits a few ms early if the
  sample has a slow attack (inferred from the speaking lag). Avoid fills or fast patterns.
- Use a soft bass-drum roll (pp) as a sub-bass "floor" under a quiet chord; land a single hit with the
  harmonic root on big cadences.

## Ch. 16 The cymbals (scan s154R–s155L, printed p.309–310)

**Concepts**
- cymbals (platillos; Fr. cymbales, It. piatti / cinelli, Ger. Becken): brass-alloy matched pairs, Turkish
  by tradition. Orchestral player usually has two pairs: 15–16 in. and 18 in.
- Swish and sizzle cymbals (13–14 in. Chinese, from dance bands) are not standard in the symphony
  orchestra `[era: 1955 US]`.
- two-plate stroke (choque): the normal stroke, a swinging, brushing clash. Held in the air after a loud
  clash, it rings a very long time.
- let ring (dejar vibrar; Fr. laissez vibrer, It. lasciare vibrare, Ger. klingen lassen): shown by a tie to
  nothing, the words, or a dotted line for the wanted duration.
- staccato two-plate stroke: clash then immediate damping against the chest.
- two-plate roll: repeated clashing, marked "2 cym." with trill/tremolo. Piston calls it a rather crude
  effect, distinct from the single-cymbal roll.
- suspended cymbal (platillo suspendido): one cymbal on a bracket, struck with snare stick, hard or soft
  timpani stick, metal triangle beater, fingers, wire brushes or even a penknife blade.
- choke: dance-band effect; the left hand grabs the cymbal to stop it with each stroke.

**Rules**
- R14 [MUST] Mark the duration of cymbal sound (let ring, a tie, or staccato/damped).
- R15 [ALLOWED] pp cymbals: plates barely touched or brushed, or simply pulled apart.
- R16 [PREFER] Suspended-cymbal roll with two soft timpani sticks on opposite edges. Range from a whisper to
  fff; a brilliant way to add excitement to an orchestral crescendo.
- R17 [AVOID] Overusing the cymbal roll. Piston calls it easily overused.
- R18 [ALLOWED] Notation: single line or bass-clef staff.

**Agent applicability**
- Crescendo into a climax: suspended-cymbal roll (soft mallets) from pp, rising CC/velocity, ending on the
  downbeat with a two-plate crash let ring. Use it once or twice per piece, not every section.
- Short accents: staccato crash or choke (damped sample) instead of a ringing crash.

**Tags:** #orchestration/percussion #instruments/cymbals #articulation/roll #orchestration/crescendo

## Ch. 16 The tam-tam (scan s155L–s155R, printed p.310–311)

**Concepts**
- tam-tam / gong (tam-tam, gong): Far-Eastern bronze disc with turned-down rim; Chinese ones preferred over
  Turkish. Large is 28 in.; some orchestras also have a ~20 in. one. Scores sometimes ask for large/small or
  low/high tam-tams. Freely suspended in a frame.
- Struck gently with a soft chamois-covered stick, the vibration seems to grow from the centre, with
  unpredictable overtones. Struck hard it is extremely violent.

**Rules**
- R19 [PREFER] Soft strokes. Piston finds them the most effective. Character: dramatic, evocative,
  quivering, ominous.
- R20 [ALLOWED] Other sticks (e.g. triangle beater) for special colours.
- R21 [MUST] Use the tam-tam very sparingly and mark the intended duration carefully.

**Agent applicability**
- Use a soft tam-tam hit (low velocity, long release) for dark or ominous moments and at the bottom of
  big swells. Allow its sample to bloom (several seconds) before the next event.

**Tags:** #orchestration/percussion #instruments/tam-tam #mood/ominous

## Ch. 16 The tambourine (scan s155R–s156L, printed p.311–312)

**Concepts**
- tambourine (pandereta; Fr. tambour de Basque, It. tamburo basco / tamburino, Ger. Schellentrommel /
  Tamburin): ~10 in., single calfskin head, pairs of brass jingles in the shell.
- Techniques: (a) struck with knuckles, fist, fingertips, back of hand or on the knee: single strokes and
  rhythms, jingles echo each stroke; (b) shaken: jingle roll; (c) thumb trill: moistened thumb rubbed in an
  arc so it rebounds, sounding head and jingles (some players use rosin); (d) laid flat on lap or chair,
  played near the rim with fingers or drumsticks.

**Rules**
- R22 [PREFER] Notate the jingle roll as tremolo and the thumb trill as a trill, adding "shaken" or "with the
  thumb", to avoid confusion.
- R23 [PREFER] (a) and (b) are the most used; (a)–(c) combine into all kinds of rhythms.
- R24 [ALLOWED] Tambourine in any style. It is a standard symphonic instrument, not limited to Spanish or
  Italian flavour. Bright, lively, festive colour; capable of many delicate effects.
- R25 [AVOID] Tambourine without jingles. Piston says it is rarely used.
- R26 [ALLOWED] Notation: single line or treble-clef staff.

**Agent applicability**
- Festive tutti or dance sections: tambourine hits on accents plus jingle-roll swells into downbeats.
  At pp, use finger taps or a light shake for sparkle.

**Tags:** #orchestration/percussion #instruments/tambourine #mood/festive

## Ch. 16 Wood block and castanets (scan s156L–s156R, printed p.312–313)

**Concepts**
- wood block / Chinese block (caja china): rectangular resonant wood with a slot cavity. Usual lengths
  6½, 7½ and 8 in. Played with snare sticks or xylophone mallets over the slot. Tone: high, hard, hollow;
  indefinite pitch varies with size. Used mostly by 20th-c. American composers `[era: 20th-c. American]`.
- castanets (castañuelas; Fr. castagnettes, It. castagnette, Ger. Kastagnetten): real Spanish castanets are
  hollowed hardwood shells (ebony, rosewood), two pairs, one per hand, string around thumb/fingers.
  Orchestral castanets are a pair hinged on a handle and shaken like a rattle.

**Rules**
- R27 [ALLOWED] Orchestral castanets only suggest native playing; do not expect virtuoso dance-castanet
  detail.
- R28 [PREFER] Castanets mostly for Spanish dance rhythms; occasionally for purely rhythmic clicks in
  absolute music.
- R29 [ALLOWED] Notation for both: single line or staff.

**Agent applicability**
- Castanet MIDI: short repeated clicks and rolls on Spanish dance rhythms (bolero, fandango); keep patterns
  simple. Wood block: dry rhythmic ostinati, Americana or playful character.

**Tags:** #orchestration/percussion #instruments/woodblock #instruments/castanets #style/spanish

## Ch. 16 The triangle (scan s156R, printed p.313)

**Concepts**
- triangle (triángulo; Fr. triangle, It. triangolo, Ger. Triangel): bent round steel bar with one open
  corner, average 6½ in. side. Held by a string and struck with a metal beater; two beaters if hung on a
  stand; a wooden stick for soft effects.
- Single strokes normally on the base; other points change loud/soft nuance.
- triangle trill/roll: rapid strokes back and forth inside the upper angle; written as trill or tremolo.

**Rules**
- R30 [ALLOWED] Flam- and drag-like figures and other rhythms, but they blur in the continuous ring.
- R31 [ALLOWED] The triangle is heard through the loudest tutti. Tone: high, clear, luminous; adds
  brilliance, sparkle, gaiety. Unpitched, it sounds like upper partials of the harmony underneath.
- R32 [MUST] Use the triangle with extreme economy.
- R33 [AVOID] Overusing the triangle trill. Piston warns it resembles an electric (telephone) bell.

**Agent applicability**
- One triangle hit on a key arrival adds sparkle to a full tutti without needing high velocity. Limit
  trills to brief, special moments.

**Tags:** #orchestration/percussion #instruments/triangle #orchestration/color

## Ch. 16 The glockenspiel (scan s157L–s157R, printed p.314–315)

**Concepts**
- glockenspiel / bells (glockenspiel, campanólogo; Fr. jeu de timbres / carillon, It. campanelli,
  Ger. Glockenspiel): 30 chromatic steel bars laid out like a keyboard in a portable case.
- Written range (Fig. 100): G3–C6 (written MIDI 55–84). Sounds two octaves higher: concert G5–C8
  (MIDI 79–108) (my conversion; 30 bars matches 30 semitones).
- Mallets: light, small round heads of hard rubber, soft rubber, yarn, wood or metal.
- keyed glockenspiel: the earlier form, played from a keyboard (Mozart, *The Magic Flute*). Still exists.

**Rules**
- R34 [PREFER] Stay inside the Fig. 100 range even though some instruments have a few extra semitones.
- R35 [MUST] If the part is written only one octave below the sound, say so in the part.
- R36 [PREFER] Single melodic lines with one mallet per hand. Chords with 2–3 mallets per hand are possible.
- R37 [ALLOWED] Fast figures blur from overlapping ring; this is a characteristic, not a defect.
- R38 [ALLOWED] The octave of high glockenspiel notes is hard to perceive.
- R39 [PREFER] Mallet glockenspiel over keyed: much better dynamic range and tone. Use the keyed one only for
  parts that mallets cannot play.

**Examples**
- Ex. 338. Debussy, *La Mer*: glockenspiel, Animé, p then pp. A chromatic, quick melodic line with staccato
  and tenuto marks: delicate soft glockenspiel melody.
- Ex. 339. Mahler, *Symphony no. 4*: glockenspiel, Moderato, ff, heavily accented repeated notes in a
  simple rhythmic tune: loud, marked glockenspiel.

**Agent applicability**
- Glockenspiel MIDI: concert notes 79–108 (check the library: many sample it written-pitch two octaves
  down). Use it to double a flute/violin melody an octave or two up for sparkle; single line, not dense
  chords.

**Tags:** #orchestration/percussion #instruments/glockenspiel #ranges #orchestration/doubling

## Ch. 16 The vibraphone (scan s157R, printed p.315)

**Concepts**
- vibraphone (vibráfono): American development of the glockenspiel from dance bands. Steel bars, a tuned
  stopped resonator tube under each bar, and motor-driven rotating discs at the tube tops that give a slow
  pulsing vibrato and longer sustain. Has a foot damper pedal like a piano.
- Range of the large vibraphone (Piston): four octaves up from 4-foot C, usually written at sounding
  pitch: C3–C7 (MIDI 48–96) (my reading of "4-foot C" as C3; [?] modern instruments are commonly smaller,
  not stated by the source).

**Rules**
- R40 [ALLOWED] `[era: 1955]` Few composers had brought the vibraphone into the symphony orchestra; Piston
  attributes this to its exaggerated sweetness (likened to the organ vox humana).
- R41 [ALLOWED] The foot damper pedal works like the piano sustain pedal, so notes can ring together or be
  damped.

**Agent applicability**
- Vibraphone MIDI: use CC64 for the damper; vary motor/vibrato settings in the patch for a warm,
  pulsing pad or jazz colour. Keep it out of strict period-symphonic textures unless the style is mid-20th-c.
  American or film.

**Tags:** #orchestration/percussion #instruments/vibraphone #ranges

## Ch. 16 Bells / chimes (scan s158L, printed p.316)

**Concepts**
- bells (campanas; Fr. cloches, It. campane, Ger. Glocken): real deep church bells are impractical; a bell
  able to sound below middle C would be far too large for a hall. Bells also have a hum tone an octave
  below the strike tone, but it is too weak to place the pitch in that octave.
- tubular bells / chimes (campanas tubulares): steel tubes 1–2 in. in diameter on a frame, struck near the
  top with a special hammer.
- Range (Fig. 101): C4–F5, sounding as written per Piston (MIDI 60–77). A few semitones more can be added
  with extra tubes.

**Rules**
- R42 [MUST] When "real bells" are asked for, expect an imitation; actual bells sound much higher than the
  composer's notated low pitch.
- R43 [PREFER] Use chimes mainly for dramatic and realistic (church, clock, alarm) effects. Piston also
  recommends studying the many ways composers have suggested bell sounds with combinations of orchestral
  colours (no specific combinations given).

**Agent applicability**
- Tubular bells: notes 60–77 (concert as given by Piston; check the library mapping). For a deep bell
  below C4, synthesize it orchestrally (piano/harp low notes + tam-tam + low brass) instead of a chime note.

**Tags:** #orchestration/percussion #instruments/tubular-bells #ranges #orchestration/color

## Ch. 16 Antique cymbals / crotales (scan s158R, printed p.317)

**Concepts**
- antique cymbals / crotales (crótalos; Fr. crotales / cymbales antiques, It. crotali, Ger. antiken
  Zimbeln): modern replicas of ancient Greek instruments. Small thick brass discs (some under a hand's width)
  in pairs tuned to a specific pitch; one in each hand, rims struck gently, then left to ring. Very clear,
  delicate, bell-like.

**Rules**
- R44 [MUST] One pair = one pitch. Two different notes in quick succession need two players, each with a
  pair.
- R45 [MUST] Notation varies: state whether the part sounds as written or an octave higher.

**Examples** (Fig. 102, written pitches; [?] = uncertain reading)
- (a) Berlioz, *Roméo et Juliette*: two pairs, Bb5 and F6, sounding as written.
- (b) Debussy, *Prélude à l'après-midi d'un faune*: E5 and B5 written, sounding an octave higher (E6, B6).
- (c) Ravel, *Daphnis et Chloé*: six pairs, written B4, C5, Db5 [?], E5 [?], F5, Ab5, sounding an octave
  higher.
- (d) Stravinsky, *Les Noces*: C#6 and B6 [?], "actual pitch" marked in the score.

**Agent applicability**
- Crotales MIDI: sparse single notes (one pitch at a time per pair) in the high register for shimmer at
  cadences or soft magical moments; let them ring.

**Tags:** #orchestration/percussion #instruments/crotales #orchestration/color

## Ch. 16 The xylophone and marimba (scan s158R–s159L, printed p.317–318)

**Concepts**
- xylophone (xilófono; Fr. xylophone, It. xilofono / silofono, Ger. Xylophon): like a glockenspiel with
  rosewood bars; modern instruments have vertical resonators. Bars flat or rounded.
- Written ranges (Fig. 103), sounding an octave higher (my conversion):
  - small: written F3–C6 → concert F4–C7 (MIDI 65–96)
  - medium: written F3–F6 → concert F4–F7 (MIDI 65–101)
  - large: written F3–C7 → concert F4–C8 (MIDI 65–108)
- marimba (marimba): a more mellow xylophone; resonators an octave lower than the xylophone's. Larger
  instruments have a four-octave range from 4-foot C (C3–C7, MIDI 48–96, my reading). Tone warm and
  expressive, suited to solo melodies.

**Rules**
- R46 [ALLOWED] The exact octave of xylophone notes, especially high ones, is hard to hear; players without
  the largest instrument transpose octaves freely.
- R47 [PREFER] Use the xylophone for rapid scales, arpeggios and figuration. *Why:* dry, brittle tone with
  no lasting resonance, so fast lines stay clear (unlike glockenspiel).
- R48 [AVOID] Treating the xylophone as an expressive melodic voice. It is dry rather than expressive.
- R49 [ALLOWED] Short solo passages, and rhythmic, dynamic and colouristic uses.
- R50 [ALLOWED] `[era: 1955]` The marimba has been used rarely in the symphony orchestra, though solo pieces
  with orchestra exist (Paul Creston, Darius Milhaud).

**Examples**
- Ex. 340. Debussy, *Gigues*: xylophone, Modéré 2/4, pp. A light dotted, chromatic melodic line: soft,
  delicate xylophone solo, not only loud effects.
- Ex. 341. Copland, *Appalachian Spring*: xylophone, Presto, ff secco. Fast double-stop (two-note) figures
  and repeated notes with accents: brilliant, dry rhythmic xylophone.

**Agent applicability**
- Xylophone: double fast woodwind or string figuration an octave up to sharpen attacks; concert notes
  65–108. Marimba: warm melodic or ostinato role in film/contemporary textures.

**Tags:** #orchestration/percussion #instruments/xylophone #instruments/marimba #ranges

## Ch. 16 Sound effects and exotic instruments (scan s159R–s160L, printed p.319–320)

**Concepts** (each with the work Piston cites)
- sleigh bells (cascabeles; Fr. grelots, It. sonagli, Ger. Schellen): real sleigh bells shaken by hand —
  Mahler, *Symphony no. 4*.
- rattle / ratchet (matraca; Fr. crécelle, It. raganella, Ger. Ratsche): wooden slat against a cogwheel —
  Strauss, *Till Eulenspiegel*.
- wind machine (máquina de viento; Fr. éoliphone, It. eolifono, Ger. Windmaschine): cranked cylinder;
  faster turning = crescendo and rising pitch — Ravel, *Daphnis et Chloé*.
- whip / slapstick (látigo; Fr. fouet, It. frusta): two hinged boards clapped together — Milhaud, *Second
  Symphonic Suite*.
- Other effects found in scores: sandpaper blocks, anvils, chains, cowbells, sirens, car horns, whistles,
  bird-call, lion's roar, thunder imitations, ticking clocks, breaking glass.
- Latin-American: maracas (gourds with seeds), claves (two hardwood sticks, cupped hand as resonator),
  güiro (notched gourd scraped with a stick; Fr. râpe guero), bongós (pair of single-headed hand drums),
  timbales (pair of larger single-headed drums on a stand, played with sticks; also called tom-toms).
- Chinese: Chinese tom-tom (small two-headed pigskin drum on lacquered shell, hard or soft sticks); temple
  blocks (set of five wooden blocks, roughly pentatonic, usually red lacquer, felt mallets or snare sticks;
  sound between a gourd and a wood block).
- Indian drum / tom-tom: like the Chinese tom-tom but with one head over a wooden bowl.

**Agent applicability**
- For descriptive or programmatic cues, these named effects are idiomatic orchestral options (whip for a
  crack accent, ratchet for comic/rustic chatter, wind machine swells with the orchestra's crescendo).
- Temple blocks: pentatonic-ish hollow pitched patterns (5 notes) for clock-like or Asian-tinged colour.

**Tags:** #orchestration/percussion #instruments/latin-percussion #orchestration/effects

## Ch. 16 The percussion ensemble (scan s160L–s161L, printed p.320–322)

**Concepts**
- Percussion works as a unit: the total effect is the sum of the parts. In a tutti the basic rhythm is
  not played in unison by all; each instrument contributes a portion suited to its nature.

**Rules**
- R51 [PREFER] Give each percussion instrument a different rhythmic pattern that together builds the overall
  rhythm. *Why:* maximum transparency and vitality, with changing colour, pitch level and, above all, tone
  weight on each successive eighth note.
- R52 [AVOID] All percussion playing the same rhythm in unison in a tutti.

**Examples**
- Ex. 342. Ravel, *Daphnis et Chloé* (Animé 2/4, ff): timpani on beats, triangle, cymbals, bass drum
  (grosse caisse), tambour (tenor drum/side drum), snare (caisse claire), castanets and tambourine (with
  rolls), each with a different part. A summary staff shows the combined "over-all pattern": continuous
  eighth notes with accents and articulations. Demonstrates R51.
- Ex. 343. Hindemith, *Symphonic Metamorphosis* (Lively, half = 96): an unusual, imaginative quiet
  percussion texture. Timpani short motif p dim., chimes sustained notes p dim., triangle and tom-tom
  rhythmic interplay, wood block / small cymbal / small gong, all thinning to pp, ending on a pp chord in
  clarinets, horns, bassoons, bass clarinet, contrabassoon, cellos and basses with a fermata.

**Agent applicability**
- When writing a percussion tutti groove, distribute one bar of eighths across instruments: e.g. bass drum
  beat 1, cymbals beat 2, snare eighths, triangle off-beats, tambourine rolls into accents. Check that the
  sum covers every eighth while no two instruments double the same rhythm.
- Quiet percussion-only passages are viable (Hindemith): mix pitched (timpani, chimes) and unpitched light
  instruments at p–pp, then hand over to a soft low wind/string chord.

**Tags:** #orchestration/percussion #orchestration/texture #rhythm/layering

## Ch. 17 The Harp: range and strings (scan s161R, printed p.323)

**Concepts**
- harp (arpa; Fr. harpe, It. arpa, Ger. Harfe).
- The 19th-c. chromatic harp (one string per semitone) and the single-action diatonic harp are fully
  superseded by the double-action diatonic harp `[era: post-1810 double-action pedal harp]`.
- 47 strings tuned to Cb major. Range (Fig. 104): Cb1 to Gb7 (top written with 8va): concert MIDI 23–102
  (Cb1 = B0 = 23, Gb7 = F#7 = 102; my conversion).
- Strings tuned with a key on pins on the right of the neck; retuning during a piece is common and can even
  be done while the left hand plays.

## Ch. 17 The Harp: pedal mechanism (scan s162L–s162R, printed p.324–325)

**Concepts**
- Seven pedals; left foot D C B, right foot E F G A (Fig. 105).
- Each pedal has three notches. Upper = flat (open string, Cb major); middle = natural (upper disc stops
  the string, +1 semitone); lower = sharp (lower disc stops it, +2 semitones) (Fig. 106). Springs return
  the pedal to flat when released.
- One pedal acts on every string of that letter name in all octaves.

**Rules**
- R53 [NEVER] C# and C natural (or any two forms of the same letter) at the same time, except via an
  enharmonic spelling on another string (e.g. Db for C#).
- R54 [ALLOWED] The lowest Cb string has no pedal mechanism; it can be retuned to another pitch before
  playing.
- R55 [ALLOWED] Pedal changes are quick and silent. A pedal can move during playing while its string letter
  is not sounding. Exceptionally, a foot can work a pedal on the other side, and two pedals can move at once
  with one foot (sentence continues past p.325).

**Agent applicability**
- Harp-part validator: at any moment, each letter name A–G has exactly one accidental across all octaves.
  A chromatic line needs pedal changes between notes (give ≥ a beat when the letter is silent) or
  enharmonic respelling. Avoid fast chromatic passages on the harp.
